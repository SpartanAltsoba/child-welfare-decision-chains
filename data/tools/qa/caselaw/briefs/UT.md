## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Cammack-White v. Harbaugh**, 2008 UT App 147, 184 P.3d 631
  Holding: The court affirmed that the statutory good-faith reporter immunity of Utah Code § 62A-4a-410(1) did not protect the petitioners, because an ex parte petition for a child protective order filed with a juvenile judge is not a 'report' made to a peace officer, law enforcement agency, or DCFS.
  Why here: It fixes the boundary of Utah's reporter immunity: the protection attaches to reports made to the statutorily designated recipients, not to court filings.
  Quote: “An ex parte petition for a child protective order, however, does not qualify as a "report" under the statute”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2633057/cammack-white-v-harbaugh/
- **Allen v. Ortez**, 802 P.2d 1307 (Utah 1990)
  Holding: The Court reversed summary judgment in a libel action, holding that the good-faith immunity of Utah's child abuse reporting statute attaches only to a report made to the nearest peace officer, law enforcement agency, or the Division, so a social worker's letters to a city mayor, to opposing counsel and to a court commissioner were not immunized reports and were not protected by the judicial-proceeding privilege either.
  Why here: It is the foundational Utah Supreme Court decision fixing the boundary of reporter immunity: the protection runs to reports channelled to the statutory recipients, not to communications broadcast elsewhere.
  Quote: “Its purpose is certainly not to require, much less immunize, the general dissemination of allegations of child abuse, one of the most defamatory charges that can be made against anyone.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1351415/allen-v-ortez/
- **Roska ex rel. Roska v. Peterson**, 328 F.3d 1230 (10th Cir. 2003)
  Holding: On rehearing in a case arising from a Utah DCFS removal, the Tenth Circuit held that removing a child from his home with no notice, no hearing, no ex parte order and no extraordinary circumstances violated clearly established Fourteenth Amendment law, reversed the grant of qualified immunity on that claim, and held that a caseworker's reliance on a state removal statute is only one factor bearing on objective reasonableness and cannot immunize conduct the statute never authorized.
  Why here: It shows that a mandatory reporter's professional concern, even when relayed by a school nurse and treating physicians, does not by itself authorize removal without pre-deprivation process.
  Quote: “defendants point to no extraordinary circumstances that would justify the complete absence of pre-deprivation procedural safeguards”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/781963/roska-v-peterson/
- **Gomes v. Wood**, 451 F.3d 1122 (10th Cir. 2006)
  Holding: In a Utah DCFS case the Tenth Circuit adopted the reasonable-suspicion standard for warrantless emergency removals — officials may remove a child without prior notice and a hearing when they reasonably suspect an immediate threat to the child's safety if she remains, and must still provide a prompt post-removal hearing — but affirmed qualified immunity because that standard was not clearly established when these defendants acted.
  Why here: The case began with a pediatrician's report of a child's injury to DCFS, and the court measures what that mandatory report could and could not justify.
  Quote: “In our view, the reasonable suspicion standard appropriately balances the interests of the parents, the child, and the state.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/167696/gomes-v-wood/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **State in the Interest of A.R.**, 937 P.2d 1037 (Utah Ct. App. 1997)
  Holding: The court held that the Fourth Amendment applies to a warrantless entry into a home made for child protective rather than criminal investigative purposes, but that the exclusionary rule does not bar the resulting evidence in a civil child protective proceeding, while remaining fully applicable in any related criminal case.
  Why here: The entry here was by a police officer acting in a child protection role, the paradigm law-enforcement/CPS interface.
  Quote: “we hold that the Fourth Amendment's protection is nonetheless applicable”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1152916/state-in-interest-of-ar/
- **Malik v. Arapahoe County Department of Social Services**, 191 F.3d 1306 (10th Cir. 1999)
  Holding: The Tenth Circuit affirmed the denial of absolute and qualified immunity, holding that it was clearly established that procuring a child-seizure order through distortion, misrepresentation, or omission violates the Fourth Amendment, and that a parent's liberty interest in familial association requires pre-deprivation process absent extraordinary circumstances.
  Why here: It involves coordinated police and social services action and the limits on that joint conduct.
  Quote: “except in extraordinary circumstances, a parent has a liberty interest in familial association and privacy that cannot be violated without adequate pre-deprivation procedures”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/158631/malik-v-arapahoe-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Lowther v. Children Youth and Family Department**, 101 F.4th 742 (10th Cir. 2024)
  Holding: The Tenth Circuit clarified that a threat of imminent harm is required under both the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments before officials may enter a home or seize children without a warrant or judicial authorization, while affirming qualified immunity on the claims before it.
  Why here: The case arises from joint law-enforcement and child welfare action and defines the shared constitutional limits on both.
  Quote: “even in the child welfare context, there must be a threat of imminent harm when officials enter a home or seize children without a warrant or judicial authorization”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9500095/lowther-v-children-youth-and-family-department/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **State, Division of Child & Family Services v. Bazzelle**, 2017 UT App 61, 397 P.3d 681
  Holding: The court denied the State's petition for extraordinary relief, holding that the juvenile court retained jurisdiction — including concurrent paternity jurisdiction — because the child welfare case remained open, and therefore did not exceed its authority.
  Why here: It confirms the breadth of the juvenile court's authority to order further inquiry and relief while a child welfare case remains open.
  Quote: “Because we conclude the juvenile court did not exceed its jurisdiction, the State is ineligible for extraordinary relief.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5345519/state-division-of-child-family-services-v-bazzelle/
- **S.A. v. State**, 2016 UT App 191, 382 P.3d 642
  Holding: The court affirmed a disposition order requiring a father to complete a domestic violence assessment, holding that once a child is adjudicated dependent the juvenile court has continuing jurisdiction and may impose any condition on a parent that is reasonable, proportionate, and calculated to serve the child's best interest.
  Why here: It describes how adjudication establishes the jurisdiction that makes all subsequent court-ordered referrals possible.
  Quote: “Under section 78A-6-117(2), the juvenile court may impose conditions on a parent so long as the conditions are "reasonable."”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6655746/sa-v-state/
- **In re K.F.**, 2009 UT 4, 201 P.3d 985
  Holding: The Court affirmed, holding that the juvenile court had subject matter jurisdiction because the mother voluntarily placed K.F. in state custody, that the order ending reunification and setting a goal of individualized permanency was final and appealable and appropriate where compelling reasons ruled out reunification, adoption, guardianship and kinship placement, and that the juvenile court's findings on service-plan compliance and on DCFS's reasonable efforts were owed broad discretion and were not clearly in error.
  Why here: It describes the jurisdictional predicate for every later court-ordered referral and service in a dependency case.
  Quote: “appellate courts defer to juvenile courts on matters of credibility, and juvenile courts have broad discretion in determining whether reasonable reunification efforts were made”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2634909/state-ex-rel-kf/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Deseret News Publishing Co. v. Salt Lake County**, 2008 UT 26, 182 P.3d 372
  Holding: The Court held that government records are presumptively public under GRAMA and that the governmental entity bears the burden of justifying a nonpublic classification; it ordered release of an internal investigative report because the public interest in governmental accountability outweighed the asserted privacy intrusion.
  Why here: It makes internal investigative reports — the raw material of oversight — presumptively obtainable.
  Quote: “government records are presumptively public under GRAMA, and thus, the County bears the burden of proving that it properly classified the investigative report as nonpublic”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2567457/deseret-news-publishing-co-v-salt-lake-county/
- **Schroeder v. Utah Attorney General's Office**, 2015 UT 77, 358 P.3d 1075
  Holding: The Court held that records lawfully obtained by warrant or subpoena are not exempt from GRAMA on search-and-seizure grounds, that two documents were properly classified as attorney work product, but that disclosure was nonetheless required because the closed investigation weakened the interests favoring protection.
  Why here: It supports outside oversight by making closed-investigation records obtainable when the public interest in accountability predominates.
  Quote: “we ultimately reverse the district court's ruling because the State terminated its investigation years ago, so the interests favoring protection are not as compelling as those favoring disclosure”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2830913/schroeder-v-utah-attorney-generals-office/
- **J.J.W. v. State, Division of Child & Family Services**, 2001 UT App 271, 33 P.3d 59
  Holding: The court held that the Juvenile Expungement Statute gives the juvenile court broad authority to order DCFS to expunge its records, including entries in the non-public child abuse Database, but reversed the particular order because DCFS had no notice or opportunity to be heard.
  Why here: It delineates the limited access rules that oversight bodies must work within when seeking DCFS records.
  Quote: “the plain language of section 78-82-905 grants the juvenile court broad authority to expunge records”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2516023/jjw-v-state-division-of-child-family-services/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In re Adoption of Halloway**, 732 P.2d 962 (Utah 1986)
  Holding: The Court held that ICWA domicile is a matter of federal law that state abandonment doctrine cannot defeat, so the Navajo Nation had exclusive jurisdiction over the adoption of a child whose mother was domiciled on the reservation; the United States Supreme Court later adopted this reasoning in Holyfield.
  Why here: It is the foundational Utah authority on ICWA's exclusive tribal jurisdiction, expressly approved by the Supreme Court in Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield.
  Quote: “the interests of the tribe in custodial decisions made with respect to Indian children are as entitled to respect as the interests of the parents”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1154237/matter-of-adoption-of-halloway/
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that 'domicile' under ICWA is a matter of uniform federal law, that children born off-reservation to reservation-domiciled parents were domiciled on the reservation, and that the tribal court therefore had exclusive jurisdiction over their adoption.
  Why here: It is the controlling federal authority on ICWA's exclusive tribal jurisdiction, and it expressly adopts the Utah Supreme Court's reasoning in Halloway.
  Quote: “the law of domicile Congress used in the ICWA cannot be one that permits individual reservation-domiciled tribal members to defeat the tribe's exclusive jurisdiction”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **In re Adoption of B.B.**, 2017 UT 59, 417 P.3d 1
  Holding: The Court held that whether an unwed father has acknowledged or established paternity under ICWA is governed by a federal reasonability standard rather than state law, that the birth father qualified as a 'parent' under ICWA, and that he was entitled to notice of and intervention in the adoption.
  Why here: It is Utah's leading modern ICWA decision on who counts as a 'parent,' on federal rather than state definitions controlling, and on the rejection of the 'existing Indian family' exception.
  Quote: “because he acknowledged paternity as required under ICWA, he had a right to receive notice of and to intervene in the adoption proceeding”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4423655/adoption-bb-v-rkb/
- **In re Adoption of B.B.**, 2020 UT 53
  Holding: The Court held that the birth mother was domiciled in Utah at the child's birth and that initiating adoption proceedings is not an 'abandonment' shifting the child's domicile to the reservation, so the state district court had jurisdiction rather than exclusive tribal jurisdiction under 25 U.S.C. § 1911(a).
  Why here: It supplies the Utah test for the ICWA domicile question that decides whether a tribe has exclusive jurisdiction over a child custody proceeding.
  Quote: “Because a change of domicile occurs once a person establishes physical presence in a new jurisdiction with the intent to remain, we hold that C.C. was a Utah domiciliary”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4771533/in-re-adoption-of-bb/
- **In re A.J.B.**, 2017 UT App 237, 414 P.3d 552
  Holding: The court affirmed, holding that the juvenile court properly exercised jurisdiction where the child was found not to be an 'Indian child' (the mother's tribe being federally unrecognized) and did not abuse its discretion in declining to communicate with the tribal court, since the UCCJEA makes such communication permissive.
  Why here: It addresses the threshold ICWA question — federal recognition of the tribe — and the discretionary nature of state-tribal judicial communication in Utah.
  Quote: “we conclude that the juvenile court properly exercised its jurisdiction and did not abuse its discretion in declining to contact the tribal court”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6656169/state-ex-rel-ajb-v-state/
- **A.D.T. v. State (In re C.D.)**, 2008 UT App 477, 200 P.3d 194
  Holding: The court affirmed the finding that further ICWA active efforts with the grandfather would be futile, but held that the State must begin complying with ICWA's placement preferences immediately after the shelter hearing and demonstrate compliance, good cause to deviate, or a concrete plan by the dispositional hearing.
  Why here: It is Utah's most detailed appellate treatment of ICWA active efforts and placement preferences in a dependency case.
  Quote: “the State must begin its attempts to comply with the ICWA's placement preferences immediately after the shelter hearing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5325594/adt-v-state/

## DEC-01 · Screen-Out / Unfounded at Intake
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative duty on a State to protect a child from private violence, even where the agency knew of the danger, absent a custodial or other special relationship that the State itself created.
  Why here: It is the controlling federal answer to the family whose report was screened out and whose child was later harmed: no federal constitutional duty absent custody.
  Quote: “nothing in the language of the Due Process Clause itself requires the State to protect the life, liberty, and property of its citizens against invasion by private actors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Ferree v. State**, 784 P.2d 149 (Utah 1989)
  Holding: The Court affirmed summary judgment for the State on the ground of duty rather than immunity, holding that corrections and supervision officials owed no duty of care to a member of the general public killed by a released offender absent a specific relationship to that individual.
  Why here: It is the counterweight to Little: absent a specific assumed relationship to the child, the State owes no general tort duty arising from a screening or release decision.
  Quote: “we agree with the trial court's conclusion that no defendant had a duty of care toward the victim”

- **State ex rel. B.R.**, 2007 UT 82, 171 P.3d 435
  Holding: The Court reversed the court of appeals, holding that using the heightened clear-and-convincing standard at a permanency hearing is permissible and does not predetermine termination, and that the appellate court had improperly substituted its judgment for the juvenile court's.
  Why here: It explains that the permanency hearing asks only whether the child may safely be returned home, and that findings there do not dictate the later termination outcome.
  Quote: “When a foundation for the court's decision exists in the evidence, an appellate court may not engage in a reweighing of the evidence.”

  Why here: Reunification efforts that ignore a parent's disclosed disability may be legally inadequate, affecting whether reunification was genuinely attempted.
  Quote: “We conclude that the ADA applies to the provision of reunification services”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6655725/nd-v-state/
- **In re J.L.**, 2022 UT 12
  Holding: The Court retained deferential review of best-interest determinations but vacated in part because the juvenile court committed threshold legal errors in assessing whether the father had made sufficient progress in rehabilitation under the reunification statute.
  Why here: It holds that a juvenile court must correctly assess a parent's statutory progress toward rehabilitation before it can lawfully conclude that reunification should give way to termination.
  Quote: “we reject the father's request that we abandon a deferential standard of review of a best interest determination”

- **A.W. v. State (In re C.C.)**, 2017 UT App 134, 402 P.3d 17
  Holding: The court affirmed termination, holding that DCFS's individualized modifications to the mother's service plan for her learning disabilities, agoraphobia, and domestic-violence history satisfied both the reasonable-efforts requirement and Title II of the ADA.
  Why here: Reunification efforts must be tailored to a parent's disclosed disability for the resulting failure to reunify to be attributed to the parent.
  Quote: “Title II of the ADA prohibits public entities from discriminating against individuals with disabilities”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6655795/aw-v-state/
- **In re K.F.**, 2009 UT 4, 201 P.3d 985
  Holding: The Court affirmed, holding that the juvenile court had subject matter jurisdiction because the mother voluntarily placed K.F. in state custody, that the order ending reunification and setting a goal of individualized permanency was final and appealable and appropriate where compelling reasons ruled out reunification, adoption, guardianship and kinship placement, and that the juvenile court's findings on service-plan compliance and on DCFS's reasonable efforts were owed broad discretion and were not clearly in error.

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2634909/state-ex-rel-kf/
- **In re P.J.R.**, 2023 UT App 27
  Holding: The court affirmed termination, holding that the juvenile court is presumed to have applied the clear-and-convincing standard absent record evidence to the contrary, that the State made out a prima facie case of reasonable efforts, and that the court did not abuse its discretion in determining after trial that DCFS had made reasonable reunification efforts.
  Why here: It holds that friction with a caseworker and delay in starting family therapy did not make the reunification effort unreasonable, defining how much shortfall a parent must prove before the failure to reunify can be laid at the agency's door.
  Quote: “we discern no abuse of the juvenile court's discretion in its ultimate determination, made as factfinder after trial, that DCFS provided reasonable efforts toward reunification”

## OUT-02 · Termination of Parental Rights / Adoption
- **In re B.T.B.**, 2020 UT 60, 472 P.3d 827 (as amended Aug. 14, 2020)
  Holding: The Utah Supreme Court affirmed the court of appeals' disavowal of the 'almost automatically' line of cases and held that the statutory 'strictly necessary' requirement is not a separate third element but a mandatory part of the best-interest inquiry, so a juvenile court must expressly consider feasible alternatives to termination.
  Why here: This is the controlling Utah framework for terminating parental rights: grounds alone never suffice, and the court must find termination strictly necessary from the child's point of view.
  Quote: “as part of that inquiry, a court must specifically address whether termination is strictly necessary to promote the child's welfare and best interest”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4763080/in-re-btb/
- **In re B.T.B.**, 2018 UT App 157
  Holding: The court disavowed its 'almost automatically' line of cases, held that the statutory 'strictly necessary' requirement is analyzed as part of the best-interest element rather than as a separate third element, held that best interest must therefore be applied more thoroughly and independently than earlier cases suggested, and remanded for reconsideration — the decision the Utah Supreme Court affirmed in 2020.
  Why here: It is the working exposition of Utah's termination test that practitioners cite alongside the Supreme Court's affirmance, and it is the decision that ended the inference that statutory grounds carry best interest almost automatically.
  Quote: “we conclude that courts should analyze the "strictly necessary" language as part of the "best interest" element of our historical test”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5310096/in-re-btb/
- **In re A.H.**, 2024 UT 26, 554 P.3d 969
  Holding: The Court reversed the court of appeals, holding that In re B.T.B. created no new substantive right, that appellate courts may not graft a 'materially better' test onto the statutory strictly-necessary standard, and that the court of appeals exceeded appropriate appellate review by reweighing evidence.
  Why here: It fixes the current Utah formulation of the strictly-necessary/best-interest test and rejects judicially added glosses on the statute.
  Quote: “the court erred by applying the "materially better" standard instead of the relevant statutory language”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10014131/in-re-ah/
- **Santosky v. Kramer**, 455 U.S. 745 (1982)
  Holding: The Court held that before a State may sever the rights of parents in their natural child, due process requires proof by at least clear and convincing evidence, and that New York's fair-preponderance standard was unconstitutional.
  Why here: It is the federal constitutional floor — clear and convincing evidence — that Utah's termination statute and In re G.D. implement.
  Quote: “We hold that such a standard adequately conveys to the factfinder the level of subjective certainty about his factual conclusions necessary to satisfy due process.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/110676/santosky-v-kramer/
- **In re G.D.**, 2021 UT 19, 491 P.3d 867
  Holding: The Court declined to raise the burden of proof for termination from clear and convincing evidence to beyond a reasonable doubt, and held the juvenile court adequately conducted the strictly-necessary analysis, including considering the grandmother as a kinship alternative.
  Why here: It settles that clear and convincing evidence, not proof beyond a reasonable doubt, is the Utah standard for terminating parental rights.
  Quote: “The juvenile court did not err in declining to apply the "beyond a reasonable doubt" standard of proof.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4890629/in-re-gd/
- **In re D.S.**, 2025 UT 11
  Holding: The Court reversed the court of appeals and reinstated the termination order, holding that the juvenile court's best-interest determination — including its rejection of permanent guardianship with the grandmother in favor of adoption — was supported by the evidence and not against its clear weight.
  Why here: It is the most recent Utah Supreme Court application of the strictly-necessary standard and confirms the deference owed to a juvenile court's termination decision.
  Quote: “Because the juvenile court's best interest determination was supported by the evidence, we reverse.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10384274/in-re-ds/

## OUT-06 · Child Fatality / Serious Injury
- **Little v. Utah State Division of Family Services**, 667 P.2d 49 (Utah 1983)
  Holding: The Court affirmed a judgment against the Division of Family Services for the death of an autistic child in a foster home, holding that although the decision to place a child may be discretionary, once placement occurs the agency owes and can be held to an ordinary duty of due care in supervising that placement.
  Why here: It is the Utah authority permitting recovery when a child dies from inadequate supervision of a state-arranged placement.
  Quote: “the question was no longer whether the child was to receive foster care but whether due care was exercised under a duty assumed”

  Holding: The Court affirmed summary judgment for the State on the ground of duty rather than immunity, holding that corrections and supervision officials owed no duty of care to a member of the general public killed by a released offender absent a specific relationship to that individual.
  Why here: It defines when a death cannot be laid at the agency's door, and so bounds Utah's fatality-liability doctrine.
  Quote: “we agree with the trial court's conclusion that no defendant had a duty of care toward the victim”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1439381/ferree-v-state/

## FAIL-02 · Unlawful Removal

  Why here: It is the controlling Tenth Circuit authority — binding in Utah — for § 1983 liability when a child is removed on a court order obtained by misrepresentation or omission.

  Why here: It is the current, controlling Tenth Circuit statement of the exigency standard governing warrantless removals in Utah.

It is the case a practitioner would pair with Yvonne L. at FAIL-04 and OUT-06, and it post-dates every fatality authority the brief carries (Little, 1983; Ferree, 1989; DeShaney, 1989). Without it the brief's fatality nodes rest on two 1980s Utah tort cases and a federal no-duty rule.
- Missing (for a later pass): Dubbs ex rel. Dubbs v. Head Start, Inc., 336 F.3d 1194 (10th Cir. 2003) — verified on CourtListener (cluster 163684). The Tenth Circuit's authority on invasive physical examinations of children conducted without parental notice or consent, holding such examinations are Fourth Amendment searches and rejecting a categorical special-needs justification. It is the natural companion to Doe v. Woodard at INP-08 and DEC-02, and it is the authority ACT-06 actually needs: that node presently has only Jensen genuinely on point, with Doe v. Woodard padding it. Dubbs cuts the other way from Woodard on the special-needs question and a brief citing Woodard alone misstates the state of the law.
- Missing (for a later pass): Bolden v. Doe (In re Adoption of J.S.), 2014 UT 51, 358 P.3d 1009 — verified on CourtListener (clusters 2748023, 2747938, 2754059). The Utah Supreme Court's major modern decision on the rights of an unmarried biological father under the Adoption Act and the constitutional status of an inchoate parental interest. It is the doctrinal predecessor to In re Adoption of K.T.B. (2020 UT 51), which the brief does include, and it is what a practitioner would cite at DEC-05 and OUT-02 for the proposition that the Act's procedural preconditions are measured against the father's constitutionally protected interest. Note the decision is fractured with a dissent by Justice Parrish, so any quotation must be checked against the alignment.
- Missing (for a later pass): Salt Lake City Corp. v. Jordan River Restoration Network, 2018 UT 62, 435 P.3d 179 — verified on CourtListener (cluster 4576258, Petersen, J., opinion of the Court). The Utah Supreme Court's controlling modern decision on how a GRAMA request must be framed and how a governmental entity must respond to a broad request. PMC-05, PMC-02, PMC-03 and PMC-06 all rest on Deseret News (2008) and Schroeder (2015) for the presumption and the balancing test, but neither tells a requester what a legally sufficient request looks like or what the entity's obligations are in responding — which is the first practical question in any transparency effort and the ground on which such requests are most often defeated.
