## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **T.H. v. University of Kansas Hospital Authority**, 53 Kan. App. 2d 332, 388 P.3d 181 (2017)
  Holding: The court affirmed dismissal of a malpractice suit against a physician who reported suspected sexual abuse, holding that K.S.A. 38-2223(f) immunizes reporters who negligently misdiagnose abuse and that the immunity is lost only if the plaintiff pleads facts supporting actual malice, meaning a specific intent to injure.
  Why here: This is the controlling Kansas construction of the mandatory reporter immunity in K.S.A. 38-2223(f), including the malice exception that defeats it.
  Quote: “In sum, we find that K.S.A. 2015 Supp. 38-2223(f) provides immunity to doctors who negligently misdiagnose child abuse”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4336421/th-and-cc-v-university-of-kansas-hosp-auth/
- **Beebe v. Fraktman**, 22 Kan. App. 2d 493, 921 P.2d 216 (1996)
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for SRS workers sued for a negligent child abuse investigation preceding a child's death, holding that the reporting and investigation statute creates only a duty to the public at large and that the decision whether to open a file for further investigation is a discretionary function immune under the Kansas Tort Claims Act.
  Why here: A report made to SRS creates no enforceable individual duty; the statutory reporting scheme is owed to the public at large.
  Quote: “The decision whether to open a file for further investigation is a discretionary function”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1119423/beebe-v-fraktman/
- **Burney v. Kansas Department of Social & Rehabilitation Services**, 23 Kan. App. 2d 394, 931 P.2d 26 (1997)
  Holding: The court reversed a negligence verdict for a substitute teacher who had been investigated for child abuse, holding that SRS owes no duty to an alleged abuser, that there is no special relationship between the agency and a suspect, and that both the decision whether to open a file and the manner of conducting a child abuse investigation are discretionary functions immune under the Kansas Tort Claims Act.
  Why here: A professional accused after a report has no negligence claim against the investigating agency, whatever the quality of the investigation.
  Quote: “we hold that in investigating child abuse cases, SRS owes no duty to the alleged abuser”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1169765/burney-v-kansas-department-of-social-rehabilitation-services/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Halley v. Huckaby**, 902 F.3d 1136 (10th Cir. 2018)

  Why here: The case addresses joint police–child welfare action and separates the liability of the decisionmakers from that of the officer who merely executed the order.

- **Lowther v. Children, Youth & Families Department**, 101 F.4th 742 (10th Cir. 2024)
  Holding: The court affirmed qualified immunity for an investigator and county defendants on a warrantless-entry claim, holding that under circuit precedent a reasonable official could believe reasonable suspicion of past abuse justified warrantless entry, so the law was not clearly established to the contrary.
  Why here: This is the most recent Tenth Circuit treatment of a joint law-enforcement and child-welfare entry into a home during an abuse investigation.
  Quote: “a reasonable investigator or officer could reasonably believe warrantless entry was justified by reasonable suspicion of past abuse”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9500095/lowther-v-children-youth-and-family-department/

  Why here: Where police and caseworkers act together to enter a home, the Fourth Amendment warrant rule applies to the caseworkers just as to the officers.
  Quote: “We find no special need that renders the warrant requirement impracticable when social workers enter a home to remove a child, absent exigent circumstances”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/162558/roska-ex-rel-roska-v-peterson/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral

  Holding: The court held the father's challenge to the temporary custody order moot because later adjudication and disposition orders superseded it, but retained the appeal under the public-importance exception and upheld K.S.A. 38-2243(f)(3) — the temporary custody standard — against vagueness and overbreadth attack.
  Why here: This case walks through the ex parte protective custody application and the temporary custody hearing that follow a court-authorized intervention.
  Quote: “K.S.A. 2011 Supp. 38-2243(f)(3) is neither unconstitutionally vague nor over-broad”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7213414/in-the-interest-of-aes/
- **In re A.A.-F.**, 444 P.3d 938 (Kan. 2019)
  Holding: The court affirmed Kansas courts' exercise of UCCJEA jurisdiction transferred from California and held that, where minimal due process was afforded, the district court's failure to hold the statutorily required permanency hearing within 30 days did not establish a constitutional due process violation, while stressing that the statutory requirement still binds district courts.
  Why here: A CINC case can begin in Kansas on a jurisdictional transfer from another state's court, and this case explains when Kansas courts may accept it under the UCCJEA.
  Quote: “Mother has failed to show a constitutional due process violation resulting from the failure to comply with a Kansas statute”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4639102/in-re-interests-aa-f/
- **In re J.L.**, 57 Kan. App. 2d 60, 449 P.3d 762 (2019)
  Holding: The court reversed and remanded a default CINC adjudication entered at a pretrial conference when the father arrived ten minutes late, holding that the blanket notice in the petition did not satisfy K.S.A. 60-255(a) and that due process independently required advance notice that adjudication could occur at that hearing.
  Why here: Once a court proceeding is under way, the parent must receive specific advance notice of any hearing at which the child may be adjudicated.
  Quote: “Father had a due process right to advance notice that a CINC adjudication could happen at the pending pretrial hearing before such an order could be entered”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4641222/in-re-jl/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In re Guardianship & Conservatorship of B.H.**, 442 P.3d 457 (Kan. 2019)
  Holding: The court reversed the Court of Appeals and remanded, holding that the parental preference doctrine governs a parent's petition to terminate a voluntary guardianship absent unfitness or extraordinary circumstances, and that the district court's findings were too sparse to permit appellate review of whether extraordinary circumstances existed.
  Why here: Parents who voluntarily place children with a guardian during a family crisis retain the parental preference when they later seek the children back, absent unfitness or extraordinary circumstances.
  Quote: “Because we determine that the district court findings were insufficient to allow proper appellate review, we remand the case to the district court for more complete and specific findings”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4629727/in-re-guardianship-and-conservatorship-of-bh/
- **In re P.R.**, 480 P.3d 778 (Kan. 2021)
  Holding: The court held that a parent's relinquishment of parental rights to the Kansas Department for Children and Families is valid without the agency's formal written acceptance, and that the mother's relinquishment was knowing, effective, and attended by the procedural due process she was owed.
  Why here: A parent who voluntarily surrenders rights to DCF is bound once the relinquishment is executed, so guardian-initiated contacts with the agency can have irreversible legal effect.
  Quote: “formal written acceptance by the Kansas Department for Children and Families (DCF) is not required in order for a parent's relinquishment of parental rights to that agency to be valid”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4856194/in-re-pr/
- **In re Guardianship of Williams**, 254 Kan. 814, 869 P.2d 661 (1994)
  Holding: The court reversed the denial of a natural mother's petition to terminate a voluntary guardianship and overruled In re Marriage of Criqui, holding that the best-interests test does not apply, and the parental preference doctrine controls, in a custody dispute between a nonparent and a parent who has not been found unfit, absent highly unusual or extraordinary circumstances.
  Why here: A parent who voluntarily hands a child to a caregiver during a crisis does not forfeit the parental preference when she later asks for the child back.
  Quote: “absent highly unusual or extraordinary circumstances it has no application in determining whether a parent, not found to be unfit, is entitled to custody as against a third-party nonparent”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1229715/in-re-guardianship-of-williams/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court reversed, holding that the 'reasonable efforts' requirement of 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15) in the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act creates neither a right enforceable by beneficiaries under § 1983 nor an implied private right of action.
  Why here: Federal child welfare plan requirements are enforced through the Secretary's oversight and funding levers, not by private suit, which is why audits and reviews matter.
  Quote: “We conclude that 42 U. S. C. § 671(a)(15) neither confers an enforceable private right on its beneficiaries nor creates an implied cause of action on their behalf”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Blessing v. Freestone**, 520 U.S. 329 (1997)
  Holding: The Court vacated and remanded, holding that individuals have no § 1983 right to require a State's 'substantial compliance' with a federal Social Security Act program, and that courts must analyze each statutory provision separately rather than treating a multifaceted funding scheme as a single source of rights.
  Why here: Systemic non-compliance found by federal audit does not translate into individually enforceable rights, so oversight findings are the operative lever.
  Quote: “The Court of Appeals erred not only in finding that individuals have an enforceable right to substantial compliance”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/118101/blessing-v-freestone/
- **Baker v. Hayden**, 490 P.3d 1164 (Kan. 2021)
  Holding: The court dismissed a Kansas Open Records Act appeal, holding that a KORA plaintiff must satisfy both KORA's statutory standing requirements and traditional standing, that standing can be lost during litigation, and that loss of standing deprives the courts of jurisdiction.
  Why here: Citizen-initiated records litigation aimed at triggering oversight of an agency or court must clear both statutory and traditional standing hurdles in Kansas.
  Quote: “Baker must establish both (1) any statutory standing requirements imposed by KORA and (2) the traditional requirements of standing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4897076/baker-v-hayden/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In re M.F.**, 290 Kan. 142, 225 P.3d 1177 (2010)
  Holding: The court reversed the CINC adjudication and the termination of parental rights, holding that ICWA's 'qualified expert witness' requirement demands expertise beyond ordinary social-worker credentials, that Kansas courts should consult the ICWA legislative history and the BIA Guidelines, and that the absence of such testimony is not harmless error.
  Why here: Once ICWA applies to a Kansas child, the State must produce a witness with tribal or Indian child-welfare expertise before the child can be placed in foster care or rights terminated.
  Quote: “the ICWA heightens the requirements for an expert's qualifications beyond those normally required in a proceeding governed solely by state statutes”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7970654/in-the-interest-of-mf/
- **In re T.S.W.**, 294 Kan. 423, 276 P.3d 133 (2012)
  Holding: Reviewing a collateral order, the court held that a non-Indian biological mother's preference for a non-Indian adoptive family is not, absent a request for anonymity, good cause to deviate from ICWA's placement preferences, and reversed the district court's good-cause finding.
  Why here: This is the leading Kansas statement of how 25 U.S.C. § 1915 placement preferences bind state courts once a child is an 'Indian child,' regardless of the parent's own wishes.
  Quote: “absent a request for anonymity by a biological parent, a parent's placement preference cannot override ICWA's placement factors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7970877/in-re-tsw/
- **In re A.J.S.**, 288 Kan. 429, 204 P.3d 543 (2009)
  Holding: The court overruled In re Adoption of Baby Boy L. and abandoned the judicially created 'existing Indian family' doctrine, holding that ICWA applies to a child of both Indian and non-Indian heritage without any threshold inquiry into the family's cultural connection to the tribe.
  Why here: Kansas no longer screens out ICWA cases by asking whether the child came from an 'existing Indian family,' so ICWA attaches whenever the statutory definition of Indian child is met.
  Quote: “we hereby overrule Baby Boy L., 231 Kan. 199, and abandon its existing Indian family doctrine”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7970564/in-re-ajs/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court rejected all challenges to the Indian Child Welfare Act, upholding Congress's authority to enact it, reversing on the anticommandeering claims, and vacating for lack of standing the equal protection and nondelegation challenges to the placement preferences.
  Why here: ICWA remains fully enforceable against Kansas courts and agencies, including its notice, active efforts, expert witness and placement preference requirements.
  Quote: “the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners' challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/
- **In re E.L.**, 61 Kan. App. 2d 311, 502 P.3d 1049 (2021)
  Holding: The court affirmed termination of parental rights, holding that ordering a parent to undergo a clinical or substance abuse evaluation in a CINC case does not violate the Fifth Amendment, that non-compliance may be considered in the unfitness analysis, and that a parent's demonstrated unwillingness to participate in treatment may be weighed in deciding whether the State made ICWA 'active efforts.'
  Why here: This is the leading published Kansas decision applying ICWA's 'active efforts' standard and the 25 C.F.R. § 23.2 definition to a state agency's reunification work.
  Quote: “we do not find Mother's reluctance to participate in individual therapy meant SFM did not engage in active efforts to persuade her to do so”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5300653/in-re-el/

  Why here: This is the Kansas Supreme Court source of the discretionary-function immunity that the Court of Appeals later extended in Beebe to the decision whether to open a file at all.

  Why here: This is the federal constitutional floor for the standard of proof in every termination of parental rights case, including Kansas proceedings under K.S.A. 38-2269.

- **Lassiter v. Department of Social Services**, 452 U.S. 18 (1981)

  Why here: This is the federal baseline on appointed counsel at termination; Kansas exceeds it by statute, and Kansas courts apply its Mathews framework to related counsel questions.
  Quote: “neither can we say that the Constitution requires the appointment of counsel in every parental termination proceeding”

  Why here: The deferential standard of review announced here — no reweighing of evidence, no credibility redetermination — governs every Kansas appeal seeking reversal of a CINC or termination order.
  Quote: “We agree with those courts that have essentially defined clear and convincing evidence as that which is sufficient to establish that the truth of the facts asserted is "highly probable."”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7970469/in-the-interest-of-bd-y/

## FAIL-02 · Unlawful Removal
- **Roska ex rel. Roska v. Peterson**, 304 F.3d 982 (10th Cir. 2002)
  Holding: The court held that caseworkers who entered a family's home and removed a child without a warrant, court order, or exigent circumstances violated the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments, rejecting the argument that the 'special needs' doctrine excuses the warrant requirement for child welfare removals.
  Why here: This is the leading Tenth Circuit authority for a § 1983 wrongful-removal claim arising from a warrantless, non-exigent removal, which controls in Kansas.
  Quote: “We find no special need that renders the warrant requirement impracticable when social workers enter a home to remove a child, absent exigent circumstances”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/162558/roska-ex-rel-roska-v-peterson/
- **Halley v. Huckaby**, 902 F.3d 1136 (10th Cir. 2018)
  Holding: In a case beginning with an anonymous hotline call, the court affirmed the denial of qualified immunity to the child welfare specialist and the officer who directed a six-year-old's seizure and transport from school for questioning, while reversing as to the officer who merely transported the child and as to the Fourteenth Amendment familial association claim.
  Why here: This is a recent Tenth Circuit decision allowing a § 1983 unlawful seizure claim against a child welfare worker to proceed past qualified immunity.
  Quote: “The district court correctly determined that two of the three defendants were not entitled to qualified immunity on the Fourth Amendment unlawful seizure claim”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4530346/halley-v-huckaby/
- **Lowther v. Children, Youth & Families Department**, 101 F.4th 742 (10th Cir. 2024)
  Holding: The court affirmed qualified immunity for an investigator and county defendants on a warrantless-entry claim, holding that under circuit precedent a reasonable official could believe reasonable suspicion of past abuse justified warrantless entry, so the law was not clearly established to the contrary.
  Why here: Read together with Roska, this decision shows how narrow the clearly-established path is for a Kansas plaintiff's warrantless-entry claim today.
  Quote: “a reasonable investigator or officer could reasonably believe warrantless entry was justified by reasonable suspicion of past abuse”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9500095/lowther-v-children-youth-and-family-department/
- **Doe v. Woodard**, 912 F.3d 1278 (10th Cir. 2019)
  Holding: The court affirmed dismissal on qualified immunity of Fourth Amendment claims brought over a caseworker's warrantless visual body search of a four-year-old at school without parental consent, holding that the law was not clearly established that the special needs doctrine could not justify such a search, and affirmed dismissal of the parents' Fourteenth Amendment claims.
  Why here: The circuit's unresolved treatment of the special needs doctrine means many investigative-search claims in Kansas will fail on qualified immunity rather than on the merits.
  Quote: “the Does have not shown that Ms. Woodard's search violated clearly established Fourth Amendment law”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4578612/doe-v-woodard/
- **Stanley v. Illinois**, 405 U.S. 645 (1972)
  Holding: The Court held that an unwed father was entitled as a matter of due process to a hearing on his fitness before his children could be taken from him, and that Illinois' presumption of unfitness applied only to unwed fathers denied him equal protection.
  Why here: This is the foundational federal holding that removal without an individualized hearing violates due process, on which wrongful-removal claims ultimately rest.
  Quote: “as a matter of due process of law, Stanley was entitled to a hearing on his fitness as a parent before his children were taken from him”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/108497/stanley-v-illinois/

## FAIL-03 · Federal Compliance Failure
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court reversed, holding that the 'reasonable efforts' requirement of 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15) in the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act creates neither a right enforceable by beneficiaries under § 1983 nor an implied private right of action.
  Why here: Failure to meet federal IV-E plan requirements is remediable through federal administrative and funding mechanisms rather than private litigation.

  Holding: The Court vacated and remanded, holding that individuals have no § 1983 right to require a State's 'substantial compliance' with a federal Social Security Act program, and that courts must analyze each statutory provision separately rather than treating a multifaceted funding scheme as a single source of rights.
  Why here: A State's aggregate failure to meet federal performance standards is not itself privately actionable; each provision must be examined for rights-creating language.
  Quote: “The Court of Appeals erred not only in finding that individuals have an enforceable right to substantial compliance”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/118101/blessing-v-freestone/

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/

## FAIL-04 · Child Death Under Agency Supervision
- **Estate of B.I.C. ex rel. C.S.C. v. Gillen**, 702 F.3d 1182 (10th Cir. 2012)
  Holding: In a case arising from the death of a Kansas toddler, the court reversed summary judgment for a Kansas SRS social worker on the state-created danger claim, holding that genuine issues of material fact precluded qualified immunity, while affirming summary judgment on the familial association claims.
  Why here: A social worker who refuses evidence of abuse, deflects to law enforcement, and declares allegations unsubstantiated may face a danger-creation claim if the child is later killed.
  Quote: “We agree that qualified immunity was not appropriate on the state danger-creation claim given genuine issues of material fact”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/813920/estate-of-bic-ex-rel-csc-v-gillen/
- **Schwartz v. Booker**, 702 F.3d 573 (10th Cir. 2012)
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of qualified immunity, holding that foster care is a custodial relationship giving rise to a special relationship between the State and the child, which creates a continuing constitutional duty to protect that is violated when officials know of a danger or fail to exercise professional judgment.
  Why here: This supplies the doctrinal basis for § 1983 liability when a child is harmed while under agency supervision in out-of-home placement.
  Quote: “foster care is recognized as one of the custodial relationships that creates a special relationship”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/813921/schwartz-v-booker/
- **T.D. v. Patton**, 868 F.3d 1209 (10th Cir. 2017)
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of qualified immunity to a caseworker who recommended and facilitated a child's placement with an abusive father while withholding known adverse information, holding that on the facts viewed in the child's favor she violated his clearly established substantive due process right to be free from state-created danger.
  Why here: This is the clearest recent Tenth Circuit statement that a caseworker's affirmative conduct, including withholding known risk information, defeats qualified immunity.
  Quote: “we conclude that, under Currier, Ms. Patton violated T.D.'s clearly established substantive due process constitutional right to be free of a state official's creation of danger from a private actor”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4422214/td-v-patton/
- **Beebe v. Fraktman**, 22 Kan. App. 2d 493, 921 P.2d 216 (1996)
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for SRS workers sued for a negligent child abuse investigation preceding a child's death, holding that the reporting and investigation statute creates only a duty to the public at large and that the decision whether to open a file for further investigation is a discretionary function immune under the Kansas Tort Claims Act.
  Why here: Families of children who die after a screened-out report have no Kansas tort remedy against the agency workers who made that call.
  Quote: “The decision whether to open a file for further investigation is a discretionary function”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1119423/beebe-v-fraktman/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court affirmed judgment for the county, holding that the Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative duty on a State to protect a child from private violence merely because caseworkers knew of the danger, while noting that a custodial relationship such as state-operated foster care might be different.
  Why here: This is the controlling federal limit on agency liability for failure to protect, and it drives the state-created danger and special relationship exceptions the Tenth Circuit applies.
  Quote: “nothing in the language of the Due Process Clause itself requires the State to protect the life, liberty, and property of its citizens against invasion by private actors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/
- **C.J.W. ex rel. L.W. v. State**, 253 Kan. 1, 853 P.2d 4 (1993)
  Holding: The court reversed summary judgment for the State in a suit by a 12-year-old assaulted in juvenile detention, holding that the State owed him a duty under Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 315, 319 and 320 to warn of and protect against a known dangerous detainee, that the discretionary function exception did not immunize breach of that legally imposed duty, and that controverted facts precluded summary judgment.
  Why here: Where a mandatory legal duty to protect a child in custody exists, the discretionary function exception does not shield the agency from damages liability.
  Quote: “Children who are taken into custody should be protected from others in custody”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7969056/cjw-ex-rel-lw-v-state/

## FAIL-05 · Discrimination / Retaliation
- **In re A.J.S.**, 288 Kan. 429, 204 P.3d 543 (2009)
  Holding: The court overruled In re Adoption of Baby Boy L. and abandoned the judicially created 'existing Indian family' doctrine, holding that ICWA applies to a child of both Indian and non-Indian heritage without any threshold inquiry into the family's cultural connection to the tribe.
  Why here: Abandoning the existing-Indian-family doctrine removes a device that had been used to withhold ICWA's protections from children on the basis of perceived cultural assimilation.
  Quote: “we hereby overrule Baby Boy L., 231 Kan. 199, and abandon its existing Indian family doctrine”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7970564/in-re-ajs/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court rejected all challenges to the Indian Child Welfare Act, upholding Congress's authority to enact it, reversing on the anticommandeering claims, and vacating for lack of standing the equal protection and nondelegation challenges to the placement preferences.
  Why here: The equal protection challenge to ICWA's placement preferences was left unresolved for lack of standing, so the preferences continue to govern placements.
  Quote: “the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners' challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/
- **In re T.S.W.**, 294 Kan. 423, 276 P.3d 133 (2012)
  Holding: Reviewing a collateral order, the court held that a non-Indian biological mother's preference for a non-Indian adoptive family is not, absent a request for anonymity, good cause to deviate from ICWA's placement preferences, and reversed the district court's good-cause finding.
  Why here: Deviating from ICWA preferences on the strength of a parent's non-Indian placement preference is the kind of placement decision the court identified as unlawful.
  Quote: “absent a request for anonymity by a biological parent, a parent's placement preference cannot override ICWA's placement factors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7970877/in-re-tsw/
- **Lindenman v. Umscheid**, 255 Kan. 610, 875 P.2d 964 (1994)
  Holding: In a suit by day care operators against state and local health officials, the court affirmed judgment for most defendants but reversed summary judgment for KDHE on the abuse-of-process and malicious-prosecution claims, holding that the Kansas Tort Claims Act's discretionary function exception applies only where no mandatory duty or guideline exists and confers no immunity for maliciously prosecuting a citizen.
  Why here: A person who claims a state agency pursued enforcement against him maliciously has a remedy in Kansas notwithstanding the Tort Claims Act, unlike a claim of mere negligent investigation.
  Quote: “The government has no power or grant of immunity under the KTCA to maliciously prosecute a citizen of the state”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2612878/lindenman-v-umscheid/

## FAIL-06 · Disclosure / Transparency Failure
- **Baker v. Hayden**, 490 P.3d 1164 (Kan. 2021)
  Holding: The court dismissed a Kansas Open Records Act appeal, holding that a KORA plaintiff must satisfy both KORA's statutory standing requirements and traditional standing, that standing can be lost during litigation, and that loss of standing deprives the courts of jurisdiction.
  Why here: This is the controlling Kansas authority on who may sue when a public agency or court refuses to disclose records, and on how such suits fail on standing rather than the merits.
  Quote: “Baker must establish both (1) any statutory standing requirements imposed by KORA and (2) the traditional requirements of standing”

- **Burney v. Kansas Department of Social & Rehabilitation Services**, 23 Kan. App. 2d 394, 931 P.2d 26 (1997)
  Holding: The court reversed a negligence verdict for a substitute teacher who had been investigated for child abuse, holding that SRS owes no duty to an alleged abuser, that there is no special relationship between the agency and a suspect, and that both the decision whether to open a file and the manner of conducting a child abuse investigation are discretionary functions immune under the Kansas Tort Claims Act.
  Why here: The court also held SRS immune for failing to turn over its files to law enforcement, illustrating the breadth of the disclosure immunity.
  Quote: “we hold that in investigating child abuse cases, SRS owes no duty to the alleged abuser”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1169765/burney-v-kansas-department-of-social-rehabilitation-services/
- **Data Tree, LLC v. Meek**, 279 Kan. 445, 109 P.3d 1226 (2005)
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for the Register of Deeds, holding that KORA's exceptions to disclosure are narrowly construed with the burden on the agency opposing disclosure, but that where releasing personal identifiers would not significantly serve KORA's purpose nondisclosure is favored and the requester may be charged the reasonable cost of redaction.
  Why here: This is the Kansas Supreme Court's allocation of the burden when a public agency refuses to release records: the agency, not the requester, must justify the withholding.
  Quote: “narrowly interpreted, and the burden is on the public agency opposing disclosure”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2520291/data-tree-llc-v-meek/

The entry is built from the discretionary-function immunity for the decision not to open a file (Beebe), the reasonable-efforts flexibility recognized in In re T.S., and the federal rule that reasonable efforts are not privately enforceable (Suter v. Artist M.).
- OUT-04 (Extended foster care / aging out) has no Kansas appellate decision construing the Chafee or FCSIAA-funded extended-care provisions or K.S.A. 38-2203(b) custody past age 18. The cases mapped (In re N.A.C., In re N.E., In re Guardianship & Conservatorship of B.H.) govern the jurisdictional and permanency framework within which an older youth's placement is decided; they are the closest available Kansas authority and no case squarely addresses aging-out rights.
- ACT-05 (Protective Order) has no Kansas child-welfare appellate case on no-contact or abuser-removal orders entered under the Revised Kansas Code for Care of Children. The mapped cases address the protective custody / temporary custody machinery (In re A.E.S.), the notice due before protective adjudication (In re J.L.), and the Fourth Amendment limits on protective seizures (Halley v. - No reported Kansas or Tenth Circuit decision addresses children who go missing from foster care. That topic is not separately mapped; ACT-03 and OUT-06 carry the nearest authority on the State's custodial duty (Schwartz v. Booker; T.D. v. Patton).
- Kansas child-welfare tort exposure is unusually narrow. Beebe v. Fraktman and Burney v. Kansas SRS together hold that (1) the reporting/investigation statutes create a duty to the public at large only, (2) the decision whether to open a file is a discretionary function, and (3) the manner of conducting an abuse investigation is also a discretionary function immune under K.S.A. 75-6104(d) and (e). A Kansas plaintiff harmed by a negligent investigation will ordinarily have to proceed under 42 U.S.C. section 1983 in the Tenth Circuit rather than in state tort.
- For section 1983 removal and investigation claims, the Tenth Circuit is the controlling federal forum for Kansas. Roska (2002) and Lowther (2024) should be read together: Roska establishes that warrantless, non-exigent entry to remove a child violates the Fourth Amendment, while Lowther holds that reasonable suspicion of past abuse can make warrantless entry at least not clearly unlawful, so qualified immunity frequently defeats such claims today. Doe v. Woodard likewise resolves the searching of a child at school on clearly-established grounds without deciding whether the special needs doctrine applies, leaving the underlying question open in this circuit.
- Estate of B.I.C. ex rel. C.S.C. v. Gillen arises from Kansas facts (a Kansas SRS social worker and the 2008 death of a 23-month-old in Kansas) and is the most directly transferable federal authority in this brief for child-fatality accountability in this jurisdiction. A later appeal in the same litigation (761 F.3d 1099 (10th Cir. 2014)) was not retrieved or relied on here.

- In re B.D.-Y. is the load-bearing Kansas case for this entire dataset: it defines 'clear and convincing evidence' as 'highly probable' for both CINC adjudication and termination, disapproves all contrary prior Kansas holdings, and supplies the appellate standard restated in In re D.G. (2024). Any Kansas node that turns on a burden of proof should cite it.
- In re A.S. (2024) is the newest structural due-process rule in Kansas termination practice: a parent who appears must be able to see, hear, speak, and consult counsel, and any waiver must be knowing, voluntary, intelligent, and on the record. It is likely to be the most frequently invoked recent authority for families challenging a termination hearing's conduct.
- Appellate jurisdiction is a recurring trap in Kansas CINC practice. In re N.A.C. (2014) and In re N.E. (2022) together hold that K.S.A. 38-2273(a) lists only five appealable order types, that post-termination permanency orders are unappealable, and that placement orders (as distinct from custody orders) cannot be reviewed at all. Relatives, kin, and foster parents should be advised that the practical window for appellate review closes at the termination order.
