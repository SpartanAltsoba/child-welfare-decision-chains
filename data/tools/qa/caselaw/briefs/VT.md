# VT — 47 authorities, verified 2026-09-21

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Willis S. Sheldon, Individually and as Administrator of the Estate of Dezirae Sheldon v. Nicholas Ruggiero**, 2018 VT 125, 202 A.3d 241
  Holding: Summary judgment for a DCF administrative reviewer was affirmed because the mandated-reporter duty under 33 V.S.A. § 4913(a) is triggered only by reasonable cause to believe a child has been abused, and a repetitious allegation adding nothing new did not supply it.
  Why here: Sheldon is Vermont's leading modern decision on when § 4913(a) actually obliges a mandated reporter to report, and it holds the trigger is reasonable cause to believe, judged on what the reporter newly learned.
  Quote: “In this context, as a matter of law, defendant did not have reasonable cause to make a report.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4552217/willis-s-sheldon-individually-and-as-administrator-of-the-estate-of/
- **LaShay v. Department of Social & Rehabilitation Services**, 160 Vt. 60, 625 A.2d 224 (1993)
  Holding: A caseworker supervisor was not entitled to official immunity on a claim that he ignored warnings about a foster home where the child was later sexually abused, and SRS waived sovereign immunity to the extent of its liability insurance.
  Why here: LaShay holds that where reporting is mandatory it is a ministerial duty, so a Vermont official who fails to report cannot claim discretionary-act immunity.
  Quote: “Further, if reporting were mandatory, it was a ministerial, not a discretionary duty.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8209320/lashay-v-department-of-social-rehabilitation-services/
- **Sabia v. State**, 164 Vt. 293, 669 A.2d 1187 (1995)
  Holding: SRS (now DCF) owed the reporting children an actionable duty of care because 33 V.S.A. § 4915 imposes mandatory investigative acts for the protection of a particular class, and sovereign immunity did not bar the negligence suit.
  Why here: Sabia holds that the mandatory-investigation statute triggered by a report creates a duty running to the identified children, which is what gives a mandated report legal consequence in Vermont.
  Quote: “Accordingly, we conclude that SRS had a duty to protect plaintiffs under the circumstances of this case.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1983925/sabia-v-state/

## INP-02 · Voluntary / Third-Party Report
- **Sabia v. State**, 164 Vt. 293, 669 A.2d 1187 (1995)
  Holding: SRS (now DCF) owed the reporting children an actionable duty of care because 33 V.S.A. § 4915 imposes mandatory investigative acts for the protection of a particular class, and sovereign immunity did not bar the negligence suit.
  Why here: A report from any source that identifies particular endangered children can create a special duty of care in DCF, not merely a duty to the public at large.
  Quote: “Accordingly, we conclude that SRS had a duty to protect plaintiffs under the circumstances of this case.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1983925/sabia-v-state/

  Why here: It clarifies that a third-party allegation that duplicates information DCF already has does not itself generate a fresh reporting duty.
  Quote: “In this context, as a matter of law, defendant did not have reasonable cause to make a report.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4552217/willis-s-sheldon-individually-and-as-administrator-of-the-estate-of/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **In re E.W.**, 2015 VT 7, 198 Vt. 311, 114 A.3d 112
  Holding: A fifteen-year-old in DCF custody was in custody for Miranda purposes when questioned by police at his foster home, so the resulting statements were suppressed and the trial court judgment reversed.
  Why here: E.W. governs police questioning of a child in state custody and the role of an 'independent interested adult' when the foster parent is the adult present.
  Quote: “We conclude that the juvenile was in custody, and therefore reverse the trial court judgment.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2770572/in-re-ew/
- **In re E.T.C.**, 141 Vt. 375, 449 A.2d 937 (1982)
  Holding: The delinquency adjudication was reversed and the juvenile's statements suppressed because, under Chapter I, Article 10 of the Vermont Constitution, a juvenile may waive counsel and the privilege against self-incrimination only after an opportunity to consult privately with an informed adult who is independent of the prosecution, and the state-contracted group-home director was not such an adult.
  Why here: E.T.C. is the foundational Vermont rule governing police contact with children, requiring an independent interested adult before any juvenile waiver, and it arose from state troopers questioning children who were in SRS custody in a state-contracted group home.
  Quote: “Although our prior cases apply only to waivers in court, we now hold that the same principles apply to police interrogation of juvenile suspects.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8170913/in-re-e-t-c/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **In re M.C.P.**, 153 Vt. 275, 571 A.2d 627 (1989)
  Holding: The Court remanded for ICWA notice to the Mohawk Tribe because the trial court had reason to believe an Indian child was involved, and otherwise affirmed the CHINS merits and disposition orders, holding the preponderance standard governs a CHINS adjudication and that merits and disposition orders must rest on findings of fact.
  Why here: M.C.P. requires that any court-driven juvenile order rest on timely written findings, which constrains a judge who directs CPS action.
  Quote: “the standard of proof for a CHINS adjudication is that the findings be supported by a preponderance of the evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8209107/in-re-mcp/
- **In re A.M., Juvenile**, 2015 VT 109, 200 Vt. 189, 130 A.3d 211
  Holding: A disposition order continuing DCF custody was affirmed; the trial court had discretion to decline new evidence on a relative's suitability where it had recently taken extensive evidence on that question, and could rely on its earlier findings.
  Why here: A.M. defines the evidentiary control a judge retains over disposition proceedings in a CHINS case.
  Quote: “mother's rights were not violated by the court's decision not to hear additional evidence of grandmother's suitability”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3132329/in-re-am-juvenile/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In re K.M.M.**, 2011 VT 30, 189 Vt. 372
  Holding: Denial of the grandfather's petition to terminate parental rights was affirmed, but the refusal to terminate the voluntary guardianship was reversed and the case remanded to transfer custody of the child to her fit father.
  Why here: K.M.M. arises from a voluntary guardianship the parents themselves entered, and shows how that consensual arrangement is later unwound.
  Quote: “Once the court made findings supporting the denial of the termination of parental rights petition, there was no reason not to return K.M.M. to her fit parent”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8210061/in-re-kmm/
- **In re C.B., Juvenile**, 2020 VT 80
  Holding: Termination was affirmed; while a CHINS case is pending the probate division cannot adjudicate a minor guardianship petition, but the family division may do so after transfer under 14 V.S.A. § 2624(b), and father's unpreserved objection to the absence of a relative suitability assessment failed.
  Why here: C.B. maps where a relative's own guardianship petition must be filed and heard once DCF is already involved with the child.
  Quote: “while a CHINS case is pending, the probate division does not have jurisdiction to adjudicate the minor guardianship petition”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4789485/in-re-cb-juvenile/
- **In re Guardianship of S.O. (L.O. and T.O., Appellants)**, 2021 VT 89, 268 A.3d 49
  Holding: Dismissal of grandparents' guardianship petition without a merits hearing was affirmed because, once parental rights were terminated and residual rights transferred to DCF, 14 V.S.A. §§ 2626 and 2627 gave them no right to a consensual or nonconsensual guardianship.
  Why here: S.O. shows the statutory limits on a relative's own petition for guardianship once DCF holds custody and rights have been terminated.
  Quote: “we conclude as a matter of law that grandparents are not entitled to a consensual or nonconsensual guardianship under the plain language of 14 V.S.A. §§ 2626 and 2627”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5296943/in-re-guardianship-of-so-lo-and-to-appellants/

## INP-07 · Medical Disclosure
- **Elizabeth Lawson v. Patricia Halpern-Reiss and Central Vermont Medical Center**, 2019 VT 38, 212 A.3d 1213
  Holding: The Court recognized a common-law private right of action for a medical provider's unjustified disclosure of treatment information, but affirmed judgment for the hospital because the disclosure fell within the exception for mitigating an imminent and serious threat.
  Why here: It is the controlling Vermont authority on the scope and limits of a medical provider's disclosure of treatment information to third parties including law enforcement.
  Quote: “no reasonable factfinder could determine that the disclosure was for any purpose other than to mitigate the threat of imminent and serious harm to plaintiff and the public”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4620838/elizabeth-lawson-v-patricia-halpern-reiss-and-central-vermont-medical/
- **Kia P. v. McIntyre**, 235 F.3d 749 (2d Cir. 2000)
  Holding: Summary judgment for the defendants was affirmed because during the relevant period the newborn was held by the hospital for medical reasons rather than by the child-welfare agency, so no Fourth or Fourteenth Amendment violation arose from the retention.
  Why here: Kia P. separates a medical hold arising from a positive newborn toxicology screen from state child-protective custody, which determines which constitutional rules apply.
  Quote: “Mora was held by the Hospital for medical reasons, not concerns about possible parental mistreatment and CWA policies”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7085469/kia-p-v-mcintyre/
- **In re K.R.**, 2015 VT 58, 199 Vt. 191, 121 A.3d 1207
  Holding: The substantiation and registry placement were reversed because they had to be supported by sufficient evidence known to DCF at the time of substantiation, and the petitioner's later admissions could not retroactively justify them.
  Why here: A hospital report of a parent's overdose triggered the investigation, and K.R. limits how far that medical information can carry a finding.
  Quote: “However, after-acquired evidence cannot salvage a substantiation that was not supported by sufficient evidence in the first place.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2792824/in-re-kr/
- **Willis S. Sheldon, Individually and as Administrator of the Estate of Dezirae Sheldon v. Nicholas Ruggiero**, 2018 VT 125, 202 A.3d 241
  Holding: Summary judgment for a DCF administrative reviewer was affirmed because the mandated-reporter duty under 33 V.S.A. § 4913(a) is triggered only by reasonable cause to believe a child has been abused, and a repetitious allegation adding nothing new did not supply it.
  Why here: Dezirae's injuries came to DCF through a hospital presentation, and the opinion traces how medical findings feed the § 4915 investigation.
  Quote: “In this context, as a matter of law, defendant did not have reasonable cause to make a report.”

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **In re Appeal of T.O. & L.O.**, 2021 VT 41, 257 A.3d 240
  Holding: The Human Services Board lacked jurisdiction over grandparents' claim that DCF failed to comply with state and federal relative-placement requirements; their remedy lies in the family division of the superior court, and no due process violation resulted.
  Why here: T.O. & L.O. fixes the boundary of the Human Services Board's oversight jurisdiction over DCF conduct in an open CHINS case.
  Quote: “That procedure is access to the family division, not to the Human Services Board.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4890927/in-re-appeal-of-to-lo/
- **Toensing v. Attorney Gen. of Vt.**, 178 A.3d 1000 (Vt. 2017)
  Holding: Records produced or acquired in the course of agency business are public records under the Public Records Act even when they sit in employees' private accounts, and where a requester specifically seeks such communications the agency must ask the named employees to produce them.
  Why here: Toensing determines what agency material an outside investigator or oversight body can actually obtain from DCF and other Vermont agencies.
  Quote: “the PRA's definition of "public record" includes digital documents stored in private accounts, but emphasize that it extends only to documents that otherwise meet the definition of public records”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6483671/toensing-v-attorney-gen-of-vt/
- **In re Appeal of H.H.**, 2020 VT 107
  Holding: Summary judgment applying collateral estoppel to a registry substantiation was reversed because the Human Services Board could not inspect the related CHINS file without a need-to-know designation from the family court under 33 V.S.A. § 5117(b)(1)(F), and the stipulated facts did not establish what the substantiation required.
  Why here: H.H. limits what an oversight or adjudicative body may see in juvenile-court files absent an express judicial designation.
  Quote: “A need-to-know designation from the family court was required before the Board could inspect the contents of the juvenile files in question.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4844385/in-re-appeal-of-hh/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In re M.C.P.**, 153 Vt. 275, 571 A.2d 627 (1989)
  Holding: The Court remanded for ICWA notice to the Mohawk Tribe because the trial court had reason to believe an Indian child was involved, and otherwise affirmed the CHINS merits and disposition orders, holding the preponderance standard governs a CHINS adjudication and that merits and disposition orders must rest on findings of fact.
  Why here: This is Vermont's first ICWA decision; it requires the court itself to notify a tribe once it has reason to believe an Indian child is involved, and it construes the 'Indian child' and 'Indian tribe' definitions.
  Quote: “the standard of proof for a CHINS adjudication is that the findings be supported by a preponderance of the evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8209107/in-re-mcp/
- **In re J.T.**, 166 Vt. 173, 693 A.2d 283 (1997)
  Holding: The termination case was remanded for notice to the Bureau of Indian Affairs because the court itself had a duty to give ICWA notice once information in a court-ordered evaluation gave it reason to believe the children were of Indian ancestry.
  Why here: J.T. places the ICWA notice duty on the Vermont family court, not only on the petitioning party, and triggers it on information already in the court's own record.
  Quote: “We conclude that the trial court had a duty to provide notice once it had reason to believe the children were of Indian ancestry.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8209504/in-re-jt/
- **In re A.J.**, 169 Vt. 577, 733 A.2d 36 (1999) (mem.)
  Holding: No ICWA notice was required where the asserted tribes, the Abenaki and Micmac, are not federally recognized, and excluding non-recognized tribes from ICWA does not violate equal protection.
  Why here: This is the decisive Vermont case for practitioners, because the tribes most often asserted in Vermont, the Abenaki and Micmac, lack federal recognition and therefore do not trigger ICWA.
  Quote: “we agree with the court's conclusion in T.I.S. that the ICWA exclusion of non-recognized tribes does not violate the Equal Protection Clause”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8209593/in-re-aj/
- **In re K.S., Juvenile**, 2021 VT 51
  Holding: The family division had reason to know K.S. might be an Indian child once a parent reported Cherokee heritage on the record, but after notice was given and the tribes responded, the determination that K.S. was not an Indian child was affirmed along with the termination order.
  Why here: K.S. applies the 2016 federal ICWA regulations in Vermont, holding that a participant's statement of tribal heritage anywhere in the record, including a permanency case plan, triggers the inquiry and notice duty.
  Quote: “father's statement was sufficient to give the court reason to know that K.S. was an Indian child for purposes of the ICWA”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4897065/in-re-ks-juvenile/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **Tenenbaum v. Williams**, 193 F.3d 581 (2d Cir. 1999)
  Holding: Triable issues existed on the parents' procedural due process claim and the child's Fourth Amendment unreasonable-seizure claim arising from her removal from school without a court order, although the defendants retained qualified immunity because the rule was not clearly established in 1990.
  Why here: Tenenbaum is the governing Second Circuit rule for Vermont on when protective custody may be taken without prior judicial authorization.
  Quote: “where there is reasonable time consistent with the safety of the child to obtain a judicial order, the “emergency” removal of a child is unwarranted”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7079141/tenenbaum-v-williams/
- **Cornejo v. Bell**, 592 F.3d 121 (2d Cir. 2010)
  Holding: Agency lawyers who initiate and prosecute child-protective proceedings have absolute immunity from § 1983 claims, but caseworkers do not, because their removal decisions are the functional equivalent of an arrest; on these facts the caseworkers were nevertheless protected by qualified immunity.
  Why here: It confirms that the official who takes a child into protective custody is subject to the ordinary objective-reasonableness test rather than absolute immunity.
  Quote: “The caseworker defendants are not entitled to absolute immunity on plaintiffs § 1983 claims because their actions were the functional equivalent of arresting officers in criminal cases.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1356186/cornejo-v-bell/
- **Kia P. v. McIntyre**, 235 F.3d 749 (2d Cir. 2000)
  Holding: Summary judgment for the defendants was affirmed because during the relevant period the newborn was held by the hospital for medical reasons rather than by the child-welfare agency, so no Fourth or Fourteenth Amendment violation arose from the retention.
  Why here: It shows that not every hospital retention of a child amounts to a state seizure triggering protective-custody analysis.
  Quote: “Mora was held by the Hospital for medical reasons, not concerns about possible parental mistreatment and CWA policies”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7085469/kia-p-v-mcintyre/
- **In re I.B., Juvenile**, 2016 VT 70, 202 Vt. 311, 149 A.3d 160
  Holding: A post-disposition order transferring custody of a child back to DCF is a final appealable order, and the preponderance standard, not clear and convincing evidence, governs it because the deprivation is temporary rather than a permanent severance.
  Why here: I.B. distinguishes the lower proof required for temporary state custody from the clear and convincing showing required for permanent severance.
  Quote: “In its decision to modify the plan to transfer legal custody of I.B. back to DCF, therefore, the trial court here applied the proper standard of proof.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3214409/in-re-ib-juvenile/
- **Southerland v. City of New York**, 680 F.3d 127 (2d Cir. 2012)
  Holding: Summary judgment for the caseworker was vacated on the Fourth Amendment unlawful-search claims arising from a family-court entry order obtained through allegedly false statements, on the procedural due process claims, and on the children's Fourth Amendment unlawful-seizure claim, because qualified immunity could not be resolved as a matter of law on that record; summary judgment was affirmed only on the father's substantive due process claim.
  Why here: Southerland is the leading modern Second Circuit statement, binding in Vermont, that a child may not be taken into protective custody absent a court order, parental consent, or genuine emergency circumstances.
  Quote: “the Children's right not to be taken from the care of their parent without court order, parental consent, or emergency circumstances was firmly established”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/800656/southerland-v-city-of-new-york/

## OUT-02 · Termination of Parental Rights / Adoption
- **Santosky v. Kramer**, 455 U.S. 745 (1982)
  Holding: The Due Process Clause requires a State to support its allegations by at least clear and convincing evidence before it may permanently terminate the rights of parents in their natural child, so New York's fair-preponderance standard was unconstitutional.
  Why here: Santosky supplies the constitutional standard of proof that 33 V.S.A. ch. 53 termination proceedings must meet, and Vermont courts apply clear and convincing evidence at termination for exactly this reason.

- **Lassiter v. Department of Social Servs. of Durham Cty.**, 452 U.S. 18 (1981)
  Holding: The Constitution does not require appointment of counsel for indigent parents in every termination proceeding; the trial court decides case by case, subject to appellate review, and no error occurred on these facts.
  Why here: Lassiter is the federal floor for counsel at termination; Vermont exceeds it by statute (13 V.S.A. § 5232(3)), as In re C.B. and In re L.H. confirm, so a Vermont parent's counsel claim rests on the statute rather than on Lassiter alone.

- ICWA in Vermont is unusual and this is the single most important practical point in the layer. Under In re A.J. the Abenaki and Micmac - the tribes most often asserted by Vermont families - are not federally recognized and therefore do not trigger ICWA, and the exclusion survives equal-protection review. ICWA still applies where a federally recognized tribe is implicated: In re M.C.P. (Mohawk) required remand for notice, In re J.T. placed the notice duty on the court itself, and In re K.S. applies the 2016 federal regulations at 25 C.F.R. sec. 23.107. Vermont has no state ICWA statute and no case law on qualified expert witnesses, active efforts or the sec. 1915 placement preferences - if those issues arise, there is no Vermont authority and counsel must argue from the federal statute, the regulations and out-of-state persuasive authority.
- Standards of proof in Vermont run on three levels and the layer reflects them: preponderance at CHINS merits and at post-disposition custody modification (In re M.C.P.; In re B.R.; In re I.B.), the DCF 'reasonable person would believe' standard for registry substantiation (In re R.H.; In re M.G. and K.G.), and clear and convincing evidence only where the State seeks to sever the parent-child relationship permanently (In re I.B., citing In re T.E.; Santosky v. Kramer).
- Vermont's registry line (In re R.H., In re M.G. and K.G., In re K.R., In re M.S., In re Appeal of H.H.) is the richest body of Vermont child-welfare case law and should be treated as the backbone of DEC-03. Note the practical asymmetry: the Supreme Court has reversed the Human Services Board for being too lenient toward parents (R.H., M.G. and K.G.) and reversed DCF for substantiating on an inadequate record (K.R., H.H.).
- Right to counsel: Vermont parents' counsel right in juvenile proceedings comes from 13 V.S.A. sec. 5232(3) and is broader than the federal case-by-case floor in Lassiter (see In re C.B. and In re L.H.). As of In re K.F.
