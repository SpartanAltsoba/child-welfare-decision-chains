## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **B.H. v. County of San Bernardino**, 62 Cal. 4th 168, 361 P.3d 319 (2015)
  Holding: The court held that a law enforcement agency's CANRA duty to cross-report a child abuse report it receives to the county child welfare agency is a mandatory duty enforceable under Government Code section 815.6, distinct from the responding officer's separate investigative duties.
  Why here: Is the leading California Supreme Court decision on the mandatory character of CANRA reporting and cross-reporting duties and the consequences of breaching them.
  Quote: “These provisions indicate that a law enforcement agency's duty to cross-report the receipt of an initial child abuse or neglect report is separate from its investigative duties.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3158633/bh-v-county-of-san-bernardino/
- **Ferraro v. Chadwick**, 221 Cal. App. 3d 86, 270 Cal. Rptr. 379 (1990)
  Holding: The court held that the absolute immunity Penal Code section 11172(a) confers on mandated reporters barred the parents' claims against the physician and hospital arising from a report of suspected child abuse, notwithstanding the loss of any civil remedy.
  Why here: States the rationale and reach of California's absolute statutory immunity for mandated reporters under Penal Code section 11172.
  Quote: “The Legislature's solution in bestowing absolute immunity to the enumerated professionals was an attempt to remove one of the largest impediments to reporting”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2126493/ferraro-v-chadwick/
- **Landeros v. Flood**, 17 Cal. 3d 399, 551 P.2d 389 (1976)
  Holding: The court reversed a dismissal and held that a child states a cause of action against a physician and hospital for negligently failing to diagnose battered child syndrome and to report it as Penal Code section 11161 then required, because both the standard of care and proximate cause are questions of fact.
  Why here: Is the foundational California decision that a mandated reporter's failure to report can itself be actionable negligence, the duty side of the reporting scheme.
  Quote: “Accordingly, the trial court in the case at bar could not properly rule as a matter of law that the defendants' negligence was not the proximate cause of plaintiff's injuries.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1175924/landeros-v-flood/
- **Cuff v. Grossmont Union High School District**, 221 Cal. App. 4th 582, 164 Cal. Rptr. 3d 487 (2013)
  Holding: The court reversed summary judgment and held that the CANRA immunity of Penal Code section 11172(a) does not shield a school employee who gave a copy of her suspected child abuse report to the children's father, a person not among the recipients the statute authorizes.
  Why here: Marks the boundary of California's mandated reporter immunity: it protects the act of reporting to an authorized agency and nothing beyond it.
  Quote: “In other words, a mandated reporter may not be sued for making a required or authorized report to an authorized agency.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2642880/cuff-v-grossmont-union-high-school-district/

  Quote: “We conclude that the standard of proof for termination of parental rights under the child dependency statutes comports with the requirements of due process.”

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In re Isaiah W.**, No. S221263 (Cal. July 7, 2016)
  Holding: The court held that because the juvenile court has a continuing duty to inquire into a child's Indian status, a parent may challenge an ICWA-inapplicability finding on appeal from a later termination order even though no timely appeal was taken from the earlier order that subsumed the finding.
  Why here: States the affirmative and continuing ICWA inquiry duty that attaches at the very first dependency hearing and never expires during the case.
  Quote: “In sum, a juvenile court has an affirmative and continuing duty in all dependency proceedings to inquire into a child's Indian status.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4236568/in-re-isaiah-w/
- **In re Abbigail A.**, 1 Cal. 5th 83, 375 P.3d 879 (2016)
  Holding: The court held that California Rule of Court 5.482(c), which directed juvenile courts to proceed as if a child were an Indian child while a tribal membership application was pending, is invalid as inconsistent with the governing statutes, while rule 5.484(c)(2) is valid.
  Why here: Defines who is and is not an 'Indian child' for California ICWA purposes and rejects a state rule that would extend ICWA protections to children merely eligible for membership.
  Quote: “Rule 5.482(c) is inconsistent with those statutes, and with the Legislature's intent, and thus invalid.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4238565/in-re-abbigail-a/
- **In re Dezi C.**, No. S275578 (Cal. Aug. 19, 2024)
  Holding: The court held that an agency's failure to conduct the initial inquiry required by California's ICWA statutes requires conditional reversal of the order terminating parental rights, with a remand directing the agency to complete an adequate, record-documented inquiry.
  Why here: Is the current controlling California remedy rule for inadequate Cal-ICWA initial inquiry by the child welfare agency.
  Quote: “The conditional reversal rule we adopt today clearly distinguishes between the separate issues of whether an inquiry is adequate and whether inquiry error is harmless.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10045012/in-re-dezi-c/
- **C.F. v. Superior Court**, 230 Cal. App. 4th 227, 178 Cal. Rptr. 3d 456 (2014)
  Holding: The court held that substantial evidence supported the juvenile court's finding by clear and convincing evidence that active efforts had been made, and that under California law 'active efforts' under ICWA and 'reasonable services' under section 366.21(g)(1) do not differ significantly.
  Why here: Defines the content of the ICWA 'active efforts' obligation as California courts apply it and the standard of review for that finding.
  Quote: “Under California law, there is no significant difference between "active efforts" required by ICWA and "reasonable services" required in both ICWA and non-ICWA cases under section 366.21, subdivision (g)(1).”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5811817/cf-v-superior-court/
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that 'domicile' under ICWA is a matter of uniform federal law, that children born off-reservation to reservation-domiciled parents were domiciled on the reservation, and that the tribal court therefore had exclusive jurisdiction over their adoption.
  Why here: Is the foundational Supreme Court decision on ICWA's exclusive tribal jurisdiction and on reading ICWA's terms as uniform federal law rather than by state definitions.
  Quote: “One reason for this rule of construction is that federal statutes are generally intended to have uniform nationwide application.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **Kirkpatrick v. County of Washoe**, 843 F.3d 784 (9th Cir. 2016)
  Holding: Sitting en banc, the court held that officials ordinarily must obtain prior judicial authorization before removing a child from parental custody, and that a genuine dispute existed as to whether the newborn here was in imminent danger of serious bodily injury such that no warrant was required.
  Why here: Sets the constitutional ceiling on protective custody taken without a court order in California's circuit.
  Quote: “Under the Fourth Amendment, government officials are ordinarily required to obtain prior judicial authorization before removing a child from the custody of her parent.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4328788/jamie-kirkpatrick-v-county-of-washoe/
- **Rogers v. County of San Joaquin**, 487 F.3d 1288 (9th Cir. 2007)
  Holding: The court held that a California social worker violated the family's Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights by removing children without a warrant where the conditions observed presented no imminent risk of serious bodily harm, and denied qualified immunity.
  Why here: Marks the constitutional boundary of emergency protective custody taken by a California social worker.
  Quote: “Thus, we hold that, under any view of the facts, the Rogerses' Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights were violated when Royal removed the children without a warrant.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/797914/rogers-v-county-of-san-joaquin/
- **In re Henry V.**, 119 Cal. App. 4th 522, 14 Cal. Rptr. 3d 496 (2004)
  Holding: The court reversed a dispositional removal order because the record did not show by clear and convincing evidence that there were no reasonable means of protecting the child short of removal, and it was not apparent the juvenile court had applied the clear and convincing standard.
  Why here: Sets the substantive limit on continued protective custody once the matter reaches the dependency court.
  Quote: “Removal on any ground not involving parental rejection, abandonment, or institutionalization requires a finding that there are no reasonable means of protecting the child without depriving the parent of custody.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5809151/in-re-henry-v/
- **Wallis v. Spencer**, 202 F.3d 1126 (9th Cir. 2000)
  Holding: The court held that parents and children have a constitutional right to live together free of governmental interference, that children may not be removed without a court order absent reasonable cause to believe of imminent danger of serious bodily injury, and that intrusive medical examinations of children in protective custody may not be conducted without a court order or parental notice and consent.
  Why here: Defines the constitutional limits on emergency protective custody and on what follows it.
  Quote: “Parents and children have a well-elaborated constitutional right to live together without governmental interference.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7079922/wallis-ex-rel-wallis-v-spencer/
- **Mabe v. San Bernardino County**, 237 F.3d 1101 (9th Cir. 2001)
  Holding: The court held that a California county social worker was not entitled to summary judgment on qualified immunity for a warrantless removal, because a jury could find that the child faced no imminent danger of serious bodily injury even though there was probable cause sufficient to obtain a removal warrant.
  Why here: Marks how little time pressure will excuse taking a child into protective custody before a court order issues.
  Quote: “Nonetheless, imminent danger of future harm is required to show exigency.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/771798/mabe-v-san-bernardino-county/

## PMC-04 · Fatality Review Commission
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative obligation on a state to protect a child from private violence, so a county's failure to remove a child it knew was being abused by his father did not violate substantive due process.
  Why here: Is the reason fatality review commissions exist: the Constitution supplies no remedy for most agency failures to protect.
  Quote: “But nothing in the language of the Due Process Clause itself requires the State to protect the life, liberty, and property of its citizens against invasion by private actors.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-cty-dept-of-social-servs/
- **B.H. v. County of San Bernardino**, 62 Cal. 4th 168, 361 P.3d 319 (2015)
  Holding: The court held that a law enforcement agency's CANRA duty to cross-report a child abuse report it receives to the county child welfare agency is a mandatory duty enforceable under Government Code section 815.6, distinct from the responding officer's separate investigative duties.
  Why here: Supplies the private enforcement mechanism available when a fatality review traces the death to an agency's failure to perform a mandatory reporting duty.
  Quote: “These provisions indicate that a law enforcement agency's duty to cross-report the receipt of an initial child abuse or neglect report is separate from its investigative duties.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3158633/bh-v-county-of-san-bernardino/
- **Jacob B. v. County of Shasta**, 40 Cal. 4th 948, 154 P.3d 1003 (2007)
  Holding: The court held that the Civil Code section 47(b) litigation privilege barred a damages action based on a county's disclosure of confidential juvenile record information in a letter filed in a family law proceeding, and that the privilege applies even to a claim grounded in the state constitutional right of privacy.
  Why here: Bears on who may obtain confidential juvenile records when reviewing an agency's handling of a case.
  Quote: “We conclude that the litigation privilege applies even to a constitutionally based privacy cause of action.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2626972/jacob-b-v-county-of-shasta/

- Missing (for a later pass): In re Stephanie M. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 295, 871 P.2d 1198 — the California Supreme Court's leading decision on the section 361.3 relative placement preference and the abuse-of-discretion standard governing placement decisions. Its absence is the most serious gap in the brief: ACT-04 (Kinship Placement) currently has one genuine California case (Isabella G.) plus Palmore.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Chantal S. (1996) 13 Cal.4th 196, 921 P.2d 1229 — California Supreme Court on juvenile court exit orders and the family court's authority after dependency jurisdiction terminates. Directly fills OUT-01 and OUT-05, which currently have no authority on what happens when a case actually closes.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Drake M. (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 754 — the source of the 'tender years' substance abuse presumption that In re N.R. rejected. A practitioner citing N.R. at DEC-03 or DEC-05 must know what N.R. disapproved; the brief cites N.R. without it.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re A.R. (2021) 11 Cal.5th 234, 486 P.3d 1076 — California Supreme Court holding that a parent may obtain relief when appointed appellate counsel fails to file a timely notice of appeal from a termination order. A core FAIL-01 authority on relief from counsel error, far more apt there than Lassiter.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Christopher L. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 1063, 512 P.3d 928 — California Supreme Court on an incarcerated parent's rights to presence and counsel at jurisdiction and disposition, and the harmless-error analysis that applies. Belongs at FAIL-01 alongside In re James F.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Jesusa V. (2004) 32 Cal.4th 588, 86 P.3d 878 — California Supreme Court applying harmless-error review to statutory violations in dependency, including an incarcerated parent's absence. A standard companion to James F. at FAIL-01.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Josiah Z. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 664, 115 P.3d 1133 — California Supreme Court on appointed appellate counsel's authority in dependency appeals and protection of the child's interests on appeal. Pairs with In re Sade C. at FAIL-01 and OUT-02.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Autumn H. (1994) 27 Cal.App.4th 567 — the origin of the beneficial parental relationship 'benefit to the child' standard that In re Caden C. construed and partly reframed. OUT-02 cites Caden C. without the authority it was interpreting.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Alexandria P. (2016) 1 Cal.App.5th 331 — leading California decision on ICWA placement preferences and the 'good cause' showing required to deviate from them. INP-11 covers ICWA inquiry, membership and active efforts but has nothing on placement preference, which is ICWA's operative protection.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Elizabeth R. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1774 — reunification services and the reasonable-services finding for a parent with a mental disability, including the section 352 continuance route. Fills the disability dimension missing from both ACT-02 and FAIL-05.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Angelia P. (1981) 28 Cal.3d 908, 623 P.2d 198 — California's pre-Santosky adoption of the clear and convincing standard for termination of parental rights. The California half of the standard-of-proof story that OUT-02 tells only through Santosky and Cynthia D.
- Missing (for a later pass): Planned Parenthood Affiliates v. Van de Kamp (1986) 181 Cal.App.3d 245 — leading California decision on the scope of the CANRA reporting duty and what does and does not constitute reportable abuse. Would give INP-01 a scope-of-duty authority and INP-08 a genuine second case.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Nolan W. (2009) 45 Cal.4th 1217, 213 P.3d 1031 — California Supreme Court holding that contempt is not available to coerce a parent's participation in court-ordered reunification services. A direct ACT-02 authority on the limits of enforcing a service order.
