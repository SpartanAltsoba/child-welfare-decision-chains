# NH — 42 authorities, verified 2026-09-21

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **State v. Howland**, 125 N.H. 497, 484 A.2d 1076 (1984)
  Holding: The court held that the good faith immunity of RSA 169-C:31 reaches only liability arising from the act of making a report and extends only to persons within the class defined by RSA 169-C:29, so an alleged perpetrator who reports his own abuse is not immunized from prosecution for the underlying abuse.
  Why here: This decision fixes the scope of reporter immunity that accompanies the mandatory reporting duty: immunity attaches to the report, not to the reporter's own conduct.
  Quote: “the words “person having reason to suspect,” which define the class of persons required to report, also define the grant of immunity”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8089658/state-v-howland/
- **Berry v. Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc.**, 152 N.H. 407, 879 A.2d 1124 (2005)
  Holding: Affirming dismissal, the court held that even assuming congregation elders were obligated to report suspected abuse under RSA 169-C:29, the abused children had no private cause of action for the failure to report and the religious organizations owed them no common law or fiduciary duty of protection.
  Why here: The court applied the Marquay rule to non-professional mandated reporters, confirming that the reporting mandate reaches 'any other person' but is not privately enforceable.
  Quote: “the plaintiffs may not bring a private cause of action for the alleged failure of the elders to comply with RSA 169-C:29”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8093047/berry-v-watchtower-bible-tract-society-of-new-york-inc/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Tower v. Leslie-Brown**, 326 F.3d 290 (1st Cir. 2003)
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for a caseworker and a state trooper, holding that removal of all five children was supported by an objectively reasonable suspicion of abuse and an imminent threat of abuse, and that a post-deprivation hearing three business days after the removal satisfied procedural due process.
  Why here: The removal grew out of a school guidance counselor's report and the ensuing police response, and the court evaluates the joint law enforcement and caseworker action.
  Quote: “no substantive due process violation is stated if there existed a reasonable suspicion that abuse had occurred or that a threat of abuse was imminent at the time of removal”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/200436/tower-v-leslie-brown/
- **Petition of Kerry D.**, 144 N.H. 146, 737 A.2d 662 (1999)
  Holding: On habeas corpus, the court vacated a consent order that had stripped a non-offending mother of custody, holding that a parent entering a RSA 169-C consent order waives important constitutional rights and that the State must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the parent understood the consequences and consented voluntarily and intelligently; the matter was remanded for an adjudicatory hearing.
  Why here: The case began with a police referral to DCYF by the mother herself, and shows how a law-enforcement-initiated referral can end in the reporting parent losing custody.
  Quote: “the parent in an abuse and neglect proceeding who enters into a consent order is waiving critically important constitutional rights”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8092038/petition-of-kerry-d/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **Watterson v. Page**, 987 F.2d 1 (1st Cir. 1993)
  Holding: In a case arising from a New Hampshire DCYS investigation and ex parte removal, the court affirmed dismissal of the 42 U.S.C. § 1983 claims against a court-appointed psychologist and her supervisor because the complaint stated no violation of any federal constitutional right, and affirmed dismissal of the state law claims as barred by the reporter immunity of RSA 169-C:31.
  Why here: The psychologist was acting under a district court order for counseling and reporting, which the court treated as sufficient to make her a state actor.
  Quote: “the relationship between parent and child may be investigated and terminated by the state provided constitutionally adequate procedures are followed”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/601477/valerie-watterson-v-eileen-page/
- **In re J.H.; In re A.H.**, 188 A.3d 1030 (N.H. 2018)
  Holding: The court held that after the neglect petitions against the father were dismissed the circuit court could maintain the children's out-of-home placement pending a parental fitness hearing at which the State bore the burden of rebutting his presumed fitness by a preponderance of the evidence, and that this process did not impermissibly burden his constitutional right to parent.
  Why here: The circuit court's own order directing a fitness inquiry, rather than a new agency petition, is the mechanism by which a judge reopens the child protection question.
  Quote: “We conclude that this process struck the proper balance between Father’s fundamental right to raise and care for his children and the State’s interest in protecting the children’s welfare”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4505338/in-re-jh-in-re-ah/
- **In re Bill F.**, 145 N.H. 267, 761 A.2d 470 (2000)
  Holding: The court held that a natural or adoptive parent who has not been found to have abused or neglected his child may not be deprived of custody unless, after a full hearing, the State proves by a preponderance of the evidence that the parent abused or neglected the child or is otherwise unfit, and it reversed and remanded for that hearing.
  Why here: The district court itself directed DCYF to perform a home study and investigate the unnamed father, and the opinion fixes the process owed once a judge reopens the custody question that way.
  Quote: “we hold that parents who have not been charged with abuse or neglect be afforded, upon request, a full hearing in the district court regarding their ability to obtain custody”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8092166/in-re-bill-f/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In re Guardianship of Reena D.**, 163 N.H. 107 (2011)
  Holding: The court held that when a parent moves to terminate a guardianship over a minor that the parent originally established by consent, the guardian opposing termination bears the burden of proof by clear and convincing evidence; the trial court's use of a lower standard required vacatur and remand.
  Why here: Parents who voluntarily place a child with a relative through a consensual guardianship retain a constitutionally weighted right to have the child returned.
  Quote: “we now hold that the clear and convincing standard of proof applies to the guardian’s burden of proof in a proceeding to terminate a guardianship established by consent”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8093398/in-re-guardianship-of-reena-d/
- **In re Guardianship of Brittany S.**, 147 N.H. 489, 792 A.2d 384 (2002)
  Holding: Applying the three-part due process balancing test, the court held the State Constitution did not require appointment of counsel for an indigent parent petitioning to terminate a guardianship over her daughter, and that the probate court did not unsustainably exercise its discretion in declining to appoint counsel.
  Why here: A parent who asks the court to undo a guardianship and regain her child proceeds without any constitutional entitlement to appointed counsel.
  Quote: “based upon our analysis of the three-prong balancing test, we hold that the State Constitution does not require that counsel be appointed to represent Tammy G.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8092473/in-re-guardianship-of-brittany-s/
- **Petition of Kerry D.**, 144 N.H. 146, 737 A.2d 662 (1999)
  Holding: On habeas corpus, the court vacated a consent order that had stripped a non-offending mother of custody, holding that a parent entering a RSA 169-C consent order waives important constitutional rights and that the State must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the parent understood the consequences and consented voluntarily and intelligently; the matter was remanded for an adjudicatory hearing.
  Why here: A parent who calls for help can become a respondent, which is precisely the risk the court's informed-consent requirement is designed to check.
  Quote: “the parent in an abuse and neglect proceeding who enters into a consent order is waiving critically important constitutional rights”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8092038/petition-of-kerry-d/

## INP-08 · Educational Disclosure
- **Berry v. Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc.**, 152 N.H. 407, 879 A.2d 1124 (2005)
  Holding: Affirming dismissal, the court held that even assuming congregation elders were obligated to report suspected abuse under RSA 169-C:29, the abused children had no private cause of action for the failure to report and the religious organizations owed them no common law or fiduciary duty of protection.
  Why here: For institutions that supervise children, the case separates the statutory reporting mandate from the special-relationship and premises-based duties that can support tort liability.
  Quote: “the plaintiffs may not bring a private cause of action for the alleged failure of the elders to comply with RSA 169-C:29”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8093047/berry-v-watchtower-bible-tract-society-of-new-york-inc/
- **Schneider v. Plymouth State College**, 144 N.H. 458, 744 A.2d 101 (1999)
  Holding: The court held that a post-secondary institution owes its students a fiduciary duty to provide an environment free of sexual harassment by faculty, affirmed the jury verdict for the student on that claim together with enhanced compensatory damages and prejudgment interest, and remanded the Title IX issues.
  Why here: Where the reporting statute supplies no private remedy, this is the New Hampshire authority imposing an independent institutional duty on a school toward a student sexually abused by staff, and it is the fiduciary standard Berry v. Watchtower applies.
  Quote: “In the context of sexual harassment by faculty members, the relationship between a post-secondary institution and its students is a fiduciary one.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8092087/schneider-v-plymouth-state-college/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Union Leader Corp. v. N.H. Dep't of Safety**, 2024 N.H. 35
  Holding: The court reversed the dismissal of a Right-to-Know suit for State Police records of the response to the Sununu Youth Services Center, clarifying that juvenile confidentiality under RSA 169-B:35 reaches records held by non-court agencies only to the extent disclosure would run counter to the purpose of rehabilitating delinquent minors, and remanded for that determination and for consideration of redaction.
  Why here: Public and press access to records of the State's response at a youth facility is the practical mechanism by which systemic problems in state custody are surfaced.
  Quote: “This refinement effectuates the juvenile delinquency statutes’ purpose of rehabilitating delinquent minors while also ensuring “the greatest possible public access to the actions, discussions and records of all public bodies.””
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10271805/union-leader-corp-v-nh-dept-of-safety/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that a child protection agency's failure to remove a child from an abusive parent, despite repeated reports and its own suspicions, did not deprive the child of liberty without due process, because the Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative duty on the State to protect against private violence absent a custodial relationship.
  Why here: Oversight findings that an agency failed to act do not by themselves generate a federal constitutional claim, which is why administrative and legislative review carries the burden.
  Quote: “nothing in the language of the Due Process Clause itself requires the State to protect the life, liberty, and property of its citizens against invasion by private actors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court held that the 'reasonable efforts' requirement of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act, 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15), does not create a right enforceable by beneficiaries either through 42 U.S.C. § 1983 or by implied private right of action.
  Why here: Federal oversight of state child welfare performance operates through the Secretary's plan-approval and funding levers rather than through private suits.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court held that the Indian Child Welfare Act is within Congress's Article I power and that its requirements do not unconstitutionally commandeer the States, and that the petitioners lacked standing to press the equal protection and nondelegation challenges to the placement preferences.
  Why here: This is the controlling federal authority that ICWA validly governs state child custody proceedings involving Indian children, including its notice, active efforts, heightened proof and qualified expert witness requirements.
  Quote: “But the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners' challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that 'domicile' in ICWA section 1911(a) has a uniform federal meaning under which children take the reservation domicile of their parents, so the tribal court had exclusive jurisdiction over twins born and voluntarily surrendered off the reservation and the state adoption decree had to be vacated.
  Why here: This is the leading decision on when the tribal jurisdiction trigger fires: a state court has no jurisdiction over an Indian child domiciled on a reservation, and parents cannot defeat that jurisdiction by placing the child off-reservation.
  Quote: “In enacting the ICWA Congress confirmed that, in child custody proceedings involving Indian children domiciled on the reservation, tribal jurisdiction was exclusive as to the States.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/601477/valerie-watterson-v-eileen-page/
- **Frazier v. Bailey**, 957 F.2d 920 (1st Cir. 1992)
  Holding: The court held that private individuals under contract with the State to perform child abuse counseling and investigation may assert qualified immunity because they are the functional equivalent of public officials, and that they were entitled to it as a matter of law because the right to family integrity was not sufficiently particularized to put them on notice that their conduct was unlawful.

  Quote: “the right to family integrity has not been so particularized as to put defendants on notice that their conduct was unlawful”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/578605/kevin-frazier-v-edward-n-bailey/
- **Hatch v. Department for Children, Youth & Their Families (State of Rhode Island)**, 274 F.3d 12 (1st Cir. 2001)
  Holding: The court held that the Constitution permits a caseworker to take a child into temporary custody without a prior hearing when the caseworker has a reasonable suspicion that abuse has occurred or that a threat of abuse is imminent, and affirmed the denial of leave to amend because the caseworker had such a suspicion and was entitled to qualified immunity.
  Why here: This is the First Circuit decision that actually establishes the objectively reasonable suspicion threshold for taking a child into emergency protective custody, the standard later applied in Tower v. Leslie-Brown.
  Quote: “Because the welfare of the child is paramount, an objectively reasonable suspicion of abuse justifies protective measures.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/775863/richard-h-hatch-jr-v-department-for-children-youth-and-their-families/

  Quote: “We conclude that due process requires the same standard of proof for hearings of registry appeals when registration would deprive individuals of their protected liberty interests”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8091916/in-re-preisendorfer/
- **In re Tracy M.**, 137 N.H. 119, 624 A.2d 963 (1993)
  Holding: The court held that the preponderance of the evidence standard prescribed by RSA 169-C:13 for abuse and neglect adjudications satisfies due process under Part I, Article 15 of the State Constitution and the Fourteenth Amendment, and affirmed the finding that the petitioner had sexually abused his stepdaughter.
  Why here: The court weighed the risk that a higher burden of proof would cause meritorious reports to be found unfounded, which is the error the screen-out and unfounded decision must guard against.
  Quote: “Based on our analysis above, we find no constitutional error in the legislature's decision.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8091119/in-re-tracy-m/

## Notes
- Court structure: New Hampshire has no intermediate appellate court. Every state case here is a published opinion of the Supreme Court of New Hampshire (court_id nh), retrieved and read in full from CourtListener. The federal circuit for New Hampshire is the First Circuit (ca1). No unpublished or memorandum (3JX) orders were used.
- Critical New Hampshire departure from the federal floor: RSA 170-C:5 grounds for termination of parental rights must be proved BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT (In re Michael E., 162 N.H. 520, 523), which is higher than Santosky's clear-and-convincing constitutional minimum. Santosky is included as the federal floor only; practitioners must apply the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard in New Hampshire. Best interests is a separate, second step after the statutory ground is proved (In re C.M. & a., 166 N.H. 764).
- ICWA / state ICWA (INP-11): targeted CourtListener searches in court=nh for 'Indian Child Welfare Act', tribe, qualified expert witness, active efforts and transfer to tribal court returned ZERO hits. New Hampshire has no retrievable Supreme Court decision on ICWA and no state ICWA analogue surfaced. INP-11 therefore carries only the federal standard: Haaland v. Brackeen (2023) plus Santosky's discussion of ICWA's standard of proof. This node should be revisited if New Hampshire enacts a state ICWA.
- Nodes with no on-point New Hampshire authority, carrying the federal standard by necessity: FAIL-03 (federal compliance failure), PMC-01 (CFSR monitoring) and PMC-02 (OIG/Single Audit) rest on Suter v. Artist M. and DeShaney; PMC-03 (citizen review panels) and PMC-04 (fatality review commissions) have no New Hampshire case law at all and are supported by the federal enforcement-gap cases plus the state transparency and duty authority closest in function. Searches in court=nh for wrongful death of a child in foster care, state agency negligence for failure to protect, and child fatality review returned no on-point child-welfare decision.

- The New Hampshire substantiation and central-registry line is Petition of Bagley (1986) to Richardson v. Chevrefils (1988) to Petition of Preisendorfer (1998): notice and a hearing must precede a registry listing that permanently disqualifies a person from professional work, and the hearing must apply a preponderance standard. Preisendorfer also records that In re Tracy M., 137 N.H. 119 (1993), upheld the preponderance standard for RSA 169-C abuse and neglect adjudications; Tracy M. itself was not separately retrieved in this session and is therefore not listed as a case entry.
- Right to counsel in New Hampshire is node-specific and easy to get wrong: appointed counsel is constitutionally required for an accused STEPPARENT (In re Shelby R.) but there is no per se right for an indigent PARENT in a RSA 169-C proceeding (In re C.M., 163 N.H. 768), and none for a parent petitioning to terminate a GUARDIANSHIP (In re Guardianship of Brittany S.). The legislature abolished and then reinstated the statutory right to counsel for indigent parents in abuse and neglect cases between 2011 and 2013 (Laws 2011, 224:77; Laws 2013, 144:60), as recounted in In re C.M. & a. Counsel issues from the neglect case must be appealed in that case; they are foreclosed on appeal from a later termination (In re C.M. & a.; In re O.D.). Whether ineffective assistance of appointed counsel can be raised at all remains undecided (In re C.M., 2024 N.H. 45).
- Section 1983 note for New Hampshire practice: the controlling First Circuit rule for emergency removal is objectively reasonable suspicion of abuse or of an imminent threat, coupled with a prompt post-deprivation hearing (Tower v. Leslie-Brown, applying Hatch v. Department for Children, Youth & Their Families, 274 F.3d 12 (1st Cir. 2001), which was not separately retrieved here). Watterson v. Page is the New Hampshire-arising First Circuit case and also holds that RSA 169-C:31 immunity bars the pendent state claims against a reporter. Frazier v. Bailey extends qualified immunity to contracted private investigators and counselors.

- Missing (for a later pass): In re Juvenile 2003-195, 150 N.H. 644, 843 A.2d 318 (2004) - the true origin of the 'reasonable efforts given its available staff and financial resources' formulation, confirmed on the face of In re Juvenile 2006-833, which quotes it. In re Michael E., In re C.O. and In re J.B. all trace to it. Load-bearing for ACT-02 and for the PMC-01 agency-capacity argument; the brief carries only intermediate links.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Jonathan T., 148 N.H. 296, 806 A.2d 793 (2002) - the source of the requirement that services be 'accessible, available and appropriate,' expressly cited as such in In re Juvenile 2006-833. This is half of New Hampshire's reasonable-efforts test at ACT-02 and is presently cited only second-hand.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Adam R., 159 N.H. 788, 992 A.2d 697 (2010) - the source of the rule that the child's welfare is 'the dominant consideration in termination proceedings, which must prevail over the interests of the parents.' The brief's own notes rely on it to explain the best-interest standard at OUT-02 but do not carry it as an entry.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Brenda H., 119 N.H. 382, 402 A.2d 169 (1979) - the pre-statutory New Hampshire decision applying a clear-and-convincing standard in abuse and neglect cases, discussed and distinguished in In re Tracy M. A practitioner reading only Tracy M. from this brief will not see why the earlier standard was superseded by the enactment of RSA 169-C:13 rather than overruled on constitutional grounds.
