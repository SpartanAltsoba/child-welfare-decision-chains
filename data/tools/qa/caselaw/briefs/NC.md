# NC — 45 authorities, verified 2026-09-20

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Dobson v. Harris**, 352 N.C. 77, 530 S.E.2d 829 (2000)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held that the statutory presumption of good faith for persons reporting suspected child abuse or neglect shifts to the plaintiff the burden of producing factual (not speculative) evidence of bad faith or malice, and that summary judgment for the reporter is proper absent such a forecast.
  Why here: This is the controlling NC authority on the immunity and good-faith presumption that protect anyone who reports under N.C.G.S. § 7B-301.
  Quote: “Evidence offered to meet or rebut the presumption of good faith must be sufficient by virtue of its reasonableness, not by mere supposition.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1203470/dobson-v-harris/
- **Smith v. Jackson County Board of Education**, 168 N.C. App. 452, 608 S.E.2d 399 (2005)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals affirmed denial of dismissal, holding that a school resource officer's failure to report known child abuse fell outside the conduct the public duty doctrine shields and that the remaining negligence claims came within the special relationship and special duty exceptions.
  Why here: It is the principal NC decision holding that a mandated reporter's failure to report can support tort liability notwithstanding the public duty doctrine.
  Quote: “Hess' failure to report known child abuse was, therefore, outside the scope of conduct generally associated with law enforcement, and the public duty doctrine does not bar this claim.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1253508/smith-v-jackson-county-board-of-education/

- **In re A.P.**, 371 N.C. 14, 812 S.E.2d 840 (2018)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held that the Juvenile Code does not confine the filing of an abuse, neglect, or dependency petition to the director of the county department of social services where the juvenile resides or is found, so a petition filed by another county director does not defeat subject matter jurisdiction.
  Why here: It clarifies which agency may act on a referral and open a juvenile case, a recurring question when courts refer cases across county lines.
  Quote: “the statute does not identify which county director of social services must file the petition”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4497360/in-re-ap/

## INP-07 · Medical Disclosure
- **State v. Towe**, 366 N.C. 56, 732 S.E.2d 564 (2012)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held it was plain error to admit an expert's opinion that the child had in fact been sexually abused where there was no supporting physical evidence, while confirming that a properly qualified expert may testify about the characteristics of sexually abused children and whether the alleged victim exhibits them.
  Why here: Towe governs what a medical or clinical evaluation of a child may and may not be offered to prove once a disclosure is reported.
  Quote: “However, if a proper foundation has been laid, an expert may testify about the characteristics of sexually abused children and whether an alleged victim exhibits such characteristics.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6835782/state-v-towe/
- **Dobson v. Harris**, 352 N.C. 77, 530 S.E.2d 829 (2000)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held that the statutory presumption of good faith for persons reporting suspected child abuse or neglect shifts to the plaintiff the burden of producing factual (not speculative) evidence of bad faith or malice, and that summary judgment for the reporter is proper absent such a forecast.
  Why here: Health-care reporters enjoy the same presumption of good faith and statutory immunity described here.
  Quote: “Evidence offered to meet or rebut the presumption of good faith must be sufficient by virtue of its reasonableness, not by mere supposition.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1203470/dobson-v-harris/
- **State v. Stancil**, 355 N.C. 266, 559 S.E.2d 788 (2002)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held per curiam that it was error to admit a pediatrician's opinion that the child was in fact sexually assaulted where thorough examination and testing revealed no physical evidence of abuse, though on this record the error did not rise to plain error; a properly founded expert may still testify to the profiles of sexually abused children and whether the complainant has consistent characteristics.
  Why here: Stancil is the controlling NC rule limiting what a medical or clinical evaluation of a child may be offered to prove after a disclosure, and it is the authority State v. Towe applies.
  Quote: “absent physical evidence supporting a diagnosis of sexual abuse, such testimony is an impermissible opinion regarding the victim's credibility”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1337407/state-v-stancil/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held that the 'reasonable efforts' requirement of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act creates no federal right enforceable by children or parents through § 1983 or an implied right of action; enforcement lies with the Secretary through the state-plan approval process.
  Why here: Suter explains why federal oversight of state child-welfare performance runs through administrative plan review rather than private litigation.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Hodge ex rel. Hodge v. Jones**, 31 F.3d 157 (4th Cir. 1994)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit held that parents cleared of abuse allegations had no clearly established constitutional right to expungement of the agency's investigative records, reversing the denial of qualified immunity because the familial privacy interest in those records is outweighed by legitimate governmental interests in retaining them.
  Why here: It addresses the legitimate governmental interest in retaining investigative records for later oversight and pattern analysis.
  Quote: “The maxim of familial privacy is neither absolute nor unqualified”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7029683/hodge-ex-rel-hodge-v-jones/
- **News and Observer Publishing Co. v. Poole**, 330 N.C. 465, 412 S.E.2d 7 (1992)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held that SBI investigative reports submitted to a gubernatorial commission became the commission's public records, and that absent a clear statutory exemption all documents meeting the statutory definition of public records must be disclosed for public inspection.
  Why here: Poole is the principal NC authority supporting public access to the investigative records of state oversight bodies.
  Quote: “in the absence of clear statutory exemption or exception, documents falling within the definition of "public records" in the Public Records Law must be made available for public inspection”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1410240/news-and-observer-publishing-co-v-poole/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In re C.P.**, 181 N.C. App. 698, 641 S.E.2d 13 (2007)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals affirmed a neglect adjudication, holding that ICWA did not apply because the mother offered only bare assertions of tribal affiliation and never produced documentation or tribal testimony establishing that the children were Indian children of a federally recognized tribe.
  Why here: C.P. states the NC rule on who bears the burden of establishing ICWA applicability and tribal intervention rights in a juvenile case.
  Quote: “The burden is on the party invoking the Act to show that its provisions are applicable to the case at issue, through documentation or perhaps testimony from a tribe representative.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7474332/in-re-cp/
- **In re E.G.M.**, 230 N.C. App. 196, 750 S.E.2d 857 (2013)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals vacated and remanded a juvenile order because the record lacked findings and conclusions establishing subject matter jurisdiction under ICWA, holding that where ICWA gives greater protection to the Indian family than state law, ICWA controls.
  Why here: E.G.M. is the NC authority requiring express findings on ICWA applicability and jurisdiction before a court custody proceeding may proceed.
  Quote: “where the Act provides a higher standard of protection to the Indian family than is otherwise provided by state law, the ICWA standard prevails”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8913937/in-re-egm/
- **In re E.R.**, 248 N.C. App. 345, 795 S.E.2d 103 (2016)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals reversed a permanency planning order awarding guardianship to non-relatives because the trial court made no findings explaining why placement with the children's paternal grandmother was not in their best interests, as N.C.G.S. § 7B-903(a1) requires, and held that ICWA compliance did not excuse those findings.
  Why here: The Court treated the move from a voluntary kinship placement to guardianship as a foster care placement subject to ICWA.
  Quote: “Such concerns, however, do not obviate the need for findings of fact under N.C. Gen. Stat. § 7B-903(a1) if the court chooses a nonrelative placement for a juvenile.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7477123/in-re-er/

  Holding: The Fourth Circuit upheld an emergency removal statute facially and as applied, holding that a 65-hour delay over a weekend before judicial review did not violate due process where removal was permitted only on imminent danger of death or severe or irremediable injury and judicial review was required within 72 hours.
  Why here: Jordan is the Fourth Circuit's fullest treatment of the substantive and procedural limits on taking a child into emergency protective custody.

- **Weller v. Department of Social Services**, 901 F.2d 387 (4th Cir. 1990)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit held that a father stated a procedural due process claim under § 1983 where he alleged that social services transferred custody of his child without any hearing, because a parent is entitled to a state-initiated hearing before deprivation of custody or, in an emergency, promptly afterward.
  Why here: Weller sets the Fourth Circuit rule governing protective custody taken without prior judicial authorization.
  Quote: “there is no constitutional requirement of a pre-deprivation hearing under emergency circumstances”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8987813/weller-v-department-of-social-services/
- **Martin v. Saint Mary's Department of Social Services**, 346 F.3d 502 (4th Cir. 2003)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for two social workers on a mother's § 1983 familial-integrity claim arising from the overnight removal of her children in another state, holding that the contours of the familial-integrity right were not clearly established in these circumstances and that the record showed neither the alleged misrepresentations nor an intrusion severe enough to violate the Constitution, so qualified immunity applied.
  Why here: Martin is the twenty-first-century Fourth Circuit decision on social workers who arrange a child's removal without fresh judicial authorization, and it frames the state interest against which such protective custody is measured.
  Quote: “A state has a legitimate interest in protecting children from neglect and abuse and in investigating situations that may give rise to such neglect and abuse.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2967846/martin-v-saint-marys-department-of-social-services/

- Missing (for a later pass): Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl, 570 U.S. 637 (2013) — limits ICWA sections 1912(d) and (f) where the parent never had custody; it is the authority most often dispositive in a contested state-court ICWA placement, and is absent from INP-11 and FAIL-05.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re Helms, 127 N.C. App. 505, 491 S.E.2d 672 (1997) — the NC rule that a neglect adjudication requires some physical, mental or emotional impairment, or a substantial risk of it, as a consequence of the parent's conduct; DEC-03 currently rests on Montgomery (1984) without the impairment requirement that NC courts actually apply.
- Missing (for a later pass): Braswell v. Braswell, 330 N.C. 363, 410 S.E.2d 897 (1991) — the NC Supreme Court's adoption of the public duty doctrine and its special-relationship and special-duty exceptions; Smith v. Jackson County merely applies it, and FAIL-04/OUT-06 cite the application without the source.
- Missing (for a later pass): Stone v. N.C. Department of Labor, 347 N.C. 473, 495 S.E.2d 711 (1998) — extends the public duty doctrine to state agencies and departments, which is the threshold defense in any NC suit against DSS for harm following inaction; FAIL-04 and OUT-06 need it alongside Braswell.
- Missing (for a later pass): Renn v. Garrison, 100 F.3d 344 (4th Cir. 1996) — the Fourth Circuit's statement of the state's interest in investigating suspected abuse and of the familial-integrity balance; Martin quotes it for precisely the proposition the brief cites Martin for at INP-12 and DEC-04.
