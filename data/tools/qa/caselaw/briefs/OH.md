# OH — 54 authorities, verified 2026-09-21

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Campbell v. Burton**, 92 Ohio St.3d 336, 2001-Ohio-206 (2001)
  Holding: Because R.C. 2151.421 commands designated professionals to report and R.C. 2151.99 criminally penalizes the failure to do so, the statute 'expressly imposes liability' within R.C. 2744.02(B)(5) and 2744.03(A)(6)(c), stripping a political subdivision and its employees of statutory immunity for a failure to report.
  Why here: This is the Ohio Supreme Court's controlling statement that R.C. 2151.421 creates an enforceable duty on mandated reporters, with civil consequences for the employing subdivision when it is breached.
  Quote: “we hold that within the meaning of R.C. 2744.02(B)(5) and 2744.03(A)(6)(c), R.C. 2151.421 expressly imposes liability for failure to perform the duty to report known or suspected child abuse”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10685790/campbell-v-burton/
- **Yates v. Mansfield Bd. of Edn.**, 102 Ohio St.3d 205, 808 N.E.2d 861 (2004)
  Holding: A board of education may be held liable under former R.C. 2744.02(B)(5) when its failure to report a teacher's sexual abuse of one student in violation of R.C. 2151.421 proximately results in the same teacher's sexual abuse of a different student.
  Why here: Yates states the reporting trigger itself — immediate report on knowledge or suspicion, including threatened as well as completed harm — which is the operative standard at this node.
  Quote: “R.C. 2151.421(A)(1)(a) requires designated persons to "immediately report" their "knowledge or suspicion" that a child "has suffered or faces a threat of suffering" any injury indicative of abuse or neglect”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6893121/yates-v-mansfield-board-of-education/
- **O'Toole v. Denihan**, 118 Ohio St.3d 374, 889 N.E.2d 505 (2008)
  Holding: In a suit over a child's death, the Court held that a public children services agency and its employees owe no R.C. 2151.421(A)(1)(a) duty to cross-report an incoming referral to law enforcement, that R.C. 2919.22 does not expressly impose liability on them, and that employee immunity is lost only for recklessness, which requires a perverse disregard of a known risk rather than mere negligence.
  Why here: O'Toole limits the reporting statute's reach as applied to the agency itself, which is the entity receiving rather than making the report.
  Quote: “a public children services agency and its employees, upon receipt of a case referral, do not have a duty under R.C. 2151.421(A)(1)(a) to cross-report the case to a law-enforcement agency”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6897892/otoole-v-denihan/
- **Gersper v. Ashtabula County Children Services Board**, 59 Ohio St.3d 127, 570 N.E.2d 1120 (1991)
  Holding: Former R.C. 2151.421(G) immunized a children services board and its caseworker from a negligent-investigation suit because, although as recipients of the report they were not 'participating in the making of reports,' they did participate in the judicial proceeding that resulted from the report; the Court distinguished Brodie, where the agency received reports of abuse and failed to act at all.
  Why here: The opinion sets out who must report under R.C. 2151.421(A), the fourth-degree misdemeanor penalty for not reporting under R.C. 2151.99(A), and the agency's 24-hour duty to investigate.
  Quote: “former R.C. 2151.421(G) confers immunity upon those who, as a result of a report of a known or suspected incident of child abuse and/or neglect, participate in a judicial proceeding”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6870979/gersper-v-ashtabula-county-children-services-board/
- **Doe v. Skaggs**, 2018-Ohio-5402, 127 N.E.3d 493 (Ohio Ct. App. 2018)
  Holding: Affirming immunity for a school district and its administrators in a suit arising from a teacher's abuse of students, the court held that the R.C. 2151.421 reporting duty and the R.C. 2151.421(N) damages remedy run against the individual mandated reporters rather than against the political subdivision, and that liability attaches only where the individual had reason to suspect the abuse and failed to report.
  Why here: Skaggs applies the current version of the statute, under which the mandate runs to the individuals listed in R.C. 2151.421(B) and the damages remedy in R.C. 2151.421(N) runs against those individuals.
  Quote: “Civil liability pursuant to R.C. 2151.421 only attaches when the duty to report abuse is violated.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8642194/doe-v-skaggs/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **O'Toole v. Denihan**, 118 Ohio St.3d 374, 889 N.E.2d 505 (2008)
  Holding: In a suit over a child's death, the Court held that a public children services agency and its employees owe no R.C. 2151.421(A)(1)(a) duty to cross-report an incoming referral to law enforcement, that R.C. 2919.22 does not expressly impose liability on them, and that employee immunity is lost only for recklessness, which requires a perverse disregard of a known risk rather than mere negligence.
  Why here: This is the controlling Ohio holding that the receiving agency has no statutory duty to refer the case on to police.
  Quote: “a public children services agency and its employees, upon receipt of a case referral, do not have a duty under R.C. 2151.421(A)(1)(a) to cross-report the case to a law-enforcement agency”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6897892/otoole-v-denihan/
- **Kovacic v. Cuyahoga Cty. Dept. of Children & Family Servs.**, 724 F.3d 687 (6th Cir. 2013)
  Holding: Ohio social workers who entered a home and removed children without a warrant were not entitled to qualified immunity on the children's Fourth Amendment claims because a warrantless removal requires exigency, not merely probable cause, although the workers retained absolute immunity for preparing the temporary emergency care order and initiating the proceedings.
  Why here: It addresses the joint police and caseworker entry that typically accompanies a law-enforcement referral in Ohio.
  Quote: “The relevant question is whether there was exigency, not probable cause: in the absence of exigency, a warrant is required, even "when probable cause is clearly present."”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1035935/kovacic-v-cuyahoga-county-department-of-children-family-services/
- **Andrews v. Hickman County**, 700 F.3d 845 (6th Cir. 2012)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit held that social workers are bound by the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement when entering a home and that no social-worker exception exists, but granted qualified immunity because that rule was not clearly established in the circuit at the time of the entry.
  Why here: It governs the common situation where a caseworker accompanies officers to a home following a law-enforcement referral.
  Quote: “we agree that a social worker, like other state officers, is governed by the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/812950/robert-andrews-v-hickman-county-tennessee/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **State ex rel. Allen Cty. Children Servs. Bd. v. Mercer Cty. Common Pleas Court, Probate Div.**, 150 Ohio St.3d 230, 81 N.E.3d 380 (2016)
  Holding: A probate court may exercise its exclusive original jurisdiction over an adoption petition even while a juvenile court is exercising continuing jurisdiction over the same child, because the juvenile court's continuing jurisdiction does not divest the probate court of adoption jurisdiction.
  Why here: The opinion lays out the statutory sequence and deadlines a juvenile court must follow once a complaint or referral reaches it.
  Quote: “The juvenile court must hold an adjudicatory hearing—generally within 30 days after the complaint was filed—to determine whether the child is abused, neglected, or dependent.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4313877/state-ex-rel-allen-cty-children-servs-bd-v-mercer-cty-common-pleas/
- **In re Baby Girl Baxter**, 17 Ohio St.3d 229, 479 N.E.2d 257 (1985)
  Holding: Adjudicatory and dispositional hearings must be held separately in a dependency case in which parental rights may be terminated, the Rules of Evidence apply strictly at adjudication, and dependency must be proved there by clear and convincing evidence.
  Why here: A court-generated referral still has to be tested in a separate, evidence-bound adjudicatory hearing before any disposition follows.
  Quote: “The issue at the adjudicatory stage of a dependency case is whether petitioner has proven, by clear and convincing evidence, that the child is in fact dependent.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6867809/in-re-baby-girl-baxter/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **Troxel v. Granville**, 530 U.S. 57 (2000)
  Holding: As applied, a statute authorizing any person to petition for visitation over a fit parent's objection violated the mother's due process right to make decisions concerning the care, custody and control of her children.
  Why here: A fit parent's own request for or refusal of services is entitled to the presumption of correctness Troxel describes.
  Quote: “the application of § 26.10.160(3) to Granville and her family violated her due process right to make decisions concerning the care, custody, and control of her daughters”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1087668/troxel-v-granville/
- **Bambach v. Moegle**, 92 F.4th 615 (6th Cir. 2024)
  Holding: Because the father had consented to the initial removal and safety arrangement, no clearly established law put the caseworker on notice that her conduct violated the family's Fourth or Fourteenth Amendment rights, and qualified immunity was granted.
  Why here: It shows how a parent's request for help or agreement to a plan is later treated as consent that forfeits procedural protections.
  Quote: “Absent certain exigent circumstances, the state's termination of or interference with parental rights—even temporarily—requires some measure of procedural protection, like proper notice and an opportunity for a hearing.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9473552/mark-bambach-v-gina-moegle/
- **Schulkers v. Kammer**, 955 F.3d 520 (6th Cir. 2020)
  Holding: The Fourth Amendment governs a social worker's in-school interview of a child during a child-abuse investigation, requiring at a minimum reasonable suspicion of abuse, and the court affirmed denial of qualified immunity on the children's Fourth Amendment claims and on the parents' Fourteenth Amendment claims arising from a coercive in-home safety plan.
  Why here: It shows that a parent's apparent cooperation with a caseworker's plan is not voluntary consent when it is obtained under threat of removal.
  Quote: “a social worker must have a reasonable suspicion of child abuse before conducting an in-school interview when no other exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement applies”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4740438/holly-schulkers-v-elizabeth-kammer/
- **Smith v. Williams-Ash**, 520 F.3d 596 (6th Cir. 2008)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for an Ohio caseworker, holding that where parents voluntarily consent to a safety plan placing their children outside the home and never use the plan's stated mechanism to revoke that consent, no pre-removal hearing is required and there is no procedural due process violation.
  Why here: It is the counterweight to Schulkers: a parent's agreement to a plan is treated as continuing consent until the parent affirmatively revokes it, and displeasure or repeated questions do not amount to revocation.
  Quote: “the Smiths needed to explicitly withdraw the consent they explicitly gave, thus requiring Children's Services to either return the children or file a formal complaint against them”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1201271/smith-v-williams-ash/

## INP-07 · Medical Disclosure

  Holding: A child victim's statements made for purposes of medical diagnosis or treatment are admissible under Evid.R. 803(4) regardless of whether the child has been found competent to testify, and their admission did not violate the Confrontation Clause.
  Why here: It defines the evidentiary weight that a child's disclosure during a medical encounter will later carry, which is why the healthcare setting matters so much at intake.
  Quote: “the child's statements may be admitted at trial as an exception to the hearsay rule pursuant to Evid.R. 803(4) if they were made for purposes of medical diagnosis or treatment”

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **State ex rel. Cincinnati Enquirer v. Jones-Kelley**, 118 Ohio St.3d 81 (2008)
  Holding: The Court granted mandamus compelling the Department of Job and Family Services to release a requested database, holding the custodian failed to carry its burden of showing the records fell squarely within a statutory exception and that a later amendment excluding foster-caregiver information did not apply retroactively.
  Why here: It is the decision that pried open the state child-welfare database for outside scrutiny, the classic oversight trigger.
  Quote: “A custodian does not meet this burden if it has not proven that the requested records fall squarely within the exception.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6897825/state-ex-rel-cincinnati-enquirer-v-jones-kelley/
- **State ex rel. Cincinnati Enquirer v. Bloom**, 177 Ohio St.3d 174, 251 N.E.3d 79 (2024)
  Holding: Interpreting the Ohio Constitution's open courts provision independently, the Court held that it creates a presumption of public access to juvenile delinquency proceedings which a statute cannot override, so a juvenile proceeding may not be closed without an individualized determination balancing the competing interests.
  Why here: It is the constitutional basis for outside observation of juvenile court practice in Ohio.
  Quote: “the open courts provision provides a presumption of public access to juvenile delinquency proceedings”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10680589/state-ex-rel-cincinnati-enquirer-v-bloom/
- **State ex rel. Scripps Howard Broadcasting Co. v. Cuyahoga Cty. Court of Common Pleas, Juv. Div.**, 73 Ohio St.3d 19, 652 N.E.2d 179 (1995)
  Holding: The Court granted mandamus compelling a juvenile judge to release the transcript of a contempt proceeding held in his juvenile case, holding that the right of public access extends to transcripts as well as live proceedings and that Juv.R. 37(B) could not be applied to withhold the transcript without the findings required by In re T.R. that access could harm the child and that the harm outweighs the benefits of access.
  Why here: Outside scrutiny of juvenile court practice depends on access not only to hearings but to the transcripts that document them, which is what this case secured.
  Quote: “The right of access includes both the live proceedings and the transcripts which document those proceedings.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6879392/state-ex-rel-scripps-howard-broadcasting-co-v-cuyahoga-county-court-of/
- **State ex rel. Plain Dealer Publishing Co. v. Geauga Cty. Court of Common Pleas, Juv. Div.**, 90 Ohio St.3d 79, 734 N.E.2d 1214 (2000)
  Holding: The Court granted prohibition against a juvenile judge's closure order, holding that although there is no qualified constitutional right of access to juvenile delinquency proceedings and such proceedings are neither presumptively open nor presumptively closed, the person seeking closure bears the burden of proving the T.R./Lias factors, and closing proceedings on unsupported findings is an abuse of discretion.
  Why here: It states the pre-Bloom rule that juvenile proceedings are neither presumptively open nor presumptively closed, the baseline for outside observation of Ohio juvenile courts.
  Quote: “the person seeking closure of a juvenile delinquency proceeding bears the burden of proving the existence of the factors specified in T.R. and Lias”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6887821/state-ex-rel-plain-dealer-publishing-co-v-geauga-county-court-of-common/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In re C.J.**, 2018-Ohio-931, 108 N.E.3d 677 (Ohio Ct. App. 2018)
  Holding: The juvenile court erred as a matter of law by transferring jurisdiction over an Indian child to the tribal court despite a parent's objection and without addressing 25 U.S.C. 1911(b) good cause, and it must hold an evidentiary hearing on whether good cause exists to deviate from the 25 U.S.C. 1915(b) placement preferences and on the child's best interests.
  Why here: This is the leading published Ohio appellate decision on ICWA notice, tribal intervention, the parental veto over transfer, and good cause under 25 U.S.C. 1911(b).
  Quote: “The juvenile court erred as a matter of law in disregarding the parental veto, disregarding the good cause provision of 25 U.S.C. 1911(b), and transferring jurisdiction to GRIC's tribal court.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4476860/in-re-cj/
- **Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl**, 570 U.S. 637 (2013)
  Holding: ICWA's heightened termination standard in 25 U.S.C. 1912(f) does not apply where the parent never had custody of the Indian child, and 1912(d)'s remedial-efforts requirement applies only where terminating the parent's rights would precipitate the breakup of an existing Indian family.
  Why here: It is the controlling federal construction of ICWA's active-efforts and heightened-termination provisions that an Ohio court must apply once a child is identified as an Indian child.
  Quote: “we hold that §1912(d) applies only in cases where an Indian family's "breakup" would be precipitated by the termination of the parent's rights”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/931616/adoptive-couple-v-baby-girl/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **In re Murray**, 52 Ohio St.3d 155, 556 N.E.2d 1169 (1990)
  Holding: An adjudication of dependency followed by an award of temporary custody to a children services board is a final, appealable order, and the Court grounded that conclusion in the fundamental liberty interest and paramount right of suitable parents in the custody of their children.
  Why here: It supplies the Ohio Supreme Court's statement of the constitutional interest that emergency protective custody intrudes upon.
  Quote: “Parents have a "fundamental liberty-interest" in the care, custody, and management of the child.”

- Added State ex rel. Plain Dealer Publishing Co. v. Geauga Cty. Court of Common Pleas, Juv. Div. (cluster 6887821) for INP-09, PMC-03, PMC-06 and FAIL-06. CORRECTION TO THE VERIFIER'S DESCRIPTION: 90 Ohio St.3d 79 is the proceedings-access case, not a records case — it holds there is no qualified constitutional right of access to juvenile delinquency proceedings, that such proceedings are neither presumptively open nor presumptively closed, and that the party seeking closure bears the burden of proving the T.R./Lias factors. Read with Scripps Howard it supplies the framework that In re T.R. built and that Bloom (2024) displaced for delinquency proceedings by locating a constitutional presumption of access in the Ohio open courts provision.
- Added Smith v. Williams-Ash (cluster 1201271) for INP-05, DEC-06 and ACT-01: the Ohio-origin circuit decision holding that a genuinely voluntary safety plan defeats a procedural due process claim where the parent never uses the plan's stated mechanism to revoke consent. It is the controlling counterweight to Schulkers and the case Bambach relies on, and its absence made those three nodes read one-sidedly in the family's favor.

- Added Kovacic v. Cuyahoga Cty. Dept. of Children & Family Servs., 606 F.3d 301 (cluster 147186) for INP-12 and DEC-04. CORRECTION TO THE VERIFIER'S DESCRIPTION: the first Kovacic appeal did not fix the absolute-immunity scope; it holds (1) that the mother's claims were barred by Ohio's two-year limitations period and (2) that the children's claims are not barred by Rooker-Feldman because the source of their injury is the social workers' conduct rather than the juvenile court's temporary-custody judgment. That jurisdictional holding is the predicate for the 2013 merits decision on exigency, and the two-year clock is a practical trap for families.
- Added Gersper v. Ashtabula County Children Services Board (cluster 6870979) for INP-02, INP-01, INP-06, INP-07, DEC-02 and FAIL-04. INP-02 was the only node in the reconciled brief carrying fewer than two cases (it had Yates alone). Gersper is the Ohio Supreme Court's decision on the R.C. 2151.421 reporter-immunity provision, which by its terms protects 'anyone or any hospital, institution, school, health department, or agency participating in the making of reports' — the statutory immunity that CAPTA conditions funding on and that makes a voluntary third-party report workable. It also preserves, through its discussion of Brodie v. Summit Cty. Children Services Bd. (1990), the rule that immunity does not shield an agency that receives reports and fails to act.
- Added Doe v. Skaggs (cluster 8642194; published at 127 N.E.3d 493) for INP-01, INP-08 and FAIL-04: a published Ohio appellate decision applying the CURRENT text of R.C. 2151.421, under which the mandate runs to the individuals listed in R.C. 2151.421(B) and the damages remedy in R.C. 2151.421(N) runs against those individuals rather than against the political subdivision. It is used here because it is the most recent published Ohio authority applying the reporting statute to school personnel and because it directly bears on the Campbell immunity caveat above. It is an intermediate appellate decision and yields to the Supreme Court of Ohio cases at the same nodes.
- STATUTORY RENUMBERING WARNING FOR R.C. 2151.421: Yates (2004) refers to R.C. 2151.421(B) as the provision permitting (but not requiring) other persons to report, while Doe v. Skaggs (2018) quotes R.C. 2151.421(B) as the list of professionals to whom the mandate applies, and quotes the civil-damages provision as R.C. 2151.421(N). The subdivisions have been relettered since Yates and Gersper (which construed former division (G), the immunity provision). Counsel must check the current lettering against the present statute before citing any subdivision of R.C. 2151.421.
