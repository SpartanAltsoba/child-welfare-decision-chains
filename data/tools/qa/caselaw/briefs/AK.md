# AK — 45 authorities, verified 2026-09-20

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Calabretta v. Floyd**, 189 F.3d 808 (9th Cir. 1999)
  Holding: A social worker and police officer were denied qualified immunity for coercing entry into a home and strip-searching a child to investigate an anonymous abuse tip, because a child-abuse investigation is not an exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant, consent, or exigency requirements.
  Why here: Holds that a report of suspected abuse, standing alone, does not authorize a coerced entry to verify it.
  Quote: “A social worker is not entitled to sacrifice a family's privacy and dignity to her own personal views on how parents ought to discipline their children.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7078750/calabretta-v-floyd/
- **Rogers v. County of San Joaquin**, 487 F.3d 1288 (9th Cir. 2007)
  Holding: A social worker who removed children without a warrant was denied qualified immunity because no exigency existed; the agency's own decision to treat the report as a non-emergency ten-day response, and its ability to obtain a warrant within hours, defeated any claim of imminent danger.
  Why here: Shows that how the agency itself triages an incoming report is evidence of whether the reported conditions posed imminent danger.
  Quote: “Here, the concerned officials classified the case as a ten-day response, indicating that they did not think that any exigency existed.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3048851/rogers-v-county-of-san-joaquin/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **Amy S. v. State, Department of Health & Social Services, Office of Children's Services**, 440 P.3d 273 (Alaska 2019)
  Holding: Assuming without deciding that the superior court violated due process by taking judicial notice of contested facts, pleadings, and testimony from the parents' domestic-relations file when adjudicating the child in need of aid, any error was harmless, and the adjudication was affirmed.
  Why here: Addresses AS 47.10.113, which folds custody questions into the CINA case, and limits how the custody file may be used.
  Quote: “taking judicial notice of the existence of ten years of custody disputes is different than taking judicial notice of specific facts, pleadings, and testimony from the custody case”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6658699/amy-s-v-state-department-of-health-social-services-office-of-children-s/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **Jacob v. State, Department of Health & Social Services, Office of Children's Services**, 177 P.3d 1181 (Alaska 2008)
  Holding: Grandparents were entitled to a declaratory judgment that OCS violated their statutory rights to notice of and participation in their grandchildren's CINA proceedings, but their remaining claims — including the request for systemic injunctive relief — became moot once they intervened.
  Why here: Establishes that relatives who affirmatively seek placement and information have enforceable statutory rights against OCS.
  Quote: “We hold that all other issues are moot due to the Jacobs' subsequent intervention in their grandchildren's CINA proceedings.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2561874/jacob-v-state-department-of-health-social-services-office-of-children-s/
- **Office of Public Advocacy v. Superior Court, Third Judicial District**, 462 P.3d 1000 (Alaska 2020)
  Holding: A putative father's parentage may be established in a CINA proceeding on legally sufficient non-genetic evidence, so CINA Rule 2(k)'s definition of "parent" — and the party status and appointed counsel that follow — reaches a person the superior court determines to be a parent without scientific testing.
  Why here: Governs a man who comes forward on his own to claim a child in an open CINA case and seeks recognition as a parent.
  Quote: “We conclude that a judicial determination of paternity does not necessarily need underlying scientific, genetic testing in this context”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/11102035/office-of-public-advocacy-v-superior-court-third-judicial-district/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: "Domicile" in ICWA is a uniform question of federal law, and children domiciled on a reservation fall within exclusive tribal jurisdiction that parents cannot defeat by giving birth or placing the child off-reservation.
  Why here: Establishes that ICWA's jurisdictional terms are federal and uniform, the premise Alaska courts build on when allocating authority between the superior court and Alaska Native tribal courts.
  Quote: “In enacting the ICWA Congress confirmed that, in child custody proceedings involving Indian children domiciled on the reservation, tribal jurisdiction was exclusive as to the States.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **State v. Native Village of Tanana**, 249 P.3d 734 (Alaska 2011)
  Holding: Federally recognized Alaska Native tribes may exercise inherent sovereign jurisdiction to initiate ICWA child custody proceedings without a § 1918 reassumption petition, and judgments from those proceedings may be entitled to full faith and credit by the State.
  Why here: Is the controlling Alaska authority on when a tribal forum, rather than the superior court, may open a child-protection case.
  Quote: “federally recognized Alaska Native tribes are not necessarily precluded from exercising inherent sovereign jurisdiction to initiate "child custody proceedings" as ICWA defines that term”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2359562/state-v-native-village-of-tanana/
- **Simmonds v. Parks**, 329 P.3d 995 (Alaska 2014)
  Holding: Adopting the federal tribal-exhaustion doctrine, the court held that a father who failed to appeal a Minto Tribal Court termination of his parental rights through tribal appellate remedies could not collaterally attack that judgment in state court, and his suit was dismissed.
  Why here: Establishes that once a tribal court properly exercises jurisdiction, tribal remedies must be exhausted before any state-court challenge.
  Quote: “We therefore conclude his suit in Alaska state court must be dismissed.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2685783/simmonds-v-parks/
- **Native Village of Tununak v. State, Department of Health & Social Services, Office of Children's Services**, 303 P.3d 431 (Alaska 2013)
  Holding: Overruling its prior application of Adoption Rule 11's preponderance standard, the court held that good cause to deviate from ICWA's adoptive placement preferences must be proved by clear and convincing evidence, and vacated and remanded for application of that standard.
  Why here: Demonstrates that ICWA supplies a federal standard of proof that displaces a conflicting Alaska court rule.
  Quote: “we conclude that ICWA implicitly mandates that good cause to deviate from ICWA's adoptive placement preferences be proved by clear and convincing evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/904273/native-village-of-tununak-v-state-department-of-health-social-services/
- **John v. Baker**, 982 P.2d 738 (Alaska 1999)
  Holding: Alaska Native tribes possess inherent, non-territorial sovereign authority to adjudicate child custody disputes between their own members in tribal court, so the superior court erred in refusing to give the Northway Tribal Court judgment effect; the case was remanded to decide whether that judgment should be recognized under comity.
  Why here: Is the foundational Alaska holding that tribal adjudicatory authority over children does not depend on Indian country, the premise on which Tanana and every later Alaska tribal-jurisdiction case rests.
  Quote: “we conclude that Native tribes do possess the inherent sovereign power to adjudicate child custody disputes between tribal members in their own courts”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1253018/john-v-baker/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court rejected every constitutional challenge to ICWA: Congress had Article I power to enact it, its placement and remedial-effort commands do not unconstitutionally commandeer state legislatures, courts or executive officers, and the equal-protection and nondelegation claims failed for want of standing.
  Why here: Settles that ICWA validly binds Alaska's superior courts and OCS, removing the constitutional cloud the Fifth Circuit had cast over 25 U.S.C. section 1912(d).
  Quote: “the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners’ challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9402136/haaland-v-brackeen/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **Rogers v. County of San Joaquin**, 487 F.3d 1288 (9th Cir. 2007)
  Holding: A social worker who removed children without a warrant was denied qualified immunity because no exigency existed; the agency's own decision to treat the report as a non-emergency ten-day response, and its ability to obtain a warrant within hours, defeated any claim of imminent danger.
  Why here: Holds that the availability of a warrant within hours defeats a claim of protective-custody exigency.
  Quote: “Here, the concerned officials classified the case as a ten-day response, indicating that they did not think that any exigency existed.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3048851/rogers-v-county-of-san-joaquin/
- **State, Department of Health & Social Services, Office of Children's Services v. Michelle P.**, 411 P.3d 576 (Alaska 2018)
  Holding: CINA jurisdiction derives from the child's status as a child in need of aid and is distinct from a custody or supervision grant, so dismissal of the petition was reversed; separately, the removal order was vacated because it lacked sufficient evidentiary support and did not satisfy ICWA.
  Why here: Vacates a removal order for evidentiary insufficiency, the direct check on protective-custody decisions.
  Quote: “This was error because the removal order was not supported by sufficient evidence and did not comply with the requirements of the Indian Child Welfare Act (ICWA).”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4466631/state-dept-of-health-social-services-office-of-children-s-services-v/
- **Stanley v. Illinois**, 405 U.S. 645 (1972)
  Holding: A State may not remove children from a parent's custody without a hearing on that parent's fitness; denying such a hearing to unwed fathers while granting it to all other parents violates due process and equal protection.
  Why here: Fixes the constitutional rule that protective custody must be followed by a prompt adversarial hearing on parental fitness, which Alaska implements as the AS 47.10.142(d) temporary-custody hearing.
  Quote: “We have concluded that all Illinois parents are constitutionally entitled to a hearing on their fitness before their children are removed from their custody.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/108497/stanley-v-illinois/
- **Wallis ex rel. Wallis v. Spencer**, 202 F.3d 1126 (9th Cir. 2000)
  Holding: Summary judgment for the city was reversed: a jury could find the officers lacked reasonable cause to seize the children without a court order, and, absent a risk that physical evidence would dissipate or an urgent medical problem requiring immediate attention, the state must notify parents and obtain judicial approval before subjecting a seized child to an investigatory physical examination.
  Why here: Holds that a warrantless protective seizure requires specific, articulable evidence of imminent danger and that its scope must be strictly circumscribed by the exigency that justified it.
  Quote: “the state is required to notify parents and to obtain judicial approval before children are subjected to investigatory physical examinations”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6985031/wallis-ex-rel-wallis-v-spencer/
- **State, Department of Family & Community Services, Office of Children's Services v. Karlie T.**, 538 P.3d 723 (Alaska 2023)
  Holding: The superior court's dismissal of an emergency CINA petition was reversed in part: the inquiry at that stage is only whether probable cause exists to believe the child is in need of aid, and the domestic-violence evidence sufficed under AS 47.10.011(6) and (8)(B)(i), though (8)(B)(ii) requires that the child actually have been exposed to domestic violence.
  Why here: Is Alaska's controlling recent decision on the AS 47.10.142 emergency-custody pathway and the showing OCS must make to keep a newborn in temporary custody.
  Quote: “we reiterate that the superior court was tasked with making a probable cause determination, not a finding whether the child actually was in need of aid”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9899873/state-of-alaska-department-of-family-community-services-office-of/

## PMC-03 · Citizen Review Panels
- **Jacob v. State, Department of Health & Social Services, Office of Children's Services**, 177 P.3d 1181 (Alaska 2008)
  Holding: Grandparents were entitled to a declaratory judgment that OCS violated their statutory rights to notice of and participation in their grandchildren's CINA proceedings, but their remaining claims — including the request for systemic injunctive relief — became moot once they intervened.
  Why here: Shows why an independent review mechanism matters: mootness barred the plaintiffs from obtaining systemic relief.
  Quote: “We hold that all other issues are moot due to the Jacobs' subsequent intervention in their grandchildren's CINA proceedings.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2561874/jacob-v-state-department-of-health-social-services-office-of-children-s/
- **State, Department of Family & Community Services, Office of Children's Services v. Z.C.**, Nos. S-18249/18259, Op. No. 7760 (Alaska Mar. 28, 2025)
  Holding: In a class action on behalf of foster children whose Social Security benefits OCS applied to reimburse itself, the court held the due-process claim was not federally preempted and that OCS must give the notice ordered by the superior court if it continues its self-reimbursing representative-payee practice.
  Why here: Demonstrates that in Alaska, class litigation rather than a citizen panel produced systemic corrective relief.
  Quote: “We therefore hold that OCS must provide the notice ordered by the superior court if it wishes to continue implementing its self-reimbursing rep payee scheme.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10635336/state-of-alaska-department-of-health-and-social-services-adam-crum-in-his/
- **State, Department of Health & Social Services, Office of Children's Services v. Zander B.**, 474 P.3d 1153 (Alaska 2020)
  Holding: The superior court acted within its discretion in permitting foster parents to intervene for the limited purpose of challenging OCS's decision to move the child, and its stay of that placement transfer was affirmed.
  Why here: Shows that in Alaska, caregiver intervention in court — not a citizen panel — is the practical check on placement-change decisions.
  Quote: “We hold that the court did not abuse its discretion by permitting intervention under the circumstances of this case.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10635408/state-of-alaska-dhss-ocs-v-zander-b-kelly-b-foster-parents/
- **State, Department of Health & Social Services, Division of Family & Youth Services v. Native Village of Curyung**, 151 P.3d 388 (Alaska 2006)
  Holding: Alaska Native villages may sue the Division's director in his official capacity under section 1983 as parens patriae on behalf of their member children to enforce rights created by the Adoption Assistance Act and ICWA — including the right to a case plan — but may not use section 1983 to enforce rights they hold as sovereigns and may not sue the State directly.
  Why here: Demonstrates that in Alaska the independent-review function is performed by tribes suing as parens patriae, not by a citizen review panel.
  Quote: “the villages may bring suit as parens patriae under § 1983 to enforce rights that are created by the Adoption Assistance Act and the Indian Child Welfare Act”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2638298/state-department-of-health-social-services-division-of-family-youth/

## PMC-04 · Fatality Review Commission
- **State, Department of Health & Social Services v. Mullins**, 328 P.3d 1038 (Alaska 2014)
  Holding: A jury verdict holding OCS responsible for 95% of the damages suffered by two sisters abused by grandparents while in OCS legal custody was set aside as irrational, and OCS was granted a new trial on allocation of fault.
  Why here: Shows that tort litigation, not a fatality-review process, has been the main public airing of OCS supervision failures in Alaska.
  Quote: “Because the evidence supporting the jury's allocation of fault was so insubstantial as to make the verdict plainly unreasonable, we conclude that OCS is entitled to a new trial.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2680821/state-department-of-health-social-services-v-mullins/
- **Reasner v. State, Department of Health & Social Services, Office of Children's Services**, 394 P.3d 610 (Alaska 2017)
  Holding: Summary judgment against a former foster child who sued OCS over years of sexual abuse was vacated because a genuine factual dispute existed about when she discovered OCS's possible role, though her claim did not qualify for the extended limitations period of AS 09.10.065(a).
  Why here: Illustrates why serious-injury cases surface long after the fact, a gap fatality-review systems are meant to close.
  Quote: “We conclude that a genuine factual dispute exists concerning when Reasner discovered information suggesting that OCS had played a role in allowing her to be abused.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4393222/reasner-v-state-department-of-health-social-services-office-of-children-s/
- **Blake J. v. State, Department of Health & Social Services, Office of Children's Services**, 554 P.3d 430 (Alaska 2024)
  Holding: A former foster child's suit over abuse by his adoptive mother was time-barred; neither OCS custody until age 19, collateral estoppel from an earlier guardianship competency ruling, nor equitable tolling saved the claim.
  Why here: Demonstrates that abuse in state-approved homes may never be adjudicated on the merits, leaving fatality and serious-injury review as the only accounting.
  Quote: “collateral estoppel is not available here because the competency standard in a conservatorship and partial guardianship proceeding is different from the standard of competence necessary to file suit”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10057929/blake-j-v-state-of-alaska-department-of-health-social-services-office-of/

## PMC-05 · FOIA / Transparency
- **Basey v. State, Department of Public Safety, Division of Alaska State Troopers**, 408 P.3d 1173 (Alaska 2017)
  Holding: The State failed to establish either the AS 40.25.122 litigation exception or the law-enforcement exception to the Alaska Public Records Act, so dismissal of the records requester's suit was error and the case was remanded.
  Why here: Is the leading recent Alaska authority construing the Public Records Act's exceptions narrowly in favor of disclosure.
  Quote: “We conclude it was error for the superior court to grant the State's motion to dismiss pursuant to AS 40.25.122.”

- **Rowan B. v. State, Department of Health & Social Services, Office of Children's Services**, 320 P.3d 1152 (Alaska 2014)
  Holding: Civil discovery rules, not the Public Records Act exemptions, govern a CINA parent's request for law-enforcement records, and the trial court erred by denying the request outright instead of conducting an in camera review.
  Why here: Distinguishes public-records requests from party discovery, a distinction any transparency effort in Alaska must respect.
  Quote: “We hold that the trial court erred in denying Rowan access to materials he sought through discovery without at least conducting an in camera review.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2657600/rowan-b-v-state-dept-of-health-social-services-office-of-children-s/
- **Simone H. v. State, Department of Health & Social Services, Office of Children's Services**, 320 P.3d 284 (Alaska 2014)
  Holding: The trial court acted within its discretion in refusing to release the child's therapy records to the mother for use at the termination trial, and substantial evidence supported the finding that OCS made reasonable efforts to reunify the family.
  Why here: Shows that child treatment records are a recognized confidentiality boundary any transparency effort must respect.
  Quote: “We conclude that the trial court acted within its discretion in denying Simone's request for access to Irving's therapy records”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2655918/simone-h-v-state-department-of-health-social-services-office-of-children-s/

There is no intermediate appellate court for CINA, termination, adoption or guardianship cases — every child-welfare appeal goes directly to the Supreme Court of Alaska. Accordingly every state case in this brief is from the Alaska Supreme Court (court_id 'alaska'), and there are no published intermediate appellate decisions to cite on any of these 42 nodes.
