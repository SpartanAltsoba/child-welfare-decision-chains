## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Thomas v. Sumner**, 2015 WY 7, 341 P.3d 390
  Holding: The Court held that Wyo. Stat. Ann. § 14-3-205(a) obliges any person with reasonable cause to suspect abuse to report immediately, and that a reporter who meets that threshold acts in good faith and is immune under § 14-3-209, as are those who later participate in the investigation.
  Why here: Wyoming's reporting duty falls on 'any person,' so every mandated-reporter category at this node is governed by the § 14-3-205(a) reasonable-suspicion threshold this case construes.
  Quote: “the plain language of § 14-3-205(a) does not require that the reporting party know that a child has been abused, or even reasonably believe that the child was abused”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2768609/thomas-v-sumner/
- **Elmore v. Van Horn**, 844 P.2d 1078 (Wyo. 1992)
  Holding: The Court held Wyoming's child-abuse reporting statute confers qualified, not absolute, immunity: a reporter who reports in good faith is immune, good faith is presumed, and mere negligence in reporting does not defeat the presumption.
  Why here: This is the leading Wyoming statement of the reporter-immunity rule that governs who may be held liable for a child-abuse report at this decision point, and of the presumption of good faith a plaintiff must overcome.
  Quote: “the Wyoming legislature intended to provide qualified immunity, that is immunity for all who report child abuse in good faith”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1249422/elmore-v-van-horn/
- **May v. Southeast Wyoming Mental Health Center**, 866 P.2d 732 (Wyo. 1993)
  Holding: The Court affirmed summary judgment for the reporting therapist and the investigating public employees, holding that § 14-3-209 immunity is not confined to the act of reporting but covers participation in the investigation, and that the Governmental Claims Act separately immunized the state actors.
  Why here: Establishes that the statutory immunity reaches everything a mandated reporter does under the Act, not just the initial call.
  Quote: “Section 14-3-209 does not limit immunity to suits based only on the reporting”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2609811/may-v-southeast-wyoming-mental-health-center/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **In the Interest of RTB**, 2024 WY 75, 552 P.3d 365
  Holding: The Court affirmed the neglect adjudication, holding the State must prove neglect by a preponderance of the evidence, and cautioned that an officer taking warrantless protective custody under Wyo. Stat. Ann. § 14-3-405(a)(i) must actually find the child seriously endangered rather than apply a blanket positive-drug-test policy.
  Why here: The removal here was made by a Rawlins police officer, and the opinion states the reasonable-grounds test governing law-enforcement protective custody and referral.
  Quote: “During the adjudicatory hearing, the State had the burden to prove its allegations of neglect by a preponderance of the evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10005456/in-the-interest-of-rtb-minor-child-blb-v-the-state-of-wyoming/
- **In re "H" Children**, 2003 WY 155, 79 P.3d 997
  Holding: Over a two-justice dissent, the Court affirmed a juvenile-court adjudication of neglect and the ensuing placement order, holding that neglect need be shown only by a preponderance of the evidence and that the record contained sufficient evidence to sustain the finding under the statutory definition of a neglected child.
  Why here: The case began when Cheyenne police officers responding to a report of a possible suicidal subject encountered the children, and the county attorney filed the neglect petition two days later.
  Quote: “Child neglect, on the other hand, need only be shown by a preponderance of the evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2520914/in-re-h-children/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **JET v. State, Department of Family Services**, 2010 WY 137, 241 P.3d 489
  Holding: The Court affirmed the refusal to let a young mother withdraw her admission of neglect, holding the juvenile court gave the statutorily required advisements at the initial hearing and did not abuse its discretion despite her claim of mental illness and her own earlier requests for help.
  Why here: The mother had sought help voluntarily from public-health and counseling services before the petition was filed, and the Court declined to treat that as a reason to undo the adjudication.
  Quote: “The juvenile court did not abuse its discretion in denying the appellant's motion to withdraw her admission of neglect”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5326182/jet-v-state-department-of-family-services/
- **Troxel v. Granville**, 530 U.S. 57 (2000)
  Holding: The plurality held Washington's breathtakingly broad grandparent visitation statute unconstitutional as applied, because it gave no special weight to a fit parent's decision, and reaffirmed the presumption that fit parents act in their children's best interests.
  Why here: When a parent asks for help rather than being accused, the constitutional presumption that the parent is acting in the child's interests still governs state responses.
  Quote: “there is a presumption that fit parents act in the best interests of their children”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1087668/troxel-v-granville/
- **Ailport v. Ailport**, 2022 WY 43
  Holding: Applying Troxel, the Court held Wyoming's grandparent visitation statute must be read to require grandparents to prove by clear and convincing evidence that the parents are unfit or that their visitation decisions are or will be harmful before any best-interests inquiry, and affirmed denial of the grandparents' petition.
  Why here: Confirms that a fit parent's own decisions about a child's care and contacts are entitled to constitutional deference when third parties seek state intervention.
  Quote: “Parents have a fundamental due process right to guide the upbringing of their children, including determining the level of contact with their grandparents”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9998686/jill-rebecca-ailport-and-shane-arthur-ailport-v-travis-ailport-shelley/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court held the 'reasonable efforts' clause of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act does not unambiguously confer an enforceable federal right on the Act's beneficiaries and Congress created no private remedy, leaving enforcement to the Secretary's funding mechanisms and state judicial findings.
  Why here: Federal oversight of state performance runs through administrative review and funding conditions rather than private suit.
  Quote: “we think that Congress did not intend to create a private remedy for enforcement of the “reasonable efforts” clause”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Freudenthal v. Cheyenne Newspapers, Inc.**, 2010 WY 80, 233 P.3d 933
  Holding: The Court affirmed the order compelling release of the budget-reduction plans the Department of Family Services and the Department of Health had submitted to the Governor, holding the plans were factual rather than deliberative, and declined to decide whether the Wyoming Public Records Act incorporates a deliberative process privilege at all.
  Why here: Public disclosure of agency records is how outside oversight bodies obtain the material that triggers systemic review.
  Quote: “The plans contain factual information, rather than opinions, deliberations or thought processes and are not, therefore, the sort of documents protected by the deliberative process privilege.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2584390/freudenthal-v-cheyenne-newspapers-inc/
- **Circuit Court of the Eighth Judicial District v. Lee Newspapers**, 2014 WY 101, 332 P.3d 523
  Holding: The Court held that court proceedings and judicial documents in child sexual-assault cases must generally remain open, with redaction and initials used to satisfy the victim-confidentiality statute, and that closure requires an on-the-record finding of a compelling interest, narrowly tailored, after a hearing open to the public.
  Why here: Public access to the court record is the mechanism by which outside observers detect systemic failures in child cases.
  Quote: “the circuit court must find that there is a compelling interest in closing the court proceedings and sealing the case file which is essential to preserve higher values”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2717387/circuit-court-of-the-eighth-judicial-district-v-lee-newspapers/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In the Matter of the Guardianship of LNP**, 2013 WY 20, 294 P.3d 904
  Holding: In Wyoming's principal Indian Child Welfare Act decision, the Court affirmed a permanent guardianship of an Indian child, holding that a communication giving the court reason to know the child was Indian triggered ICWA § 1912(a) notice, that a qualified expert witness under § 1912(e) need not have expertise in tribal customs, and that clear and convincing evidence supported the serious-damage finding.
  Why here: This is the Wyoming Supreme Court's controlling application of ICWA notice, qualified-expert and evidentiary requirements once a child is identified as an Indian child.
  Quote: “neither the ICWA, nor the relevant BIA Guidelines, require a “qualified expert witness” to possess expertise in tribal customs”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/822659/in-the-matter-of-the-guardianship-of-lnp-a-minor-child-kc-v-cc-and-ec/
- **Yellowbear v. State**, 2008 WY 4, 174 P.3d 1270
  Holding: In a felony-murder prosecution arising from the death of a child, the Court held that the location of the offense had been removed from the diminished Wind River Indian Reservation and was no longer Indian country, so Wyoming courts had jurisdiction.
  Why here: Wyoming's jurisdictional line between state authority and Indian country turns on the reservation-boundary analysis this case performs.
  Quote: “Therefore, the State of Wyoming had jurisdiction to pursue the criminal charge”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2519206/yellowbear-v-state/
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that 'domicile' under the Indian Child Welfare Act is a matter of uniform federal law, that children born off-reservation take the reservation domicile of their mother, and that the tribal court therefore had exclusive jurisdiction under 25 U.S.C. § 1911(a).
  Why here: Determines when a Wyoming state court must yield exclusive jurisdiction to a tribal court over an Indian child's custody proceeding.
  Quote: “In enacting the ICWA Congress confirmed that, in child custody proceedings involving Indian children domiciled on the reservation, tribal jurisdiction was exclusive as to the States”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **Northern Arapaho Tribe v. State (In re SNK)**, 2003 WY 141, 78 P.3d 1032
  Holding: Over a dissent, the Court held the Northern Arapaho Tribe's appeal from an order continuing an enrolled Indian child's placement in a non-Indian foster home was moot because the child had since been placed elsewhere, and it expressly declined to decide whether the 'existing Indian family' exception to the Indian Child Welfare Act applies in Wyoming.
  Why here: This is Wyoming's only other Indian Child Welfare Act appearance, and it establishes that whether the 'existing Indian family' exception applies in Wyoming is an important but expressly undecided question that counsel must brief from federal and out-of-state authority.
  Quote: “we conclude that the issue in this case regarding the applicability of ICWA and the "existing Indian family" exception to ICWA is moot”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2549394/in-re-snk/
- **In the Matter of the Adoption of MAJB**, 2020 WY 157, 478 P.3d 196
  Holding: The Court held the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction to approve a Hague Convention adoption and was statutorily authorized to enter a decree permitting a Wyoming birth certificate with an accurate date of birth, and reversed the dismissal.
  Why here: Shows how federal and international adoption law overlays Wyoming juvenile jurisdiction in cases with a foreign or federal dimension.
  Quote: “The district court has subject matter jurisdiction to approve the Hague Convention adoption”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10012064/in-the-matter-of-the-adoption-of-majb-minor-child-dlb-and-dab-v/

## PMC-04 · Fatality Review Commission
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)

- Practice points a lawyer should know. (1) Wyoming's reporting duty in Wyo. Stat. Ann. section 14-3-205(a) runs to 'any person' - there is no separate mandated-reporter class - and section 14-3-209 immunity is qualified, presumed, and extends beyond the report to participation in the investigation (Thomas v. Sumner; Elmore v. Van Horn; May). (2) The adjudication burden is a preponderance under section 14-3-425(a), while termination requires clear and convincing evidence under section 14-2-309(a) - do not conflate them. (3) A jury may be demanded at the adjudicatory hearing but only in writing within ten days of the advisement (In the Interest of NP). (4) There is a statutory advisement-and-appointment-of-counsel right at the shelter-care hearing under section 14-3-409(b) (FH v. State), but the Court has held section 14-2-318(a) creates neither a mandatory right to counsel nor a right to effective assistance in termination cases (Roberts), and Lassiter/BSC govern the constitutional claim - this is the single most consequential gap for parents in Wyoming.
- In the Interest of RTB, 2024 WY 75, is the most useful recent authority for challenging a removal: the Court expressly said it 'would be concerning' if a police department took protective custody based solely on a parent's positive drug test without the section 14-3-405(a)(i) serious-endangerment analysis, and accepted that a nexus must exist between the risk factor and the neglect alleged, while still affirming on the fuller record. The Court also noted in footnote 3 that its precedent does not clearly set the burden of proof at a shelter-care hearing - an open question.
