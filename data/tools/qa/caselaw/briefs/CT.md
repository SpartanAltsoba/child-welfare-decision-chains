# CT — 46 authorities, verified 2026-09-20

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Ward v. Greene**, 267 Conn. 539, 839 A.2d 1259 (2004)
  Holding: The court held that § 17a-101 protects only the child who is or should have been the subject of a mandated report, so no duty of care was owed to a different child later killed by the same caregiver.
  Why here: This is the controlling Connecticut decision on the scope of the mandated reporting statute and who may sue on it.
  Quote: “the defendant did not owe a duty of care to McBride because she was not within the class of persons protected by § 17a-101”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7900096/ward-v-greene/
- **Morales v. Kagel**, 58 Conn. App. 776, 755 A.2d 915 (2000)
  Holding: The Appellate Court affirmed summary judgment for a psychologist sued by a suspected abuser, holding she was covered by the good faith reporter immunity of § 17a-101 and owed him no duty.
  Why here: This is a published Connecticut application of statutory reporter immunity to a mandated reporter sued by the accused.
  Quote: “The affidavit raises no issue of fact that would defeat the defendant's qualification for the immunity privilege of a psychologist acting in good faith pursuant to § 17a-101 (h).”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7910346/morales-v-kagel/
- **Kruger v. Grauer**, 173 Conn. App. 539, 164 A.3d 764 (2017)
  Holding: The Appellate Court affirmed the denial of summary judgment, holding that § 17a-101e (b) confers only qualified, good faith immunity on those who report suspected child abuse and thereby abrogates any common-law absolute immunity or litigation privilege for such reports.
  Why here: This is the current and correct statement of Connecticut reporter immunity: a mandated reporter is protected only if the report was made in good faith, not absolutely.
  Quote: “It is impossible to give effect both to the qualified immunity provided by § 17a-101e (b) and any purported absolute immunity that may have existed at common law.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4395638/kruger-v-grauer/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Doe v. Whelan**, 732 F.3d 151 (2d Cir. 2013)
  Holding: The Second Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Connecticut DCF workers, holding they were entitled to qualified immunity because an objectively reasonable basis existed to believe the children faced an imminent threat when they were removed without parental consent or a court order.
  Why here: The removal followed a documented history of domestic violence reported to and known by the authorities.
  Quote: “In sum, notwithstanding the evidence plaintiffs claim the District Court overlooked or misconstrued, we readily conclude that the defendants are entitled to qualified immunity here.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1085609/doe-v-whelan/
- **Tenenbaum v. Williams**, 193 F.3d 581 (2d Cir. 1999)
  Holding: The Second Circuit held that removing a child on an emergency basis without judicial authorization is unconstitutional when there is reasonable time to obtain a court order safely, and that a state-initiated medical examination of the child implicates Fourth Amendment and bodily integrity interests, although the defendants had qualified immunity because the right was not then clearly established.
  Why here: It governs the removal of a child from school by caseworkers acting with law enforcement before any court order.
  Quote: “it is unconstitutional for state officials to effect a child's removal on an "emergency" basis where there is reasonable time safely to obtain judicial authorization consistent with the child's safety”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/766396/tenenbaum-v-williams/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **In re Tayler F.**, 296 Conn. 524, 995 A.2d 611 (2010)
  Holding: In a neglect proceeding the court held that a child may be found unavailable, allowing admission of the child's out-of-court statements under the residual hearsay exception, upon case-specific evidence that testifying would cause the child psychological harm.
  Why here: The investigation here followed court-ordered psychological evaluation and interviews of the children, the evidentiary product this node generates.
  Quote: “we conclude that a trial court properly may conclude that a child is unavailable if there is competent evidence that the child will suffer psychological harm from testifying”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2077082/in-re-tayler-f/
- **Zamstein v. Marvasti**, 240 Conn. 549, 692 A.2d 781 (1997)
  Holding: The court held that a mental health professional engaged to evaluate whether children were sexually abused owes no duty of care to the parent suspected of the abuse, because imposing one would deter such evaluations.
  Why here: The evaluation here was undertaken in the course of a dissolution action, the classic court-ordered referral posture.
  Quote: “we conclude that the sounder judicial ruling is to hold that no such duty exists”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7896983/zamstein-v-marvasti/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In re Zakai F.**, 336 Conn. 272 (2020)
  Holding: The court held that a parent who shows the factors that caused her removal as guardian have been satisfactorily resolved is entitled to a presumption that reinstatement of guardianship is in the child's best interests, which the opposing party must rebut by clear and convincing evidence.
  Why here: This is the Connecticut framework when a parent herself comes back to court asking for restoration of her rights over the child.
  Quote: “We also conclude that the party opposing reinstatement must rebut this presumption by clear and convincing evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4863335/in-re-zakai-f/
- **Eason v. Welfare Commissioner**, 171 Conn. 630, 370 A.2d 1082 (1976)
  Holding: The court construed the commitment-revocation statute to be triggered only by application of the persons or agencies it specifies, so others could not invoke the Juvenile Court's power to revoke a commitment.
  Why here: It identifies who may lawfully ask the juvenile court to undo a commitment, the statutory gateway for a guardian's request.
  Quote: “Under § 17-62 (f), one of those specified persons or agencies must apply to trigger the Juvenile Court's power to revoke the commitment to the welfare commissioner.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2267223/eason-v-welfare-commissioner/

## INP-08 · Educational Disclosure
- **Frank v. Department of Children & Families**, 134 Conn. App. 288, 37 A.3d 834 (2012)
  Holding: The Appellate Court held that the statutory definition of a neglected child in § 46b-120(3) was unconstitutionally vague as applied to the plaintiff educator's conduct, invalidating the substantiation based on it.
  Why here: The report arose from a school employee's classroom conduct, and the case marks the constitutional limit on substantiating educators.
  Quote: “Accordingly, we conclude that, under these facts, § 46b-120 (3) is unconstitutionally vague as to the plaintiff's conduct.”

  Holding: The Appellate Court affirmed dismissal of the administrative appeal, holding substantial evidence supported the hearing officer's substantiation of sexual abuse and the placement of the plaintiff's name on the central registry.

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Pamela B. v. Ment**, 244 Conn. 296, 709 A.2d 1089 (1998)
  Holding: The court held that parents stated a justiciable claim for declaratory and injunctive relief against the systemic failure to provide timely evidentiary hearings on orders of temporary custody, and that the trial court improperly struck their request for relief.
  Why here: This is Connecticut's leading systemic challenge to CPS practice, based on docket review evidence that ten-day hearings were routinely opened and continued for months.
  Quote: “It is precisely because state intervention can cause irrevocable consequences for both children and parents that "fundamentally fair" procedures provided in a timely manner must be afforded the parties.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7897514/pamela-b-v-ment/
- **Juan F. ex rel. Lynch v. Weicker**, 37 F.3d 874 (2d Cir. 1994)
  Holding: The Second Circuit affirmed the district court's order fixing specific hiring numbers, a graduated staffing timetable and a date for reimbursing foster parents at 100 percent of USDA rates under the Juan F. consent decree governing Connecticut DCF, holding this was enforcement rather than modification of the decree and that legislative budget cuts and claimed substantial compliance did not excuse the state.
  Why here: It is the appellate decision in the court-monitored institutional oversight of Connecticut DCF, the mechanism by which systemic findings about the agency are made and enforced.
  Quote: “"Substantial compliance" has no bearing, however, on the district court's power to interpret the decree and, when necessary, to make its terms more precise and realistic.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7030412/juan-f-ex-rel-lynch-v-weicker/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that domicile under the Indian Child Welfare Act is a matter of uniform federal law and that twins born off the reservation to reservation-domiciled parents were themselves domiciled on the reservation, so the tribal court had exclusive jurisdiction and the state-court adoption decree had to be vacated.
  Why here: This is the controlling authority on the tribal jurisdiction trigger: where an Indian child is domiciled on a reservation, the state court has no jurisdiction at all, whatever the parents intended.
  Quote: “In enacting the ICWA Congress confirmed that, in child custody proceedings involving Indian children domiciled on the reservation, tribal jurisdiction was exclusive as to the States.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **In re Juvenile Appeal (83-CD)**, 189 Conn. 276, 455 A.2d 1313 (1983)
  Holding: Reversing an order of temporary custody, the court held that Connecticut's summary removal scheme is constitutional only because it confines coercive intervention to serious physical illness, serious physical injury or immediate physical danger and requires that measures short of removal be used where possible.
  Why here: This is the governing Connecticut authority on when the state may summarily assume protective custody: only on probable cause of serious physical illness, injury or immediate physical danger.
  Quote: “This phrase requires that various steps short of removal from the home be used when possible in preference to disturbing the integrity of the family.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7891701/in-re-juvenile-appeal-83-cd/
- **Teresa T. v. Ragaglia**, 272 Conn. 734, 865 A.2d 428 (2005)

  Why here: This is the Connecticut Supreme Court's definitive construction of the ninety-six hour emergency hold: probable cause of imminent physical harm authorizes, but never compels, protective custody.
  Quote: “§ 17a-101g (c) does not require the commissioner to remove the child upon a finding of probable cause, but merely authorizes the commissioner to seek removal under such circumstances.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7900548/teresa-t-v-ragaglia/

  Why here: It fixes the due process consequences of a ninety-six hour emergency hold: the parent must get a real, timely hearing on continued custody.
  Quote: “It is precisely because state intervention can cause irrevocable consequences for both children and parents that "fundamentally fair" procedures provided in a timely manner must be afforded the parties.”

  Why here: This is the controlling circuit rule on when protective custody without prior judicial authorization is constitutional.
  Quote: “it is unconstitutional for state officials to effect a child's removal on an "emergency" basis where there is reasonable time safely to obtain judicial authorization consistent with the child's safety”

## DEC-01 · Screen-Out / Unfounded at Intake

- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative constitutional duty on a State to protect a child from private violence of which its caseworkers were aware, absent a custodial relationship the State itself created, and affirmed judgment for the county after the child was beaten into permanent brain damage.
  Why here: It is the federal rule that a decision to screen out or close a report, however tragic the sequel, does not by itself create constitutional liability.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-cty-dss/

## OUT-03 · Guardianship / Kinship Permanency
- **Fish v. Fish**, 285 Conn. 24, 939 A.2d 1040 (2008)
  Holding: The court held that a third party seeking custody over a fit parent's objection must plead and prove, by a fair preponderance, that continued parental custody would be clearly damaging, injurious or harmful to the child, and that such awards are justified only in exceptional circumstances.
  Why here: It is the leading Connecticut statement of the harm standard and burden of proof for third-party guardianship and custody permanency.
  Quote: “the petitioner must allege and prove, at the very least, that continued parental custody will be clearly damaging, injurious or harmful to the child”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1970926/fish-v-fish/
- **Roth v. Weston**, 259 Conn. 202, 789 A.2d 431 (2002)
  Holding: The court held § 46b-59 unconstitutional as applied where visitation was ordered over a fit parent's objection without any allegation and proof by clear and convincing evidence of a parent-like relationship and of actual, significant harm to the children if visitation were denied, and it reversed with direction to dismiss the petition.
  Why here: Together with Fish v. Fish it is the controlling Connecticut harm standard and burden of proof constraining any third-party or kinship permanency arrangement.
  Quote: “The petition must also contain specific, good faith allegations that denial of the visitation will cause real and significant harm to the child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7899239/roth-v-weston/
- **In re Zakai F.**, 336 Conn. 272 (2020)
  Holding: The court held that a parent who shows the factors that caused her removal as guardian have been satisfactorily resolved is entitled to a presumption that reinstatement of guardianship is in the child's best interests, which the opposing party must rebut by clear and convincing evidence.
  Why here: It defines the standard for undoing a guardianship permanency arrangement under § 45a-611.
  Quote: “We also conclude that the party opposing reinstatement must rebut this presumption by clear and convincing evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4863335/in-re-zakai-f/
- **In re Santiago G.**, 325 Conn. 221, 157 A.3d 60 (2017)
  Holding: The court dismissed the appeal, holding that a relative seeking the child had no colorable claim to intervene as of right in the termination proceeding, so denial of her motion was not an appealable final judgment.
  Why here: A relative hoping for guardianship cannot obtain party status as of right in the termination proceeding that precedes permanency.
  Quote: “Thus, we conclude that Maria G. has failed to plead a colorable claim to intervene as of right.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4380801/in-re-santiago-g/
- **In re Jewelyette M.**, 351 Conn. 511 (2025)
  Holding: The court held that § 46b-129 (p), which gives foster parents a right to be heard and comment, does not prohibit a trial court from permitting foster parents to intervene in neglect proceedings, rejecting the contrary rule of In re Ryan C., and reversed.
  Why here: Foster and preadoptive caregivers may be permitted to intervene and press a competing permanency position, including on revocation of commitment.
  Quote: “We conclude that the statute does not prohibit a trial court from permitting foster parents to intervene in such proceedings.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10375073/in-re-jewelyette-m/
- **Troxel v. Granville**, 530 U.S. 57 (2000)
  Holding: A four-Justice plurality held Washington's third-party visitation statute unconstitutional as applied because the trial court gave no special weight to a fit mother's own determination of her daughters' best interests, there being a presumption that fit parents act in their children's best interests.
  Why here: Any third-party guardianship or kinship permanency arrangement must give special weight to the fit parent's own determination before it can be imposed.

## OUT-06 · Child Fatality / Serious Injury
- **Ward v. Greene**, 267 Conn. 539, 839 A.2d 1259 (2004)
  Holding: The court held that § 17a-101 protects only the child who is or should have been the subject of a mandated report, so no duty of care was owed to a different child later killed by the same caregiver.
  Why here: The child's death is the paradigm fatality claim, and this case is the Connecticut authority denying recovery to a non-report child.
  Quote: “the defendant did not owe a duty of care to McBride because she was not within the class of persons protected by § 17a-101”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7900096/ward-v-greene/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative constitutional duty on a State to protect a child from private violence of which its caseworkers were aware, absent a custodial relationship the State itself created, and affirmed judgment for the county after the child was beaten into permanent brain damage.
  Why here: It governs the § 1983 claim families bring after a child fatality or serious injury and explains why such claims almost always fail absent state custody.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-cty-dss/
- **Cornejo v. Bell**, 592 F.3d 121 (2d Cir. 2010)
  Holding: The Second Circuit held that agency lawyers who initiate and prosecute child protective proceedings have absolute immunity but that caseworkers do not, being entitled only to qualified immunity for their investigative functions, and affirmed on immunity grounds.
  Why here: The suit followed a child's death after repeated reports, and the court resolved it on immunity rather than the merits.
  Quote: “the district court was incorrect in its conclusion that the caseworker defendants were also entitled to absolute immunity”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1356186/cornejo-v-bell/
- **Teresa T. v. Ragaglia**, 272 Conn. 734, 865 A.2d 428 (2005)

  Why here: It is the controlling Connecticut authority on whether the removal statute supplies a private-law duty when a child is seriously injured after DCF knew of the risk.
  Quote: “§ 17a-101g (c) does not require the commissioner to remove the child upon a finding of probable cause, but merely authorizes the commissioner to seek removal under such circumstances.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7900548/teresa-t-v-ragaglia/

  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative constitutional duty on a State to protect a child from private violence of which its caseworkers were aware, absent a custodial relationship the State itself created, and affirmed judgment for the county after the child was beaten into permanent brain damage.
  Why here: It marks the line between what a fatality review can document as a systemic failure and what is actionable as a constitutional violation.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-cty-dss/

## PMC-05 · FOIA / Transparency
- **Department of Public Safety v. Freedom of Information Commission**, 298 Conn. 703, 6 A.3d 763 (2010)
  Holding: The court held that registry information restricted from disclosure by § 54-255 may not be released under the Freedom of Information Act except for law enforcement purposes, and reversed the dismissal of the department's appeal from the commission's disclosure order.
  Why here: This is the leading recent Connecticut Supreme Court decision on the limits of FOIA access to protected state registry records.
  Quote: “In sum, we conclude that the trial court improperly dismissed the department's appeal from the commission's order.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7902166/department-of-public-safety-v-freedom-of-information-commission/
- **Rocque v. Freedom of Information Commission**, 255 Conn. 651, 774 A.2d 957 (2001)
  Holding: The court held that most of an agency's internal harassment investigation records must be disclosed because they are of legitimate public concern, and that only the sexually explicit or descriptive portions are exempt as an invasion of personal privacy.
  Why here: This is the Connecticut Supreme Court's controlling formulation of the personal-privacy exemption to disclosure.
  Quote: “we conclude that the only portions of the records that are not a matter of legitimate public concern are those portions containing sexually explicit or descriptive information”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7898692/rocque-v-freedom-of-information-commission/

## PMC-06 · Project Milk Carton AI Analysis
- **Pamela B. v. Ment**, 244 Conn. 296, 709 A.2d 1089 (1998)
  Holding: The court held that parents stated a justiciable claim for declaratory and injunctive relief against the systemic failure to provide timely evidentiary hearings on orders of temporary custody, and that the trial court improperly struck their request for relief.
  Why here: It supplies the standard against which cross-system analysis of Connecticut's decision chain should be measured: fundamentally fair procedures, delivered timely.
  Quote: “It is precisely because state intervention can cause irrevocable consequences for both children and parents that "fundamentally fair" procedures provided in a timely manner must be afforded the parties.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7897514/pamela-b-v-ment/
- **Santosky v. Kramer**, 455 U.S. 745 (1982)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held that the Due Process Clause requires a State to support its allegations by at least clear and convincing evidence before terminating parental rights, and left the precise burden at or above that level to state law.
  Why here: It supplies the constitutional benchmark against which any cross-state analysis of Connecticut's termination chain should be scored.
  Quote: “We hold that such a standard adequately conveys to the factfinder the level of subjective certainty about his factual conclusions necessary to satisfy due process.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/110676/santosky-v-kramer/
- **Juan F. ex rel. Lynch v. Weicker**, 37 F.3d 874 (2d Cir. 1994)
  Holding: The Second Circuit affirmed the district court's order fixing specific hiring numbers, a graduated staffing timetable and a date for reimbursing foster parents at 100 percent of USDA rates under the Juan F. consent decree governing Connecticut DCF, holding this was enforcement rather than modification of the decree and that legislative budget cuts and claimed substantial compliance did not excuse the state.
  Why here: It supplies the enforceable performance benchmarks against which any cross-system analysis of Connecticut's child welfare chain should be scored.
  Quote: “"Substantial compliance" has no bearing, however, on the district court's power to interpret the decree and, when necessary, to make its terms more precise and realistic.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7030412/juan-f-ex-rel-lynch-v-weicker/
