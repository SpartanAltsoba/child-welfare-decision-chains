# SC — 50 authorities, verified 2026-09-21

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Doe v. Marion**, 373 S.C. 390, 645 S.E.2d 245 (2007)
  Holding: The court affirmed dismissal, holding South Carolina's child abuse reporting statute creates a duty owed to the public and no private cause of action for failing to report, and that no common law duty to warn future victims existed.
  Why here: This is the controlling South Carolina authority that a mandated reporter's failure to report gives the victim no civil damages claim, however serious the consequences.
  Quote: “The fact that such language is missing from § 20-7-510 indicates the legislative intent was for the reporting statute not to create civil liability.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1269614/doe-v-marion/
- **Whitner v. State**, 328 S.C. 1, 492 S.E.2d 777 (1997)
  Holding: The court held that 'child' in South Carolina's child abuse and endangerment statute includes a viable fetus, so a mother's prenatal drug use can constitute criminal child neglect, and found no due process violation.
  Why here: Whitner is why prenatal substance exposure reported by a hospital is treated as a report of child abuse in South Carolina.
  Quote: “We hold the word "child" as used in that statute includes viable fetuses.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1388496/whitner-v-state/
- **Beattie v. Aiken County Department of Social Services**, 319 S.C. 449, 462 S.E.2d 276 (1995)
  Holding: The court held that parents whose case was closed as unfounded may not examine the DSS case file, that 'report' covers all investigative information and the reporter's identity is never disclosable, and that FOIA requires separation and release of non-exempt material where a court is asked to conduct that review.
  Why here: Beattie is the reason a mandated reporter's identity cannot be obtained by the family, which is the practical backbone of reporter protection in South Carolina.
  Quote: “The clear language of § 20-7-650 prohibits the disclosure of the DSS case file to Appellants or to anyone else.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1354043/beattie-v-aiken-county-department-of-social-services/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **South Carolina Department of Social Services v. Randy S.**, 390 S.C. 100, 700 S.E.2d 250 (Ct. App. 2010)
  Holding: The court held that where DSS in substance removed children and placed them with a relative, it had brought a removal action and was required to follow the removal procedures — petition, 72-hour probable cause hearing, 35-day merits hearing — rather than proceeding as an intervention.
  Why here: Randy S. sets out the statutory sequence triggered when law enforcement takes emergency protective custody and notifies DSS.
  Quote: “Despite DSS's contention that this was only an intervention action, we find the evidence supports a finding that Children were removed from Father's custody.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2394957/south-carolina-department-of-social-services-v-randy-s/
- **Hooper v. Rockwell**, 334 S.C. 281, 513 S.E.2d 358 (1999)
  Holding: The South Carolina Supreme Court upheld the emergency protective custody statute against state and federal constitutional challenge, held that orders issued after a merits hearing are final and must be timely appealed, and affirmed termination of parental rights and the adoption.
  Why here: Hooper is the controlling authority validating a law enforcement officer's power to take a child into emergency protective custody without prior judicial review.
  Quote: “We further conclude that S.C.Code Ann. § 20-7-610(A) (Supp.1997) does not violate the state or federal constitutions.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1316607/hooper-v-rockwell/
- **South Carolina Department of Social Services v. Gamble**, 337 S.C. 428, 523 S.E.2d 477 (Ct. App. 1999)
  Holding: The court reversed dismissal of a removal case, holding the statute requires that the merits hearing be scheduled to be held — not necessarily completed — within thirty-five days of the removal petition, and rejecting the parent's constitutional challenge.
  Why here: It illustrates the caseworker testimony needed at the probable cause hearing to sustain an emergency removal.
  Quote: “The statute requires the family court to schedule a hearing on the merits to be held within thirty-five days of the removal petition.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8293719/south-carolina-department-of-social-services-v-gamble/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **South Carolina Department of Social Services v. Hogan**, 410 S.C. 120, 763 S.E.2d 219 (Ct. App. 2014)
  Holding: The court reversed and remanded for a merits hearing, holding the family court had jurisdiction to order reunification and a merits hearing where no affirmative finding of abuse or neglect had ever been made, and condemning the practice of holding such findings in abeyance while children remain in foster care.
  Why here: Hogan shows the family court's own authority to order a merits hearing on a parent's request rather than deferring to DSS's litigation choices.
  Quote: “We find this practice is in clear contravention of the removal statutes and could violate the fundamental right parents have to raise their children.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8295505/south-carolina-department-of-social-services-v-hogan/
- **South Carolina Department of Social Services v. Mother Ex Rel. Minor Child**, 375 S.C. 276, 651 S.E.2d 622 (Ct. App. 2007)
  Holding: The court affirmed termination, holding among other things that DSS complied with the family court's order to provide the mother services tailored to her disabilities by making the referrals it did.
  Why here: The service obligations here originated in a court order entered during the father's TPR proceeding, showing how judicial referrals shape the agency's duties.
  Quote: “Even if Mother properly preserved this issue, we find DSS properly provided specialized services to Mother.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1387533/south-carolina-department-of-social-services-v-mother-ex-rel-minor-child/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **South Carolina Department of Social Services v. Meek**, 352 S.C. 523, 575 S.E.2d 846 (Ct. App. 2002)
  Holding: The court reversed the portion of an order continuing DSS custody based on the mother's conduct, holding she was entitled to notice that DSS would seek custody at the father's merits hearing, while holding that omission of the statutory boldface notices from the removal petition did not divest the family court of subject matter jurisdiction.
  Why here: Meek turns on a Voluntary Placement Agreement and the process DSS owed the mother when she invoked it to request her children's return.
  Quote: “Mother's fundamental right to due process entitled her to notice that DSS intended to petition the family court for custody of the children at Father's hearing.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1252424/south-carolina-department-of-social-services-v-meek/
- **Kaci May v. Dorchester School District Two**, Op. No. 6053 (S.C. Ct. App. filed May 29, 2024) (App. Case No. 2020-001352)
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of an injunction, holding S.C. Code § 63-7-920(C) authorizes DSS to interview children at school without a warrant and outside the parents' presence during a mandated investigation, and that the interviews here were reasonable in inception and scope under the Fourth Amendment.
  Why here: The investigation was triggered by the mother's own request for help, and the court holds she could not then dictate the terms of the resulting inquiry.
  Quote: “we find § 63-7-920(C) expressly authorizes DSS to interview children at school without a warrant when conducting an investigation mandated by § 63-7-920(A)(1)”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9975225/kaci-may-v-dorchester-school-district-two/
- **South Carolina Department of Social Services v. Mother**, 396 S.C. 390, 720 S.E.2d 920 (Ct. App. 2011)
  Holding: The court reversed the portion of the family court's order directing DSS to forego reunification efforts and file a TPR petition, finding the preponderance of the evidence supported returning the twins home with twelve months of DSS supervision and services.
  Why here: The decision shows a parent who engaged with services successfully enough that continued in-home supervision, not removal, was the appropriate response.
  Quote: “we find the family court erred in ordering SCDSS to initiate TPR proceedings against Mother”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2506771/south-carolina-department-of-social-services-v-mother/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court held that the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's reasonable-efforts provision, 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15), creates no right enforceable by beneficiaries under the Act itself or under 42 U.S.C. § 1983.
  Why here: Suter explains why federal oversight of state child welfare runs through HHS plan approval and funding rather than private enforcement suits.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative duty on a child protection agency to protect a child from violence by a private actor, so the agency's failure to remove the child was not a constitutional violation.
  Why here: DeShaney explains why oversight findings that an agency failed to act rarely translate into constitutional liability, pushing accountability into administrative and legislative channels.
  Quote: “nothing in the language of the Due Process Clause itself requires the State to protect the life, liberty, and property of its citizens against invasion by private actors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/
- **White Ex Rel. White v. Chambliss**, 112 F.3d 731 (4th Cir. 1997)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit held South Carolina DSS officials were entitled to qualified immunity on claims arising from the removal of children and the foster placement in which one child died, because no clearly established federal right — including any right created by the South Carolina Child Protection Act — was violated.
  Why here: White shows that audit-style findings of agency error do not convert into federal liability absent a clearly established right.
  Quote: “The facts presented by White reveal that the DSS defendants violated no "clearly established" law in removing White's children from her custody.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2966644/white-ex-rel-white-v-chambliss/
- **Disabato v. South Carolina Ass'n of School Administrators**, 404 S.C. 433, 746 S.E.2d 329 (2013)
  Holding: The court held that applying the South Carolina FOIA to a nonprofit corporation supported in part by public funds does not violate its First Amendment speech and association rights, and reversed dismissal of the FOIA action.
  Why here: Disabato extends FOIA transparency to privately organized entities that receive or spend public funds, a common posture for child welfare contractors.
  Quote: “We conclude that while the FOIA does impact upon SCASA's speech and association rights, the First Amendment is not violated.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8295135/disabato-v-south-carolina-assn-of-school-administrators/
- **Jensen ex rel. Estate of Clark v. Anderson County Department of Social Services**, 304 S.C. 195, 403 S.E.2d 615 (1991)
  Holding: The court affirmed the Court of Appeals, adopting the six-element special duty exception to the public duty rule and holding that the Child Protection Act imposes on the local child protection agency and its social workers a special duty to investigate and intervene whose breach may give rise to a private cause of action, with immunity turning on remand on whether the duty breached was ministerial or discretionary.
  Why here: Jensen is the one South Carolina decision in which a finding that the agency failed to investigate properly produced legal liability rather than only administrative or oversight consequences.
  Quote: “the Act imposes a special duty on the local child protection agency and its social workers to investigate and intervene in cases where child abuse has been reported”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1294742/jensen-ex-rel-estate-of-clark-v-anderson-county-department-of-social/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl**, 570 U.S. 637 (2013)
  Holding: Reversing the South Carolina Supreme Court, the Court held that ICWA § 1912(f) does not apply where the parent never had custody, that § 1912(d)'s active-efforts requirement applies only where termination would precipitate the breakup of an Indian family, and that § 1915(a) preferences do not bar a non-Indian adoption when no preferred party has come forward.
  Why here: This is the controlling ICWA authority for South Carolina courts — it arose from a South Carolina adoption and narrows when active efforts, the heightened §1912(f) showing, and §1915(a) placement preferences are triggered at intake.
  Quote: “Consistent with the statutory text, we hold that §1912(d) applies only in cases where an Indian family's "breakup" would be precipitated by the termination of the parent's rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/931616/adoptive-couple-v-baby-girl/
- **Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl**, 398 S.C. 625, 731 S.E.2d 550 (2012)
  Holding: The South Carolina Supreme Court affirmed the family court's denial of the adoption and transfer of custody to the Cherokee biological father, holding ICWA §§ 1912(d) and 1912(f) barred termination — a holding REVERSED by the United States Supreme Court in Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl, 570 U.S. 637 (2013).
  Why here: This is South Carolina's only substantive ICWA decision and shows how its courts analyze tribal notice, 'parent' status and transfer at intake, but its §1912(d)/(f) holdings were reversed and must not be cited as current law.
  Quote: “We affirm the decision of the family court denying the adoption and awarding custody to the biological father.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8294729/adoptive-couple-v-baby-girl/

## DEC-01 · Screen-Out / Unfounded at Intake
- **Beattie v. Aiken County Department of Social Services**, 319 S.C. 449, 462 S.E.2d 276 (1995)
  Holding: The court held that parents whose case was closed as unfounded may not examine the DSS case file, that 'report' covers all investigative information and the reporter's identity is never disclosable, and that FOIA requires separation and release of non-exempt material where a court is asked to conduct that review.
  Why here: This is the controlling authority on what happens to records after a screen-out or unfounded determination — they are sealed and slated for destruction.
  Quote: “The clear language of § 20-7-650 prohibits the disclosure of the DSS case file to Appellants or to anyone else.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1354043/beattie-v-aiken-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Kaci May v. Dorchester School District Two**, Op. No. 6053 (S.C. Ct. App. filed May 29, 2024) (App. Case No. 2020-001352)
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of an injunction, holding S.C. Code § 63-7-920(C) authorizes DSS to interview children at school without a warrant and outside the parents' presence during a mandated investigation, and that the interviews here were reasonable in inception and scope under the Fourth Amendment.
  Why here: The court notes the statutory duty to determine whether a report is 'indicated' or 'unfounded' and the discretion the agency holds in doing so.
  Quote: “we find § 63-7-920(C) expressly authorizes DSS to interview children at school without a warrant when conducting an investigation mandated by § 63-7-920(A)(1)”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9975225/kaci-may-v-dorchester-school-district-two/
- **South Carolina Department of Social Services v. Wilson**, 352 S.C. 445, 574 S.E.2d 730 (2002)
  Holding: The court held that a parent facing an intervention petition and entry on the Central Registry has a protected liberty interest and a due process right of confrontation, and that allowing the child to testify outside his presence without an individualized finding of trauma violated that right.
  Why here: It marks the line between closing a case and seeking intervention, which is the decision point a screen-out or unfounded closure sits on.
  Quote: “Because Wilson did not have the opportunity to be heard in a meaningful manner, his due process right was violated.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1307608/south-carolina-department-of-social-services-v-wilson/
- **Jensen ex rel. Estate of Clark v. Anderson County Department of Social Services**, 304 S.C. 195, 403 S.E.2d 615 (1991)
  Holding: The court affirmed the Court of Appeals, adopting the six-element special duty exception to the public duty rule and holding that the Child Protection Act imposes on the local child protection agency and its social workers a special duty to investigate and intervene whose breach may give rise to a private cause of action, with immunity turning on remand on whether the duty breached was ministerial or discretionary.
  Why here: Closing a report as 'unfounded' is protected only if a thorough investigation preceded it; Jensen treats the duty to investigate thoroughly as ministerial, so closing a file for want of facts forfeits official immunity.
  Quote: “the Act imposes a special duty on the local child protection agency and its social workers to investigate and intervene in cases where child abuse has been reported”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1294742/jensen-ex-rel-estate-of-clark-v-anderson-county-department-of-social/
- **Hodge v. Jones**, 31 F.3d 157 (4th Cir. 1994)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit reversed, holding that the confidential retention of records of a child abuse investigation classified 'unsubstantiated' and 'ruled out' violates neither substantive nor procedural due process, so the state officials were entitled to qualified immunity.
  Why here: Hodge holds that a family cleared at intake has no federal right to have the 'unsubstantiated' record destroyed, which is the federal backdrop against which Beattie's state confidentiality and destruction rules operate.
  Quote: “The record before us presents no substantive due process violation of a familial privacy liberty interest by virtue of the retention of the Hodge investigation report”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/675711/hodge-v-jones/

## DEC-02 · Accept for Investigation / Assessment
- **Kaci May v. Dorchester School District Two**, Op. No. 6053 (S.C. Ct. App. filed May 29, 2024) (App. Case No. 2020-001352)
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of an injunction, holding S.C. Code § 63-7-920(C) authorizes DSS to interview children at school without a warrant and outside the parents' presence during a mandated investigation, and that the interviews here were reasonable in inception and scope under the Fourth Amendment.
  Why here: Kaci May is the leading South Carolina decision on interviews, parental consent and the Fourth Amendment during a CPS assessment.
  Quote: “we find § 63-7-920(C) expressly authorizes DSS to interview children at school without a warrant when conducting an investigation mandated by § 63-7-920(A)(1)”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9975225/kaci-may-v-dorchester-school-district-two/
- **Wildauer v. Frederick County**, 993 F.2d 369 (4th Cir. 1993)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the county, holding the social worker's and deputies' consented entry into the home was reasonable and that medical examinations of the children did not violate the plaintiff's Fourth Amendment rights.
  Why here: Wildauer is the controlling circuit statement of how consent and reasonableness operate when CPS enters a home to assess a report.
  Quote: “investigative home visits by social workers are not subject to the same scrutiny as searches in the criminal context”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9010851/wildauer-v-frederick-county/
- **Renn ex rel. Renn v. Garrison**, 100 F.3d 344 (4th Cir. 1996)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit vacated the denial of qualified immunity, holding the social services defendants acted within the authority state statute gave them to investigate reported neglect and that their conduct stayed inside the clearly established boundaries of the family privacy right.
  Why here: Renn is the Fourth Circuit's substantive statement of the family privacy standard that governs when a caseworker's decision to open and continue an assessment becomes a constitutional intrusion rather than permitted monitoring.
  Quote: “we find as well that their actions were reasonable under the circumstances in relation to the boundaries of the family privacy right that had been clearly established”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2966519/renn-ex-rel-renn-v-garrison/
- **Vosburg v. Department of Social Services**, 884 F.2d 133 (4th Cir. 1989)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit affirmed summary judgment, holding state social workers are absolutely immune from § 1983 liability for the decision to prepare and file a removal petition, while expressly declining to extend that absolute immunity to their investigative conduct.
  Why here: Vosburg is the Fourth Circuit decision establishing that a caseworker's investigative conduct, unlike the decision to petition, carries at most a good-faith qualified immunity — the proposition Wildauer later relies on.
  Quote: “We emphasize, however, that our grant of absolute immunity applies only to those activities of social workers that could be deemed prosecutorial.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/528465/vosburg-v-department-of-social-services/

  Holding: The court held that a parent facing an intervention petition and entry on the Central Registry has a protected liberty interest and a due process right of confrontation, and that allowing the child to testify outside his presence without an individualized finding of trauma violated that right.

  Holding: The court reversed the finding of abuse and neglect and the order placing the mother on the Central Registry, holding she could not be found to have abused an unborn child by conduct occurring while she neither knew nor had reason to know she was pregnant, and that the drug-test evidence was inadmissible hearsay without foundation.
  Why here: It shows that a substantiated finding and Central Registry entry must rest on properly admitted evidence and a culpable mental state.
  Quote: “the family court erred in finding Mother abused and neglected her unborn Child based upon conduct occurring while Mother did not know or have reason to know she was pregnant”

  Holding: The court affirmed, holding the family court had subject matter jurisdiction, that an eighty-two day wait for an administrative hearing was not the 'expedited review' S.C. Code § 63-7-1230 requires, and that ordering the name removed from the Registry pending that hearing was a proper remedy.
  Why here: Wardlaw is the controlling authority on the timeliness of the administrative review that follows an early Central Registry entry.
  Quote: “we find no error by the family court in concluding eighty-two days is not expedited as contemplated by the statute”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7401623/wardlaw-v-sc-dept-of-soc-servs/
- **Whitner v. State**, 328 S.C. 1, 492 S.E.2d 777 (1997)
  Holding: The court held that 'child' in South Carolina's child abuse and endangerment statute includes a viable fetus, so a mother's prenatal drug use can constitute criminal child neglect, and found no due process violation.
  Why here: Whitner supplies the substantive definition of 'child' that underpins a substantiated finding based on prenatal conduct.
  Quote: “We hold the word "child" as used in that statute includes viable fetuses.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1388496/whitner-v-state/
- **Richland County Department of Social Services v. Earles**, 330 S.C. 24, 496 S.E.2d 864 (1998)
  Holding: The court affirmed termination, holding that unavailability based on severe emotional trauma requires a finding that the child would be traumatized by testifying in person, by videotaped deposition and by closed-circuit television, that the record here supported that finding, and that clear and convincing evidence showed the home could not be made safe.
  Why here: Earles shows how the individualized trauma finding Wilson requires is actually made and what expert proof will sustain a child's out-of-court statements as the basis for a founded finding.
  Quote: “Unavailability under § 19-1-180(B)(2)(a)(v) requires a finding that the child will suffer severe trauma by testifying under all three methods.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1398102/richland-county-department-of-social-services-v-earles/
- **Joubert v. South Carolina Department of Social Services**, 341 S.C. 176, 534 S.E.2d 1 (Ct. App. 2000)
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for DSS, holding that a damages action arising from an 'indicated' institutional neglect finding and Central Registry listing is governed by the Tort Claims Act and that, absent a verified claim, the two-year limitations period applied and had expired before suit was filed.
  Why here: Joubert traces the entire track that follows an 'indicated' finding and Central Registry entry — administrative fair hearing, family court review under former § 20-7-655, expungement — and then holds that any damages claim over the erroneous finding is time-barred under the Tort Claims Act unless a verified claim was filed.
  Quote: “if a plaintiff files a statutorily-defined claim within one year of the loss or injury, the statute of limitations is extended to three years”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1236350/joubert-v-south-carolina-department-of-social-services/

## DEC-04 · Emergency Removal Decision
- **Hooper v. Rockwell**, 334 S.C. 281, 513 S.E.2d 358 (1999)
  Holding: The South Carolina Supreme Court upheld the emergency protective custody statute against state and federal constitutional challenge, held that orders issued after a merits hearing are final and must be timely appealed, and affirmed termination of parental rights and the adoption.
  Why here: Any challenge to a pre-order emergency removal in South Carolina begins with Hooper's holding that the statutory scheme is constitutional.
  Quote: “We further conclude that S.C.Code Ann. § 20-7-610(A) (Supp.1997) does not violate the state or federal constitutions.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1316607/hooper-v-rockwell/
- **South Carolina Department of Social Services v. Randy S.**, 390 S.C. 100, 700 S.E.2d 250 (Ct. App. 2010)
  Holding: The court held that where DSS in substance removed children and placed them with a relative, it had brought a removal action and was required to follow the removal procedures — petition, 72-hour probable cause hearing, 35-day merits hearing — rather than proceeding as an intervention.
  Why here: Randy S. holds that an agency cannot avoid removal safeguards by labeling a de facto removal an intervention.
  Quote: “Despite DSS's contention that this was only an intervention action, we find the evidence supports a finding that Children were removed from Father's custody.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2394957/south-carolina-department-of-social-services-v-randy-s/
- **Jordan ex rel. Jordan v. Jackson**, 15 F.3d 333 (4th Cir. 1994)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit upheld an emergency protective custody statute facially and as applied, holding that a 65-hour delay between emergency removal and judicial review did not violate due process while warning that it approached the outer limit.
  Why here: Any decision to remove before a court order must be measured against Jordan's requirement of reasonably prompt post-deprivation judicial review.
  Quote: “We believe this period is near, if not at, the outer limit of permissible delay between a child's removal from his home and judicial review.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6929589/jordan-ex-rel-jordan-v-jackson/
- **White Ex Rel. White v. Chambliss**, 112 F.3d 731 (4th Cir. 1997)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit held South Carolina DSS officials were entitled to qualified immunity on claims arising from the removal of children and the foster placement in which one child died, because no clearly established federal right — including any right created by the South Carolina Child Protection Act — was violated.
  Why here: This is the leading Fourth Circuit case arising from South Carolina removals and sets the immunity backdrop for every emergency removal decision here.
  Quote: “The facts presented by White reveal that the DSS defendants violated no "clearly established" law in removing White's children from her custody.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2966644/white-ex-rel-white-v-chambliss/
- **Weller v. Department Of Social Services For The City Of Baltimore**, 901 F.2d 387 (4th Cir. 1990)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit reinstated a father's procedural due process claim, holding that a parent is entitled to a State-initiated hearing before being deprived of custody (or a prompt hearing ratifying emergency action) and that an after-the-fact state tort remedy does not satisfy due process.
  Why here: Weller establishes that the agency, not the parent, bears the burden of initiating judicial review after it takes a child, which is what S.C. Code § 63-7-700 codifies.
  Quote: “Ex post procedures are generally disapproved where the only remedy is an independent tort action”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/540031/weller-v-department-of-social-services-for-the-city-of-baltimore/
- **Stanley v. Illinois**, 405 U.S. 645 (1972)
  Holding: The Court held that an unwed father is entitled as a matter of due process to a hearing on his fitness before his children may be taken from him, and that denying him the hearing extended to all other parents denied him the equal protection of the laws.
  Why here: Stanley is the Supreme Court decision that Weller and Jordan implement — the State must give the parent a hearing on fitness before taking the children, so emergency action can only postpone, never replace, that hearing.
  Quote: “We conclude that, as a matter of due process of law, Stanley was entitled to a hearing on his fitness as a parent before his children were taken from him”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/108497/stanley-v-illinois/

  Why here: White holds that harm suffered by a child in a foster home was not, at the time, clearly established as state-inflicted harm, limiting federal exposure for placement decisions.

  Holding: The court affirmed termination and the foster parents' adoption, holding that an attempt to remedy the conditions causing removal is not enough because the statute requires that they in fact be remedied, and that a foster parent is an interested party with standing to petition for termination of parental rights.

## ACT-05 · Protective Order

  Why here: Any order restricting a parent's custody or contact must be preceded by notice that the issue will be decided.

  Holding: The court held that a parent facing an intervention petition and entry on the Central Registry has a protected liberty interest and a due process right of confrontation, and that allowing the child to testify outside his presence without an individualized finding of trauma violated that right.

  Why here: The mother's visitation was terminated by a permanency planning order because she had not completed her treatment plan, making Headden the South Carolina illustration of contact being restricted as a protective measure short of removal or termination.

  Why here: Visitation had been ordered to cease because contact was traumatizing the children, and Earles is the South Carolina decision that measures that kind of protective restriction against evidence of severe emotional trauma.
