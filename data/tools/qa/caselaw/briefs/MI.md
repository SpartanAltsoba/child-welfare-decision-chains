# MI — 42 authorities, verified 2026-09-20

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Marcelletti v Bathani**, 198 Mich. App. 655, 500 N.W.2d 124 (1993)
  Holding: The Court affirmed summary disposition for a physician sued for failing to report suspected abuse of another child, holding the Child Protection Law creates a private remedy only for the identified abused child and that no common-law duty ran to a later-injured third party.
  Why here: A mandated reporter's failure to report is civilly actionable only by the child identified in the suspected abuse, not by other children later harmed.
  Quote: “Michigan's child abuse reporting statute creates a private right of action only in an identified abused child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1625994/marcelletti-v-bathani/
- **Lee v Detroit Medical Center**, 285 Mich. App. 51, 775 N.W.2d 326 (2009)
  Holding: The Court held that an employer may be vicariously liable for an employee's failure to report under MCL 722.623 and that the Child Protection Law's good-faith immunity in MCL 722.625 does not extend to negligence or medical malpractice; the Supreme Court denied leave.
  Why here: Lee is the leading Michigan decision on the civil consequences of a mandated reporter's failure to report and on the limits of reporter immunity.
  Quote: “We conclude that an employer may be held liable for its employee's failure to report under MCL 722.623”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1845928/lee-v-detroit-medical-center/
- **Brent v Wayne County Department of Human Services**, 901 F.3d 656 (6th Cir. 2018)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit held Michigan's Child Protection Law immunity is an immunity from suit that is immediately appealable, granted state defendants absolute immunity for conduct connected to preparing and filing the removal petition, and reversed dismissal of certain § 1983 and gross-negligence claims.
  Why here: Michigan's Child Protection Law immunity for good-faith reporting and cooperation is a full immunity from suit, not merely a defense at trial.
  Quote: “We therefore conclude, like the Court in Forsyth, that the entitlement under Martin "is an immunity from suit rather than a mere defense to liability."”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4529474/nathaniel-brent-v-wayne-cty-dept-of-human-servs/
- **In re Piland**, 324 Mich. App. 337, 920 N.W.2d 403 (2018)
  Holding: The Court held that MCL 722.634, which provides that a parent legitimately practicing religious beliefs is not for that reason alone a negligent parent, applies in child protective proceedings, and that respondents were entitled to a jury instruction on it at adjudication.
  Why here: A report of withheld medical care must be assessed against MCL 722.634, which limits when religiously motivated non-treatment counts as neglect.
  Quote: “We conclude that the mandate of MCL 722.634 applies in child protection proceedings.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8285747/in-re-piland/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Andrews v Hickman County**, 700 F.3d 845 (6th Cir. 2012)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit held there is no social-worker exception to the Fourth Amendment and that a caseworker may not rely on an accompanying officer to excuse a warrantless home entry, but granted qualified immunity because the right was not yet clearly established in the circuit.
  Why here: A joint CPS and police response does not convert the caseworker's entry into a lawful police entry; each actor needs a warrant, consent or exigency.
  Quote: “We join other circuits in recognizing that Fourth Amendment standards are the same, whether the state actor is a law enforcement officer or a social worker.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/812950/robert-andrews-v-hickman-county-tennessee/
- **Bambach v Moegle**, 92 F.4th 615 (6th Cir. 2024)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit reversed the denial of qualified immunity to Michigan CPS workers, holding no clearly established law governed a removal that began with the father's express consent and was at most impliedly revoked before a court order issued weeks later.
  Why here: The opinion maps the circuit's spectrum, from removal with no order, consent or process at one end to fully consented placement at the other.
  Quote: “We find that no clearly established law put the state defendants on notice that they were violating the Bambachs' Fourteenth and Fourth Amendment rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9473552/mark-bambach-v-gina-moegle/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **In re Macomber**, 436 Mich. 386 (1990)
  Holding: The Court held that chapter XIIA gives the probate court broad authority to enter orders affecting adults, including an order requiring a parent to leave the home, where such orders are necessary for the well-being of a child within the court's jurisdiction.
  Why here: A court that has jurisdiction over a child may direct adults in the household, which is the statutory basis for judicially initiated protective action.
  Quote: “we hold that the Legislature has conferred very broad authority to the probate court”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8000356/kent-county-department-of-social-services-v-macomber/
- **In re AMB**, 248 Mich. App. 144, 640 N.W.2d 262 (2002)
  Holding: The Court held the family court's ex parte and referee proceedings authorizing withdrawal of an infant's life support were fatally defective, that inadequate notice to a respondent is a jurisdictional defect, and it set out the notice, evidentiary and decision-maker standards such a case requires.
  Why here: A judicially initiated protective proceeding is void as to a parent who was never properly notified, no matter how urgent the medical situation.
  Quote: “The absence of this notice to a respondent in a protective proceeding constitutes a jurisdictional defect.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1712181/in-re-amb/
- **In re Sanders**, 495 Mich. 394, 852 N.W.2d 524 (2014)
  Holding: The Court held Michigan's one-parent doctrine unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment, so a court may not enter dispositional orders infringing a parent's rights until that parent has been individually adjudicated unfit, and overruled In re CR.
  Why here: A judicially ordered CPS referral or petition against only one parent cannot by itself authorize orders against the other parent, who is entitled to his or her own adjudication.
  Quote: “due process requires a specific adjudication of a parent's unfitness before the state can infringe the constitutionally protected parent-child relationship”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8001640/in-re-sanders/
- **In re CR**, 250 Mich. App. 185, 646 N.W.2d 506 (2002)
  Holding: The Court affirmed the termination of an unadjudicated father's parental rights, holding that the court rules do not require the agency to plead and prove an adjudication against every parent before the family court may act in its dispositional capacity — the one-parent doctrine the Michigan Supreme Court held unconstitutional in In re Sanders (2014).
  Why here: OVERRULED BY IN RE SANDERS (2014): CR is the origin of Michigan's one-parent doctrine, under which jurisdiction over the child was thought to authorize orders against a parent who had never been adjudicated.
  Quote: “making it possible, under the proper circumstances, to terminate parental rights even of a parent who, for one reason or another, has not participated in the protective proceeding”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2013344/in-re-cr/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In re JK**, 468 Mich. 202, 661 N.W.2d 216 (2003)
  Holding: The Court held the trial court clearly erred in terminating the rights of a mother who had fulfilled every requirement of her parent-agency agreement, because compliance is affirmative evidence of her ability to provide proper care and custody.
  Why here: A parent who seeks help and then does everything the agency asks is entitled to have that compliance weighed in her favor.
  Quote: “the parent's compliance with the parent-agency agreement is evidence of her ability to provide proper care and custody”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/848810/in-re-jk/
- **In re Hicks/Brown**, No. 153786 (Mich. May 8, 2017)
  Holding: The Court vacated a termination order, holding that the Department's statutory duty to make reasonable efforts at reunification includes reasonably modifying its services to accommodate a parent's known disability under Title II of the ADA.
  Why here: A parent with a disability who asks for help is entitled to services designed so that she can actually benefit from them.
  Quote: “Absent reasonable modifications to the services or programs offered to a disabled parent, the Department has failed in its duty under the ADA to reasonably accommodate a disability.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4389733/in-re-hicksbrown-minors/
- **In re Terry**, 240 Mich. App. 14, 610 N.W.2d 563 (2000)
  Holding: The Court affirmed the termination, holding that the ADA is not a defense to a termination proceeding but that the agency's reunification services must comply with the ADA, and that a parent who does not raise a failure to accommodate when the service plan is adopted may not raise it at the termination hearing.
  Why here: A parent with a disability who comes to the agency for help must ask for accommodation at the front of the case, because Terry makes the timing of the request decisive.
  Quote: “The time for asserting the need for accommodation in services is when the court adopts a service plan, not at the time of a dispositional hearing to terminate parental rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7960556/in-re-terry/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Federated Publications, Inc v City of Lansing**, 467 Mich. 98 (2002)
  Holding: The Court held that FOIA places the burden on the public body to prove an exemption, that exemptions turning on legal determinations are reviewed de novo while discretionary balancing is reviewed for clear error, and remanded for application of the public-interest balancing test.
  Why here: An oversight body or requester seeking agency records starts from a presumption of disclosure that the agency must overcome.
  Quote: “we hold that MCL 15.240(4) of the FOIA specifically places the burden of proof on the public body to show that the public record is exempt from disclosure”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/848850/federated-publications-inc-v-city-of-lansing/
- **Michigan Federation of Teachers & School Related Personnel v University of Michigan**, 481 Mich. 657, 753 N.W.2d 28 (2008)
  Holding: The Court held that public employees' home addresses and telephone numbers satisfy both prongs of FOIA's privacy exemption and are exempt from disclosure because releasing them would reveal little or nothing about the conduct of government.
  Why here: Oversight requests for agency records will be trimmed where the material identifies private individuals rather than agency conduct.
  Quote: “We conclude that employees' home addresses and telephone numbers are information of an embarrassing, intimate, private, or confidential nature.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/839193/michigan-federation-of-teachers-school-related-personnel-v-univer/
- **In re Harper**, 302 Mich. App. 349, 839 N.W.2d 44 (2013)
  Holding: The Court vacated a trial court order directing DHS to remove a mother's name from the child abuse central registry, holding that MCL 722.627 vests expunction authority exclusively in the department and that the parent must exhaust the administrative process first.
  Why here: Registry corrections are an administrative, not judicial, oversight function in Michigan, which shapes where a complaint must be filed.
  Quote: “The plain language of the statute grants exclusive jurisdiction to DHS to control expunction from the central registry.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8006876/in-re-harper/
- **Bradley v Saranac Community Schools Board of Education**, 455 Mich. 285, 565 N.W.2d 650 (1997)
  Holding: The Court held that the personnel records of public school teachers and administrators must be disclosed under FOIA because information is of a personal nature only if it reveals intimate or embarrassing details of an individual's private life, and performance appraisals, discipline and complaints about public employment are not.
  Why here: An oversight request for records showing how public employees performed their public duties is not defeated by the FOIA privacy exemption.
  Quote: “we conclude that information is of a personal nature if it reveals intimate or embarrassing details of an individual's private life”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1723461/bradley-v-saranac-community-schools-board-of-education/
- **Herald Co v City of Bay City**, 463 Mich. 111, 614 N.W.2d 873 (2000)
  Holding: The Court held Bay City violated FOIA by refusing to disclose records identifying the final candidates for fire chief, because an application for a public job is not information of a personal nature and its disclosure would not be a clearly unwarranted invasion of privacy, while separately holding that a single city manager is not a public body under the Open Meetings Act.
  Why here: Herald states the interpretive default for every Michigan records request an oversight body makes: FOIA is prodisclosure and its exemptions are read narrowly against the agency.
  Quote: “On its express terms, the FOIA is a prodisclosure statute, and the exemptions stated in § 13 are narrowly construed.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2211455/herald-co-v-city-of-bay-city/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In re Morris**, 491 Mich. 81 (2012)
  Holding: The Court conditionally reversed for ICWA-notice violations, holding that almost any indicium of tribal membership triggers 25 USC 1912(a) notice, that a parent cannot waive the tribe's independent rights, and that the trial court must maintain a documentary record of notice and proof of service.
  Why here: Morris sets Michigan's very low threshold for ICWA notice and makes the trial court responsible for documenting that notice was actually served.
  Quote: “we hold that virtually any criteria on which tribal membership might be based suffices to trigger the 25 USC 1912(a) notice requirement”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8001634/in-re-morris/
- **In re Williams**, 501 Mich. 289, 915 N.W.2d 328 (2018)
  Holding: The Court held that a parent of an Indian child who executes a consent under MCL 712B.13 of MIFPA may withdraw it any time before a final adoption order, even in a child protective proceeding, although the department may then refile under MCL 712B.15.
  Why here: MIFPA and ICWA apply together in Michigan, and a parent of an Indian child keeps MIFPA's withdrawal right even inside a child protective case.
  Quote: “He was therefore entitled to withdraw his consent at any time before entry of a final order of adoption.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8284536/in-re-williams/
- **In re Detmer/Beaudry**, 321 Mich. App. 49, 910 N.W.2d 318 (2017)
  Holding: The Court held MIFPA's special protections applied to the child taken from the mother's care even though she was placed with a relative, and vacated for non-compliance, but held they did not apply to the sibling the mother voluntarily placed with his non-respondent father.
  Why here: Detmer defines what counts as a 'removal' that triggers MIFPA in Michigan, including a removal into relative care.
  Quote: “we hold that the special protections set forth in the Michigan Indian Family Preservation Act do apply to AB's removal”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8283550/in-re-detmerbeaudry/
- **In re Payne/Pumphrey/Fortson**, 311 Mich. App. 49, 874 N.W.2d 205 (2015)
  Holding: The Court reversed the termination of parental rights to two Indian children because the trial court's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt finding of likely serious harm was not supported by the qualified-expert-witness testimony ICWA and MIFPA require.
  Why here: The qualified expert witness is a mandatory component of the evidentiary finding in every Michigan ICWA/MIFPA termination.
  Quote: “in order to terminate parental rights to an Indian child, a trial court's "beyond a reasonable doubt" finding must "contain" or "encompass" testimony of a qualified expert witness”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8007066/in-re-paynepumphreyfortson/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **In re Rood**, 483 Mich. 73, 763 N.W.2d 587 (2009) (opinion by Corrigan, J.)
  Holding: In a lead opinion the Court affirmed reversal of a termination because DHS's and the court's repeated failures to notify the father of hearings and to locate, assess and engage him denied him minimal procedural due process.
  Why here: Once a child is taken into protective custody, both parents acquire notice rights that attach from the preliminary hearing forward.
  Quote: “A parent whose parental rights have not been terminated, including one who is not a named respondent, must be notified of and permitted to participate in each hearing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/837290/in-re-rood/
- **In re AMB**, 248 Mich. App. 144, 640 N.W.2d 262 (2002)
  Holding: The Court held the family court's ex parte and referee proceedings authorizing withdrawal of an infant's life support were fatally defective, that inadequate notice to a respondent is a jurisdictional defect, and it set out the notice, evidentiary and decision-maker standards such a case requires.
  Why here: Emergency protective custody does not suspend the notice requirements that give the court power over the parent.
  Quote: “The absence of this notice to a respondent in a protective proceeding constitutes a jurisdictional defect.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1712181/in-re-amb/
- **Andrews v Hickman County**, 700 F.3d 845 (6th Cir. 2012)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit held there is no social-worker exception to the Fourth Amendment and that a caseworker may not rely on an accompanying officer to excuse a warrantless home entry, but granted qualified immunity because the right was not yet clearly established in the circuit.
  Why here: Taking protective custody from inside a home requires the same Fourth Amendment justification that would be required of an officer.
  Quote: “We join other circuits in recognizing that Fourth Amendment standards are the same, whether the state actor is a law enforcement officer or a social worker.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/812950/robert-andrews-v-hickman-county-tennessee/
- **Bambach v Moegle**, 92 F.4th 615 (6th Cir. 2024)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit reversed the denial of qualified immunity to Michigan CPS workers, holding no clearly established law governed a removal that began with the father's express consent and was at most impliedly revoked before a court order issued weeks later.
  Why here: Protective custody that starts as a consented safety arrangement occupies a legal middle ground where liability is presently uncertain.
  Quote: “We find that no clearly established law put the state defendants on notice that they were violating the Bambachs' Fourteenth and Fourth Amendment rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9473552/mark-bambach-v-gina-moegle/
- **Smith v Williams-Ash**, 520 F.3d 596 (6th Cir. 2008)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the caseworker, holding that parents who voluntarily consent to a safety plan removing their children from the home are not entitled to a pre-deprivation hearing where they never used the plan's stated mechanism to withdraw that consent.
  Why here: A child moved out of the home under a signed safety plan is not taken into protective custody for due-process purposes so long as the parents' consent continues, which is the legal middle ground between voluntary placement and formal removal.
  Quote: “the Smiths needed to explicitly withdraw the consent they explicitly gave, thus requiring Children's Services to either return the children or file a formal complaint against them”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1201271/smith-v-williams-ash/
- **Schulkers v Kammer**, 955 F.3d 520 (6th Cir. 2020)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit affirmed the denial of qualified immunity on the Fourteenth Amendment claims, holding that a safety plan excuses the state from affording a parent process only where the parent's consent is voluntary and free of duress, and it reversed on the Fourth Amendment claims while holding prospectively that a social worker needs at least reasonable suspicion of abuse to conduct an in-school interview without a warrant or consent.
  Why here: Schulkers materially qualifies Smith v Williams-Ash: a plan the parent signs under threat, or repeatedly asks to have lifted, is not the consent that excuses a hearing before the state separates a family.
  Quote: “The only exception to this rule is when a parent voluntarily consents to the terms of a safety plan without duress.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4520785/holly-schulkers-v-elizabeth-kammer/

## OUT-02 · Termination of Parental Rights / Adoption
- **Santosky v. Kramer**, 455 U.S. 745 (1982)
  Holding: The Court held that before a State may terminate parental rights due process requires proof by at least clear and convincing evidence, and vacated New York's use of a fair-preponderance standard.
  Why here: Santosky is the federal constitutional floor for Michigan's clear-and-convincing standard on the statutory grounds for termination.
  Quote: “We hold that such a standard adequately conveys to the factfinder the level of subjective certainty about his factual conclusions necessary to satisfy due process.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/110676/santosky-v-kramer/
- **In re Trejo Minors**, 462 Mich. 341, 612 N.W.2d 407 (2000)
  Holding: The Court upheld the then-current MCL 712A.19b(5), holding the trial court may find termination clearly not in the child's best interests on the whole record without assigning the parent any burden of producing best-interest evidence, and reviewed both the statutory ground and best interests for clear error.
  Why here: Trejo sets Michigan's two-step termination framework and the clear-error standard of review, though the best-interest step was amended in 2008 and now runs through In re Moss.
  Quote: “Subsection 19b(5) allows the court to find that termination is "clearly not in the child's best interests" despite the establishment of one or more grounds for termination.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1862500/in-re-trejo-minors/
- **In re Moss**, 301 Mich. App. 76, 836 N.W.2d 182 (2013)
  Holding: The Court held that after a statutory ground is proved by clear and convincing evidence, the best-interest determination under MCL 712A.19b(5) need only be proved by a preponderance of the evidence, and affirmed the termination.
  Why here: Moss fixes the two different burdens of proof that control every Michigan termination after the 2008 amendment to MCL 712A.19b(5).
  Quote: “we hold that whether termination of parental rights is in the best interests of the child must be proved by a preponderance of the evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8006821/in-re-moss/
- **In re Miller**, 433 Mich. 331 (1989)
  Holding: The Court reinstated the probate court's termination order, holding that findings in a termination case may be set aside only if clearly erroneous and that MCR 2.613(C) requires deference to the trial judge's assessment of witness credibility.
  Why here: This is the source of Michigan's clear-error review of a clear-and-convincing termination finding and of the deference owed to the trial judge who saw the witnesses.
  Quote: “An appellate court should not reverse the findings of a trial court in such a case unless its findings are clearly erroneous.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8000336/department-of-social-services-v-miller/
- **In re Olive/Metts Minors**, 297 Mich. App. 35, 823 N.W.2d 144 (2012)
  Holding: The Court affirmed termination as to three children but vacated the best-interest analysis as to twins placed with a relative, holding the trial court must decide best interests for each child individually and must expressly address relative placement.
  Why here: Failure to make child-specific best-interest findings, or to address relative placement, is reversible error in a Michigan termination.
  Quote: “We hold that the trial court has a duty to decide the best interests of each child individually.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8006642/in-re-olivemetts-minors/
- **In re Ferranti**, No. 157907 (Mich. June 12, 2019)
  Holding: The Court overruled In re Hatcher, holding that a child protective proceeding is one continuous action so a parent may attack adjudication errors on appeal from the termination order, and it vacated the adjudication and termination because the plea colloquy omitted the advice MCR 3.971 requires.
  Why here: A termination order rests on a valid adjudication; if the plea taking the court's jurisdiction was defective, the termination falls with it.
  Quote: “We hold that an appeal of an adjudication error in an appeal from an order terminating parental rights is not a collateral attack.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4628995/in-re-j-ferranti-minor/

## FAIL-02 · Unlawful Removal
- **In re Sanders**, 495 Mich. 394, 852 N.W.2d 524 (2014)
  Holding: The Court held Michigan's one-parent doctrine unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment, so a court may not enter dispositional orders infringing a parent's rights until that parent has been individually adjudicated unfit, and overruled In re CR.
  Why here: Dispositional interference with an unadjudicated parent's custody is a due-process violation and is the leading Michigan ground for undoing such an order.
  Quote: “due process requires a specific adjudication of a parent's unfitness before the state can infringe the constitutionally protected parent-child relationship”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8001640/in-re-sanders/
- **In re AMB**, 248 Mich. App. 144, 640 N.W.2d 262 (2002)
  Holding: The Court held the family court's ex parte and referee proceedings authorizing withdrawal of an infant's life support were fatally defective, that inadequate notice to a respondent is a jurisdictional defect, and it set out the notice, evidentiary and decision-maker standards such a case requires.
  Why here: The Court treated the state's action on a defective order as without legal authority, the paradigm of an unlawful state intervention.
  Quote: “The absence of this notice to a respondent in a protective proceeding constitutes a jurisdictional defect.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1712181/in-re-amb/
- **Andrews v Hickman County**, 700 F.3d 845 (6th Cir. 2012)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit held there is no social-worker exception to the Fourth Amendment and that a caseworker may not rely on an accompanying officer to excuse a warrantless home entry, but granted qualified immunity because the right was not yet clearly established in the circuit.
  Why here: Andrews is the Sixth Circuit's controlling statement that a warrantless caseworker entry is unconstitutional absent consent or exigency.
  Quote: “We join other circuits in recognizing that Fourth Amendment standards are the same, whether the state actor is a law enforcement officer or a social worker.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/812950/robert-andrews-v-hickman-county-tennessee/
- **Bambach v Moegle**, 92 F.4th 615 (6th Cir. 2024)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit reversed the denial of qualified immunity to Michigan CPS workers, holding no clearly established law governed a removal that began with the father's express consent and was at most impliedly revoked before a court order issued weeks later.
  Why here: This is the most recent published Sixth Circuit application of the unlawful-removal framework to Michigan CPS workers.
  Quote: “We find that no clearly established law put the state defendants on notice that they were violating the Bambachs' Fourteenth and Fourth Amendment rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9473552/mark-bambach-v-gina-moegle/
- **Brent v Wayne County Department of Human Services**, 901 F.3d 656 (6th Cir. 2018)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit held Michigan's Child Protection Law immunity is an immunity from suit that is immediately appealable, granted state defendants absolute immunity for conduct connected to preparing and filing the removal petition, and reversed dismissal of certain § 1983 and gross-negligence claims.
  Why here: Brent is the Michigan-specific Sixth Circuit case sorting which removal-related acts get absolute immunity and which remain actionable.

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4529474/nathaniel-brent-v-wayne-cty-dept-of-human-servs/

  Why here: It supplies what Andrews (entry), Bambach (consent) and Brent (immunity) do not: the rule that seizing the children themselves without a warrant or exigency is the constitutional violation, and that a later probable-cause finding does not cure it.
  Quote: “The relevant question is whether there was exigency, not probable cause: in the absence of exigency, a warrant is required, even "when probable cause is clearly present."”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1035935/kovacic-v-cuyahoga-county-department-of-children-family-services/

- Added In re Terry, 240 Mich App 14; 610 NW2d 563 (2000) (cluster 8005993, opinion 7960556) for INP-05, ACT-02, OUT-01 and FAIL-05: it holds the ADA is not a defense to termination but that reunification services must comply with it, and it supplies the preservation rule — the accommodation claim must be raised when the service plan is adopted — that decides whether a Hicks/Brown argument can be made at all. CourtListener types this single opinion '020lead' with author Cavanagh, P.J.; the cluster (precedential status Published) contains no separate concurrence or dissent, so it is the opinion of the Court.
- Added In re Hatcher, 443 Mich 426; 505 NW2d 834 (1993) (cluster 2123972, opinion 2123972) for DEC-05, FLAGGED AS OVERRULED: In re Ferranti (2019) expressly overruled it. Hatcher is included only so that a reader who encounters it in a Westlaw or CourtListener search sees it marked and does not misapply the collateral-bar rule. Do not cite it as current law. - Added In re CR, 250 Mich App 185; 646 NW2d 506 (2002) (cluster 2013344, opinion 2013344) for INP-04, DEC-03 and ACT-05, FLAGGED AS OVERRULED: In re Sanders (2014) expressly overruled it. CR is the source of Michigan's one-parent doctrine and is included only to be marked as dead law alongside Sanders. The opinion is per curiam and unanimous; the quoted clause is from the body of the opinion.
- Added Bowie v Arder, 441 Mich 23; 490 NW2d 568 (1992) (cluster 2116959, opinion 2116959) for ACT-04 and OUT-03: it is the foundational Michigan decision on third-party standing to seek custody of a child, cited inside the In re Sanders opinion this brief already quotes, and it is the threshold question for any relative, kin caregiver or proposed guardian entering a Michigan case.
- Added Bradley v Saranac Community Schools Board of Education, 455 Mich 285; 565 NW2d 650 (1997) (cluster 1723461, opinion 1723461) for INP-09, FAIL-06, PMC-02 and PMC-05: it supplies the 'intimate or embarrassing details of an individual's private life' definition of 'information of a personal nature' that Michigan Federation of Teachers expressly revises, so the FOIA privacy exemption can now be briefed with both halves of the pair. The quoted sentence is from the majority; Bradley carries a partial dissent that objects to precisely this definition.
- Added Herald Co v City of Bay City, 463 Mich 111; 614 NW2d 873 (2000) (cluster 2211455, opinion 2211455) for INP-09, FAIL-06, PMC-05 and PMC-06: it is the third controlling Michigan Supreme Court FOIA decision of the period and states the prodisclosure/narrow-construction default that governs how a claimed exemption is read. Note that its second holding — that a single executive is not a 'public body' — is an Open Meetings Act ruling, not a FOIA ruling, and should not be transplanted into a FOIA argument.
- Added In re Gach, 315 Mich App 83; 889 NW2d 707 (2016) (cluster 8007084, opinion 7961848) for DEC-05, FAIL-01 and OUT-05: it holds MCL 712A.19b(3)(l), the prior-termination ground, unconstitutional because it creates an irrebuttable presumption of unfitness, and it is a live defense any Michigan termination brief should carry. CourtListener types Boonstra, P.J.'s opinion '020lead'; Wilder, J., concurred with it (making it the opinion of the Court on the reversal) and Meter, J., filed a separate concurrence whose text CourtListener does not carry. - Added Smith v Williams-Ash, 520 F.3d 596 (6th Cir. 2008) (cluster 1201271, opinion 1201271) for INP-12, DEC-02, DEC-06 and ACT-01: it is the controlling circuit authority on when a signed voluntary safety plan defeats a procedural due process claim about the absence of a hearing, and it is the single case most likely to decide a real Michigan safety-plan dispute. The quoted sentence is from the majority; Judge Clay dissented on the ground that voluntariness was a jury question, and Schulkers v Kammer later vindicated much of that concern.
- Added Kovacic v Cuyahoga County Department of Children & Family Services, 724 F.3d 687 (6th Cir. 2013) (cluster 1035935, opinion 1035935) for DEC-04 and FAIL-02: it is the circuit's leading treatment of the warrantless seizure of children from the home, which Andrews (entry), Bambach (consent) and Brent (immunity) do not cover. Note this is the 2013 immunity appeal, not the earlier 2010 decision at 606 F.3d 301 (cluster 147186). The quote is from the majority; Judge Sutton dissented.
- Added Schulkers v Kammer, 955 F.3d 520 (6th Cir. 2020) (cluster 4740438, opinion 4520785) for INP-12, DEC-02, ACT-01 and INP-08: it materially qualifies Smith v Williams-Ash by holding that a safety plan excuses process only where consent is voluntary and free of duress, and it separately holds that the Fourth Amendment governs a social worker's in-school interview of a child. Read together, Smith, Schulkers and Bambach show that the consent question is far more contested than Bambach alone suggests; the brief's earlier characterization of Bambach as 'the most recent published Sixth Circuit application' of the removal framework should be read with Schulkers in view.
