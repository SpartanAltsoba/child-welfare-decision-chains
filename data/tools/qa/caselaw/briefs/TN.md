## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Hawk v. Hawk**, 855 S.W.2d 573 (Tenn. 1993)
  Holding: The court held that article I, section 8 of the Tennessee Constitution protects parents' privacy in child-rearing decisions and that the State may not intervene in an intact family absent a showing of harm to the child.
  Why here: Supplies the state constitutional baseline that a report alone does not justify intrusion absent indicia of harm.
  Quote: “Absent some harm to the child, we find that the state lacks a sufficiently compelling justification for interfering with this fundamental right.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1669174/hawk-v-hawk/
- **Draper v. Westerfield**, 181 S.W.3d 283 (Tenn. 2005)
  Holding: In a wrongful-death action arising from a fatal child abuse case, the court held that the reporting statute's immunity under Tenn. Code Ann. sec. 37-1-410(a) protects only persons who actually make a report of harm, and that the physician who did not report was not immune.
  Why here: Defines the scope and limits of Tennessee's statutory immunity for mandatory reporters.
  Quote: “immunity under Tennessee Code Annotated section 37-1-410(a) is afforded only to those who actually report harm”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1058197/draper-v-westerfield/
- **Fitzpatrick v. Tennessee Department of Children's Services**, No. M2013-00823-COA-R3-CV (Tenn. Ct. App. Mar. 18, 2014)
  Holding: Reviewing DCS's classification of foster parents as indicated perpetrators of neglect, the court held they received the fundamentally fair procedures due process requires through the file review and contested administrative hearing, affirmed the lack-of-supervision indication, and remanded on attorney's fees for the reversed environmental-neglect indication.
  Why here: Shows how a report about a child in care is routed into the DCS investigative and classification system.
  Quote: “Procedural due process requires a government entity to employ fundamentally fair procedures whenever it acts to deprive a person of a right to or interest in life, liberty, or property.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2669670/jeff-and-melissa-fitzpatrick-v-state-of-tennessee-department-of/
- **Troxel v. Granville**, 530 U.S. 57 (2000)
  Holding: The Court affirmed the Washington Supreme Court's invalidation of that State's third-party visitation statute as applied, holding that where a parent is fit the trial court must accord special weight to her own determination about visitation rather than substitute the judge's view of the children's best interests.
  Why here: Supplies the federal presumption of parental fitness against which a bare report about an otherwise intact family must be weighed.
  Quote: “there is a presumption that fit parents act in the best interests of their children”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1087668/troxel-v-granville/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Andrews v. Hickman County**, 700 F.3d 845 (6th Cir. 2012)
  Holding: In a Tennessee case, the Sixth Circuit held that social workers are bound by the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement when entering a home to investigate, that the plaintiffs alleged a violation, but that the social workers were entitled to qualified immunity because the right was not clearly established in 2008; the deputy's denial of immunity was affirmed.
  Why here: Addresses a joint law-enforcement and CPS response to a referral at a Tennessee home.
  Quote: “we agree that a social worker, like other state officers, is governed by the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/812950/robert-andrews-v-hickman-county-tennessee/
- **Kovacic v. Cuyahoga County Department of Children & Family Services**, 724 F.3d 687 (6th Cir. 2013)
  Holding: The Sixth Circuit affirmed denial of absolute and qualified immunity to social workers who removed children without a warrant, holding it was clearly established by 2002 that the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement and its exigency exception apply to child removals.
  Why here: Confirms that caseworkers accompanying or acting on police information remain subject to warrant rules.
  Quote: “it was clearly established that Fourth Amendment warrant requirements, including the exigent-circumstances exception, apply to the removal of children from their homes by social workers”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1035935/kovacic-v-cuyahoga-county-department-of-children-family-services/
- **State v. Meeks**, 262 S.W.3d 710 (Tenn. 2008)
  Holding: The court held the warrantless entry lawful, setting out Tennessee's exigent-circumstances test: whether the circumstances gave rise to an objectively reasonable belief of a compelling need to act with insufficient time to obtain a warrant.
  Why here: Supplies the standard officers apply when entering a home during the call that generates a CPS referral.
  Quote: “the inquiry is whether the circumstances give rise to an objectively reasonable belief that there was a compelling need to act and insufficient time to obtain a warrant”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1057727/state-v-meeks/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held that the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's reasonable-efforts provision does not create a right enforceable by beneficiaries through section 1983 or an implied right of action.
  Why here: Explains why federal child-welfare compliance is policed through federal review rather than private suit.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Memphis Publishing Co. v. Cherokee Children & Family Services, Inc.**, 87 S.W.3d 67 (Tenn. 2002)
  Holding: The court held that a private nonprofit operating as the functional equivalent of a government agency in administering state child-care programs is subject to the Tennessee Public Records Act as to all of its records.
  Why here: Makes contractor records reachable by auditors and oversight bodies examining child-welfare spending.
  Quote: “Cherokee's status as the functional equivalent of a governmental agency is sufficient to place it within the scope of the Tennessee Public Records Act”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1060374/memphis-publishing-co-v-cherokee-children-family-services-inc/
- **The Tennessean v. Metropolitan Government of Nashville & Davidson County**, 485 S.W.3d 857 (Tenn. 2016)
  Holding: The court held that records of an open criminal investigation fall within the Public Records Act's state-law exception through Tenn. R. Crim. P. 16 and need not be disclosed while the prosecution and collateral challenges remain pending.
  Why here: Marks the limit on oversight access to records while a parallel criminal investigation is open.
  Quote: “Metro is not required to disclose the requested investigative records because the records come within the Public Records Act state law exception”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3186619/the-tennessean-v-metropolitan-government-of-nashville-and-davidson/
- **Schneider v. City of Jackson**, 226 S.W.3d 332 (Tenn. 2007)
  Holding: The court held that Tennessee common law contains no law-enforcement investigative privilege capable of operating as a state-law exception to the Public Records Act, reinstated the requesters' attorney's fee award and the injunction requiring written responses, and remanded to determine which records belong to a pending criminal investigation.
  Why here: Confines the exceptions an agency may raise against an oversight body's records request to those the General Assembly actually enacted.
  Quote: “We hold that the law enforcement privilege is not part of Tennessee common law and therefore does not operate as a "state law" exception to the Public Records Act.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1057863/schneider-v-city-of-jackson/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Palmore v. Sidoti**, 466 U.S. 429 (1984)
  Holding: The Supreme Court reversed a custody change based on the racial composition of the mother's new household, holding that the Equal Protection Clause forbids courts from giving effect to private racial biases in child custody decisions.
  Why here: Bars race-based reasoning in placement decisions except where a specific statute such as ICWA applies.
  Quote: “Private biases may be outside the reach of the law, but the law cannot, directly or indirectly, give them effect.”

  Why here: Is the Tennessee appellate decision addressing ICWA notice when a child may be an Indian child, and why that issue survives non-preservation.
  Quote: “Kianna's failure to raise the notice issue at trial should not preclude consideration of the issue on appeal in light of the potential for collateral attack in a subsequent proceeding”

Illinois, 405 U.S. 645 (1972) for INP-12, DEC-04 and FAIL-02: it is the foundational federal holding that a parent is constitutionally entitled to a hearing on fitness before his children are removed, the baseline that exigency doctrine excuses and that a no-hearing removal violates.
- Added Kottmyer v. Maas, 436 F.3d 684 (6th Cir. 2006) for DEC-01, DEC-02 and INP-10: it is the controlling circuit counterweight holding that a child-abuse investigation standing alone does not implicate the right to familial association, which keeps the investigation nodes from reading more favorably to families than circuit law supports.
- Added Holloway v. Brush, 220 F.3d 767 (6th Cir. 2000) (en banc) for DEC-05 and FAIL-04: it is the en banc functional framework separating absolute from qualified immunity for caseworkers, on which Pittman and Barber are both decided, and the case a practitioner must cite directly rather than through Pittman.
- Added Smith v. Williams-Ash, 520 F.3d 596 (6th Cir. 2008) for DEC-06, ACT-01 and INP-05: it is the voluntariness defense missing from the safety-plan nodes, holding that parents who never used the plan's stated revocation mechanism were entitled to no hearing. Read it against Schulkers (coercion) and Bambach (revocation).
- Added Barber v. Miller, 809 F.3d 840 (6th Cir. 2015) for INP-08, DEC-02 and FAIL-04: it is the in-school-interview decision that Schulkers expressly clarifies, and it also holds that where a juvenile court ordered the removal the deprivation is attributed to the court rather than the caseworker. NOTE: CourtListener's cluster carries both the F.3d citation and the designation 2015 FED App. 0780N, the Sixth Circuit's marker for an opinion not recommended for full-text publication; cite it as 809 F.3d 840 but be aware of that designation.
- Added Schulkers v. Kammer, 955 F.3d 520 (6th Cir. 2020) for INP-08, DEC-06, DEC-02 and ACT-01: the prior notes relied on Schulkers without carrying a verified entry for it. Retrieved and read this session: it holds prospectively that the Fourth Amendment governs a social worker's in-school interview and requires at least reasonable suspicion, while granting qualified immunity because Barber had left the law unsettled, and affirms denial of immunity on the due-process claims arising from a coerced safety plan.
- Added Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield, 490 U.S. 30 (1989) for INP-11 and OUT-03: INP-11 previously rested on a single unpublished Tennessee opinion about ICWA notice. Holyfield is the controlling federal authority on ICWA's exclusive tribal jurisdiction over reservation-domiciled Indian children and on the uniform federal meaning of 'domicile'. Because no Tennessee decision on ICWA jurisdiction, placement preferences, active efforts or transfer was located, INP-11 is governed by the federal standard and must be argued from 25 U.S.C. sec. 1911-1912 directly.
- Added State v. Crank, 468 S.W.3d 15 (Tenn. 2015) for ACT-06, INP-07, OUT-06 and DEC-03: ACT-06 (emergency medical/psychological care) and INP-07 (medical disclosure) were the thinnest Tennessee nodes. Crank is the Tennessee Supreme Court's decision on the spiritual-treatment exemption to the child abuse and neglect statute in a case where DCS took the child into custody and authorized medical treatment shortly before her death; it upholds the exemption against vagueness challenge and holds it severable.

- Missing (for a later pass): In re Adoption of Female Child (Bond v. McKenzie), 896 S.W.2d 546 (Tenn. 1995) — verified to exist on CourtListener (tenn, 1995-03-20). This is the Tennessee Supreme Court's foundational statement that in a contest between a parent and a non-parent, a parent cannot be deprived of custody absent a due-process finding of substantial harm, and only then may a court reach best interests. Both Nash-Putnam and In re Swanson quote it as the source of the rule the brief attributes to them. It belongs at DEC-04, ACT-04 and OUT-03 and its absence means the brief cites the derivative cases without the root.
- Missing (for a later pass): In re D.A.H., 142 S.W.3d 267 (Tenn. 2004) — verified to exist on CourtListener (tenn, 2004-08-27). - Missing (for a later pass): In re D.L.B., 118 S.W.3d 360 (Tenn. 2003) — verified to exist on CourtListener (tenn, 2003-10-20). - Missing (for a later pass): In re Giorgianna H., 205 S.W.3d 508 (Tenn. Ct. App. 2006) — verified to exist on CourtListener (tennctapp, 2006-03-21). It belongs at ACT-02 and ACT-03, with the caveat that In re Kaliyah S. abrogated the line of authority treating reasonable efforts as a precondition to termination.
- Missing (for a later pass): Griffin v. City of Knoxville, 821 S.W.2d 921 (Tenn. 1991) — verified to exist on CourtListener (tenn, 1991-12-02). It is the Tennessee Supreme Court's leading statement that the Public Records Act contains no judicially created privacy balancing test and that exceptions must come from the General Assembly. It is the doctrinal predecessor to Schneider and belongs at FAIL-06, PMC-05 and INP-09, which currently rest on Schneider alone for that proposition.
