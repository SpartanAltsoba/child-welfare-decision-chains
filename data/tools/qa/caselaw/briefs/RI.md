## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Curtis v. State Department for Children & Their Families**, 522 A.2d 203 (R.I. 1987)
  Holding: Directed verdicts for a school social worker, a DCF protective worker, a hospital and a physician were affirmed: the school worker was required to report under § 40-11-3, the physician's three-day hospital hold was authorized by § 40-11-5, and no false imprisonment, battery or emotional distress claim was proved.
  Why here: Curtis is the Rhode Island Supreme Court's fullest treatment of the § 40-11-3 reporting duty and of liability exposure for making a report.
  Quote: “Any physical injury apparently caused by other than accidental means is sufficient under § 40-11-5.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1983058/curtis-v-state-department-for-children-their-families/
- **In re Sophia M.**, 204 A.3d 605 (R.I. 2019)
  Holding: The abuse and neglect decree against the mother was vacated because the evidence did not clearly and convincingly show that she inflicted or allowed the infant's injuries, where she reported the bruising immediately and followed medical advice.
  Why here: The case arises from a pediatric report of unexplained infant bruising and shows how a medical referral becomes a DCYF petition.
  Quote: “We are of the opinion, therefore, that Anna's response to discovering the bruising, even if arguably dilatory, does not support a finding of abuse or neglect.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4606924/in-re-sophia-m/

## INP-02 · Voluntary / Third-Party Report
- **Curtis v. State Department for Children & Their Families**, 522 A.2d 203 (R.I. 1987)
  Holding: Directed verdicts for a school social worker, a DCF protective worker, a hospital and a physician were affirmed: the school worker was required to report under § 40-11-3, the physician's three-day hospital hold was authorized by § 40-11-5, and no false imprisonment, battery or emotional distress claim was proved.
  Why here: The Court reads § 40-11-3 as imposing the reporting duty on any person with reasonable cause to know or suspect abuse, not only on listed professionals.
  Quote: “Any physical injury apparently caused by other than accidental means is sufficient under § 40-11-5.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1983058/curtis-v-state-department-for-children-their-families/
- **Hatch v. Department for Children, Youth & Their Families**, 274 F.3d 12 (1st Cir. 2001)
  Holding: The Constitution permits a Rhode Island DCYF caseworker to take temporary custody of a child without a hearing when the caseworker has a reasonable suspicion that abuse has occurred or is imminent, and the DCYF worker here met that standard, so qualified immunity barred the claim.
  Why here: A citizen or professional report is the usual source of the reasonable suspicion that Hatch makes the constitutional trigger for protective custody.
  Quote: “It follows, therefore, that a parent's right to the care, custody, and control of a minor child is inviolate unless a case worker has such a suspicion.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/775863/richard-h-hatch-jr-v-department-for-children-youth-and-their-families/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Hatch v. Department for Children, Youth & Their Families**, 274 F.3d 12 (1st Cir. 2001)
  Holding: The Constitution permits a Rhode Island DCYF caseworker to take temporary custody of a child without a hearing when the caseworker has a reasonable suspicion that abuse has occurred or is imminent, and the DCYF worker here met that standard, so qualified immunity barred the claim.
  Why here: Hatch governs the joint police-DCYF response in Rhode Island: the caseworker's own reasonable suspicion, not police probable cause, is the operative federal standard.
  Quote: “It follows, therefore, that a parent's right to the care, custody, and control of a minor child is inviolate unless a case worker has such a suspicion.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/775863/richard-h-hatch-jr-v-department-for-children-youth-and-their-families/
- **Suboh v. District Attorney's Office**, 298 F.3d 81 (1st Cir. 2002)
  Holding: A parent's familial-integrity right includes the right to prompt post-deprivation process after an emergency separation; the state actor who prolonged the separation without any hearing was not entitled to qualified immunity at summary judgment, while the prosecutor was.
  Why here: Where law enforcement initiates or prolongs a child's separation from a parent, Suboh imposes the same prompt-hearing duty that applies to DCYF.
  Quote: “Even when there are such exigent circumstances, there must be an adequate post-deprivation hearing within a reasonable time.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/200053/suboh-v-district-attorneys-office/
- **Carter ex rel. Caleb O. v. Lindgren**, 502 F.3d 26 (1st Cir. 2007)
  Holding: Summary judgment for Rhode Island DCYF officers was affirmed because, applying Hatch, the officers had an objectively reasonable suspicion of abuse when they took the children into temporary custody, so no constitutional violation occurred.
  Why here: Carter shows how the Hatch test is applied to a Rhode Island DCYF response triggered by a criminal investigation of a parent.
  Quote: “We need not address the latter two aspects of the Hatch test because we conclude that plaintiffs have not established a constitutional violation.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/202929/carter-ex-rel-caleb-o-v-lindgren/

## INP-06 · Anonymous Tip / Hotline
- **In re Malachii O.**, 152 A.3d 1153 (R.I. 2017)
  Holding: Termination was affirmed on the abandonment ground because the father, who had the Family Court and DCYF addresses and was represented by counsel elsewhere, failed to maintain contact, so the Court did not need to reach the alternative unfitness ground.
  Why here: The case began with a hotline call describing the incident, illustrating how an anonymous or third-party report opens a DCYF file that can end in termination.
  Quote: “Because we are of the opinion that the Family Court justice correctly concluded that respondent abandoned his son, we need not address § 15-7-7(a)(2)(i) as an additional basis for termination.”

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Sam M. ex rel. Elliott v. Carcieri**, 608 F.3d 77 (1st Cir. 2010)
  Holding: The district court abused its discretion in refusing to let the proposed next friends represent children in DCYF custody and in dismissing their systemic class action; the complaint was reinstated and the next friends appointed.
  Why here: Sam M. is the procedural gateway through which systemic challenges to Rhode Island's child welfare system reach federal court.
  Quote: “We remand the case with directions to reinstate the complaint and allow Ms. Melvin, Ms. Collins, and Dr. Elliott to proceed as the children's Next Friends.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/148956/sam-m-ex-rel-elliott-v-carcieri/
- **Danny B. ex rel. Elliott v. Raimondo**, 784 F.3d 825 (1st Cir. 2015)
  Holding: In the Rhode Island foster care class action, the district court abused its discretion by completely barring the plaintiff foster children from communicating with their own lawyers before trial, and the resulting prejudice required reversal.
  Why here: Danny B. is the second generation of systemic oversight litigation over DCYF and shows how such cases are actually tried.
  Quote: “We hold, therefore, that the court below abused its discretion when it completely denied the plaintiffs access to their lawyers prior to trial.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2795464/danny-b-ex-rel-elliott-v-raimondo/
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's 'reasonable efforts' requirement, 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15), creates no right enforceable by foster children either directly or through 42 U.S.C. § 1983.
  Why here: Federal oversight of DCYF's intake and investigation performance runs through HHS plan approval and CFSR review, not through private suits, because the plan requirements are not privately enforceable.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Connor B. Ex Rel. Vigurs v. Patrick**, 774 F.3d 45 (1st Cir. 2014)
  Holding: Judgment on the record for the state child welfare defendants was affirmed because the foster children's class did not prove a substantial departure from accepted professional judgment under Youngberg, and class-wide liability is a precursor to any systemic injunctive relief.
  Why here: Connor B. shows what happens to a First Circuit systemic foster-care class action after it clears the standing and next-friend stage that Sam M. addresses, and it sets the merits standard such oversight litigation must meet.
  Quote: “Not only has class-wide liability not been shown, itself a needed precursor to any relief, but plaintiffs have also not met the requirements for injunctive relief.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2760820/connor-b-ex-rel-vigurs-v-patrick/
- **Blessing v. Freestone**, 520 U.S. 329 (1997)
  Holding: A section 1983 plaintiff must identify the particular statutory provision said to confer an individual federal right; Title IV-D's 'substantial compliance' requirement is only a systemwide yardstick for the Secretary and confers no such right, so the judgment was vacated for provision-by-provision analysis.
  Why here: Blessing explains why a systemic challenge to DCYF intake and investigation performance cannot be pleaded as a blanket right to 'substantial compliance' with the federal plan and must instead be tied to a specific rights-creating provision.
  Quote: “In order to seek redress through § 1983, however, a plaintiff must assert the violation of a federal right, not merely a violation of federal law.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/118101/blessing-v-freestone/
- **Health and Hospital Corporation of Marion Cty. v. Talevski**, 599 U.S. 166 (2023)
  Holding: Spending Clause statutes can create rights enforceable under section 1983; the Federal Nursing Home Reform Act's unnecessary-restraint and predischarge-notice provisions unambiguously confer individual rights, and the Act's own enforcement scheme does not foreclose a section 1983 suit, so the judgment for the resident was affirmed.
  Why here: Talevski is the current framework for deciding whether any federal child welfare plan requirement bearing on DCYF intake performance is privately enforceable, and it forecloses treating Suter as a categorical bar on Spending Clause claims.
  Quote: “our precedent sets a demanding bar: Statutory provisions must unambiguously confer individual federal rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9404968/health-and-hospital-corporation-of-marion-cty-v-talevski/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In re Tamika R.**, 973 A.2d 547 (R.I. 2009)
  Holding: The dependency decree was vacated and a new trial ordered because DCYF committed an Indian child to foster placement without the qualified expert witness testimony ICWA requires and without the finding that placement with the father would likely cause serious emotional or physical damage.
  Why here: Tamika R. is the leading Rhode Island Supreme Court decision applying ICWA and holding that its qualified-expert-witness requirement is not excused by a social worker's testimony.
  Quote: “In light of such disregard of ICWA requirements, we cannot say that the error was harmless.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2085120/in-re-tamika-r/
- **In re Roman A.**, No. 2017-113-Appeal. (R.I. Nov. 18, 2019)
  Holding: Termination of the non-Indian mother's rights to an Indian child was affirmed: ICWA applied, DCYF's efforts were active efforts, and the required qualified expert witness may be qualified in any relevant field where the case raises no culturally-relevant issues.
  Why here: Roman A. is the second published Rhode Island ICWA decision and it qualifies In re Tamika R., holding that the qualified-expert-witness requirement survives but that the expert need not be an expert in Indian cultural affairs in a culturally neutral case.
  Quote: “while at least one qualified expert witness is necessary under § 1912(f), that expert may be qualified in any relevant area if, as here, the case presents no culturally-relevant issues”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4692885/in-re-roman-a/
- **Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl**, 570 U.S. 637 (2013)
  Holding: Reversing, the Court held that ICWA section 1912(f) does not apply where the Indian parent never had custody, that section 1912(d) active-efforts remedial services are not required where the parent abandoned the child before birth and never had custody, and that section 1915(a) preferences do not apply where no preferred party has sought to adopt.
  Why here: This is the Supreme Court's construction of the two ICWA provisions Rhode Island litigated in In re Roman A., and it is the authority that defines when the heightened 'continued custody' and 'active efforts' protections attach to a non-custodial Indian parent.
  Quote: “As a result, § 1912(f) does not apply in cases where the Indian parent never had custody of the Indian child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/931616/adoptive-couple-v-baby-girl/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court upheld ICWA against Article I and anticommandeering challenges and dismissed the equal protection and nondelegation challenges to the placement preferences for lack of standing, leaving the statute intact.
  Why here: Brackeen confirms that ICWA remains fully operative federal law binding on Rhode Island's Family Court, which a 2026 brief must state rather than leaving the reader with only two Rhode Island ICWA decisions from 2009 and 2019.
  Quote: “the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners' challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/

## Notes
- Court structure: Rhode Island has no intermediate appellate court. The Supreme Court of Rhode Island (CourtListener court_id 'ri') is the only appellate court, and abuse/neglect, dependency, termination, adoption and guardianship matters are tried in the Family Court, whose decisions are not published or carried on CourtListener. Every state case in this brief is therefore a published opinion of the state's highest court. No unpublished memorandum decisions were used.
- All 37 cases were retrieved from the CourtListener API in this session and the full opinion text of each was read. Every quote was verified programmatically as an exact substring of the retrieved opinion text (whitespace, curly quotes, dashes and the section symbol normalized), and every quote is from the majority/for-the-Court opinion, not a syllabus, headnote, dissent or concurrence.
- Deliberate omission: Troxel v. Granville, 530 U.S. 57 (2000), is the case most often cited for the parental liberty interest, but its lead opinion is a four-Justice plurality rather than a majority, so it was excluded under the majority-only quoting rule. Stanley v. Illinois (majority) and Santosky v. Kramer (majority) supply the same propositions, and the First Circuit's Hatch opinion supplies the Rhode-Island-specific formulation.

- Section 1983 / removal: Hatch v. Department for Children, Youth & Their Families, 274 F.3d 12 (1st Cir. 2001), is the controlling circuit authority for Rhode Island and adopts a reasonable-suspicion (not probable-cause) standard for warrantless temporary custody. Suboh v. District Attorney's Office, 298 F.3d 81 (1st Cir. 2002), adds the requirement of an adequate post-deprivation hearing within a reasonable time. Carter ex rel. Caleb O. v. Lindgren, 502 F.3d 26 (1st Cir. 2007), applies Hatch to actual DCYF officers. J.R. v. Gloria, 593 F.3d 73 (1st Cir. 2010), supplies the conscience-shocking standard for harm suffered in DCYF foster care and also confirms Rhode Island sovereign and qualified immunity defenses to the parallel state negligence claims.
- Critical practice point for families and counsel: In re Madlyn B., 187 A.3d 1105 (R.I. 2018), holds that a parent who waives the Family Court Rule 15(c)(5) probable-cause hearing forfeits any Fourth/Fourteenth Amendment challenge to DCYF's seventy-two-hour hold. The constitutional objection to an emergency removal must be raised in the Family Court at that hearing or it is lost on appeal. In re Kyeshon J. applies the same raise-or-waive rule to adoption/termination grounds.
- Two Rhode Island decisions show terminations being undone on grounds the agency did not anticipate: In re Joziah B., 207 A.3d 451 (R.I. 2019) (no § 15-7-9 sworn affidavit of the absent parent's whereabouts before notice by publication -- decree vacated), and In re Izabella G., 140 A.3d 146 (R.I. 2016) (termination resting on incarceration collapsed when the underlying convictions were vacated on Fourth Amendment grounds).
- Reporter immunity caveat: R.I. Gen. Laws § 40-11-4 grants civil and criminal immunity to any person who participates in good faith in making a report. In Curtis v. State Department for Children & Their Families, 522 A.2d 203 (R.I. 1987), the Supreme Court expressly noted (footnote 4) that 'the precise scope of that immunity has not yet been determined by this court' and decided the case on the merits instead. That scope still appears to be undecided by the Rhode Island Supreme Court; Watterson v. Page, 987 F.2d 1 (1st Cir. 1993), is persuasive only, because it construes New Hampshire's reporting-immunity statute (its familial-integrity holding is binding First Circuit law).
- Systemic oversight in Rhode Island runs through next-friend class litigation (Sam M. ex rel. Elliott v. Carcieri, 608 F.3d 77 (1st Cir. 2010); Danny B. ex rel. Elliott v. Raimondo, 784 F.3d 825 (1st Cir. 2015)) rather than through private enforcement of Title IV-E, which Suter v. Artist M., 503 U.S. 347 (1992), forecloses.
