## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **C.T. v. Gammon**, 928 N.E.2d 847 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010)
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for the pediatrician, holding Indiana does not recognize a private right of action against a person who fails to report suspected child abuse or neglect.
  Why here: Establishes that the mandatory-reporting duty is enforced criminally, not through private damages suits.
  Quote: “Because Indiana does not recognize a private right of action for failure to report child abuse or neglect, we affirm the trial court's entry of summary judgment”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2078130/ct-v-gammon/
- **Gresk v. Demetris**, 96 N.E.3d 564 (Ind. 2018)
  Holding: The court reversed dismissal of a malpractice suit against a physician who reported suspected medical child abuse to DCS, holding Indiana's anti-SLAPP statute did not apply because the suit targeted her medical care rather than speech on a public issue.
  Why here: Shows that the anti-SLAPP statute is not a shield for a mandated reporter sued over the care surrounding the report.
  Quote: “a person's actions must be “in furtherance of” his or her right of petition or free speech and “in connection with a public issue.””
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4496837/paul-gresk-trustee-for-the-bankruptcy-estate-of-derek-vanwinkle-and-stacey/
- **John Doe #1 v. Indiana Department of Child Services**, 81 N.E.3d 199 (Ind. 2017)
  Holding: The court held that the statute requiring DCS to protect a child-abuse reporter's identity creates no private right of action and that the hotline operator's assurance of anonymity created no common-law duty, so the reporter's family had no remedy when DCS released his unredacted report.
  Why here: Tells mandated reporters that the statutory promise of confidentiality is not privately enforceable if the agency breaches it.
  Quote: “We denounce DCS's thoughtlessness, but find no basis for liability under either theory.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4421402/john-doe-1-v-indiana-department-of-child-services/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Siliven v. Indiana Department of Child Services**, 635 F.3d 921 (7th Cir. 2011)
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for the Indiana DCS caseworkers on qualified immunity grounds, holding that the physician's opinion that the infant's injuries were non-accidental gave them probable cause, and therefore also reasonable suspicion, to remove the child without a court order.
  Why here: Shows the joint agency and law-enforcement investigation posture that precedes an emergency removal in Indiana.
  Quote: “The pertinent inquiry, then, is whether defendants had probable cause to believe that C.S.'s physical health would be seriously endangered if he was not immediately removed from his father's custody.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/212473/siliven-v-indiana-department-of-child-services/
- **Brokaw v. Mercer County**, 235 F.3d 1000 (7th Cir. 2000)
  Holding: The court held that a child removed from his home on a false report of neglect stated Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment claims under section 1983, because a removal is reasonable only if supported by a court order, probable cause, or exigent circumstances.
  Why here: Addresses removals carried out by officials acting on a referral that turned out to be false.
  Quote: “governmental officials will not remove a child from his home without an investigation and pre-deprivation hearing resulting in a court order of removal, absent exigent circumstances”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/771526/ca-brokaw-v-mercer-county-james-brokaw-weir-brokaw/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **Indiana Department of Child Services v. LaPorte Circuit Court**, 906 N.E.2d 801 (Ind. 2009)
  Holding: The court held that expedited Appellate Rule 14.1 review is available when a juvenile court rejects a DCS placement recommendation, that the court must accept the recommendation unless it finds by a preponderance that the recommendation is unreasonable or contrary to the child's welfare and best interests, and that such a finding is reviewed for clear error.
  Why here: Defines the division of authority between the juvenile court and DCS once the court is involved in a child's case.
  Quote: “a finding by the juvenile court that DCS's recommendation is unreasonable or contrary to the child's welfare and best interests is reviewed on appeal for clear error”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7166498/indiana-department-of-child-services-v-laporte-circuit-court/
- **Indiana Department of Child Services v. A.B.**, 949 N.E.2d 1204 (Ind. 2011)
  Holding: The court upheld the juvenile court's out-of-state residential placement over the agency's objection, held the agency's insistence on an in-state placement arbitrary and capricious, and ordered DCS to pay for the placement.
  Why here: Confirms the juvenile court's authority to direct the agency's involvement and placement notwithstanding the agency's contrary position.
  Quote: “Finally, we hold that DCS shall pay for the placement of the child at Canyon State Academy.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/852132/indiana-department-of-child-services-v-ab/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In re E.K.**, 83 N.E.3d 1256 (Ind. Ct. App. 2017)
  Holding: The court reversed the CHINS finding because the parents had cooperated fully and voluntarily obtained services, so there was insufficient evidence that the court's coercive intervention was necessary, noting an informal adjustment was available instead.
  Why here: Directly credits a family's own initiative in obtaining services as a reason not to open a court case.
  Quote: “There is insufficient evidence that the coercive intervention of a court is necessary to protect E.K.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4430437/in-the-matter-of-ek-minor-child-a-child-in-need-of-services-and-jm/
- **In re S.D.**, 2 N.E.3d 1283 (Ind. 2014)
  Holding: The court reversed the CHINS adjudication, holding the evidence did not show the mother was unlikely to meet the children's needs without the coercive intervention of the court, and it declined to find the appeal moot because of the collateral consequences of a CHINS judgment.
  Why here: Confirms that a family that seeks and accepts help voluntarily is not a proper subject of a coercive CHINS case.
  Quote: “The intrusion of a CHINS judgment, then, must be reserved for families who cannot meet those needs without coercion”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2653534/in-the-matter-of-sd-alleged-to-be-a-child-in-need-of-services-jb-v/

## INP-08 · Educational Disclosure
- **Doe v. Heck**, 327 F.3d 492 (7th Cir. 2003)
  Holding: The court held that caseworkers violated the Fourth Amendment by entering a private school and interviewing a student without a warrant, consent, probable cause, or exigent circumstances, and violated the parents' right to familial relations by threatening removal, though the officials retained qualified immunity on some claims.
  Why here: The governing Seventh Circuit authority on interviewing a child at school without parental consent or a court order.
  Quote: “the amendment's prohibition against unreasonable searches and seizures protects against warrantless intrusions during civil as well as criminal investigations by the government”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/781752/doe-v-heck/
- **Michael C. v. Gresbach**, 526 F.3d 1008 (7th Cir. 2008)
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of qualified immunity, holding that a child-protection caseworker violated the children's clearly established Fourth Amendment rights by conducting under-the-clothes body searches at their private school where the principal's consent to interview did not extend to searching the children's bodies.
  Why here: The Seventh Circuit decision that actually denied immunity for a school-based child-protection search, giving INP-08 an enforceable rule rather than Doe v. Heck's immunity-protected warning alone.
  Quote: “it is a violation of a child's constitutional rights to conduct a search of a child at a private school without a warrant or probable cause, consent, or exigent circumstances”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1190279/michael-c-v-gresbach/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court held that the reasonable efforts clause of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act creates no privately enforceable right and no implied cause of action, so beneficiaries cannot sue a state agency under section 1983 to enforce it.
  Why here: Establishes that federal child-welfare plan requirements are enforced through federal administrative oversight rather than private suit.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **D.S. v. County Department of Public Welfare of St. Joseph County**, 577 N.E.2d 572 (Ind. 1991)
  Holding: The court reversed a termination of parental rights because the trial court failed to comply with the Indian Child Welfare Act on both transfer of jurisdiction and the Act's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt evidentiary standard supported by qualified expert testimony, and it rejected the 'existing Indian family' limitation.
  Why here: The controlling Indiana Supreme Court decision on when ICWA's jurisdictional and evidentiary requirements attach to a state child-welfare case.
  Quote: “the trial court did not follow the provisions of the ICWA as they relate to jurisdiction and the evidentiary standard to be applied in terminating parental rights, we must reverse”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7163477/ds-v-county-department-of-public-welfare-of-st-joseph-county/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court rejected the Article I and anticommandeering challenges to the Indian Child Welfare Act and held that the individual petitioners and Texas lacked standing to press the equal protection and nondelegation challenges to the placement preferences, leaving ICWA intact.
  Why here: Confirms that ICWA's jurisdictional, procedural, and placement requirements remain binding on Indiana state courts.
  Quote: “Courts must adhere to the placement preferences absent “good cause” to depart from them.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/

## FAIL-02 · Unlawful Removal
- **Brokaw v. Mercer County**, 235 F.3d 1000 (7th Cir. 2000)
  Holding: The court held that a child removed from his home on a false report of neglect stated Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment claims under section 1983, because a removal is reasonable only if supported by a court order, probable cause, or exigent circumstances.
  Why here: The leading circuit authority under which an unlawful removal in Indiana is actionable under section 1983.
  Quote: “governmental officials will not remove a child from his home without an investigation and pre-deprivation hearing resulting in a court order of removal, absent exigent circumstances”

  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for the Indiana DCS caseworkers on qualified immunity grounds, holding that the physician's opinion that the infant's injuries were non-accidental gave them probable cause, and therefore also reasonable suspicion, to remove the child without a court order.
  Why here: Sets out when a warrantless removal by Indiana caseworkers is, and is not, an unconstitutional seizure actionable under section 1983.
  Quote: “The pertinent inquiry, then, is whether defendants had probable cause to believe that C.S.'s physical health would be seriously endangered if he was not immediately removed from his father's custody.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/212473/siliven-v-indiana-department-of-child-services/
- **Mabes v. Thompson**, 136 F.4th 697 (7th Cir. 2025)
  Holding: The court reversed the denial of qualified immunity to the Indiana DCS workers and the consulting physician, holding that a reasonable caseworker could have believed probable cause supported removing the children after a medical child abuse assessment, which also defeated the parents' Fourteenth Amendment familial-relations claims.
  Why here: Demonstrates the practical difficulty of a section 1983 wrongful-removal claim in Indiana given qualified immunity.
  Quote: “After conducting our own review of the factual record and evaluating each defendant and claim individually, as the law of qualified immunity demands, we reverse.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10386171/erika-mabes-v-shannon-thompson/
- **Millspaugh v. County Department of Public Welfare of Wabash County**, 937 F.2d 1172 (7th Cir. 1991)
  Holding: The court held that an Indiana caseworker has absolute immunity for initiating and prosecuting child-removal proceedings and for her testimony, but only qualified immunity for physically taking custody of the children.
  Why here: Determines which Indiana caseworker conduct can actually be reached by a section 1983 damages claim for wrongful removal.
  Quote: “Absolute immunity is appropriate when the social worker presents the case to a court, which can protect parents against misuse of public position.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/564362/lois-millspaugh-and-tina-dyson-v-county-department-of-public-welfare-of/
- **Hernandez ex rel. Hernandez v. Foster**, 657 F.3d 463 (7th Cir. 2011)
  Holding: The court affirmed qualified immunity on the claims arising from the child's initial removal but vacated summary judgment on the Fourth Amendment and substantive due process claims for continuing to withhold the child after probable cause had dissipated and on the claims that the caseworker coerced the parents into a safety plan.
  Why here: The Seventh Circuit's leading decision denying qualified immunity to child-welfare workers, the necessary counterweight to Siliven, Mabes and Millspaugh in assessing a wrongful-removal claim.
  Quote: “The danger must be imminent, or put another way, the circumstances must be exigent.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/612665/hernandez-ex-rel-hernandez-v-foster/
- **Jerger v. Blaize**, 41 F.4th 910 (7th Cir. 2022)
  Holding: The court vacated summary judgment for the Indiana DCS caseworkers and remanded for trial, holding a jury must decide whether the parents' consent to a blood draw on their infant was voluntary or the product of a threatened CHINS filing and loss of the right to make her medical decisions.
  Why here: Supplies the Indiana-origin section 1983 case in which qualified immunity did NOT dispose of the claim, because the facts on coerced consent were genuinely disputed.
  Quote: “Taking these facts together, the Jergers paint a picture of coercion, not “freely and voluntarily given” consent.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7440743/lelah-jerger-v-shannon-blaize/
