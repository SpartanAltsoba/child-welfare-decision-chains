## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Ramsey v. Yavapai Family Advocacy Center**, 225 Ariz. 132, 235 P.3d 285 (App. 2010)
  Holding: The court of appeals affirmed summary judgment for the reporter and the forensic interviewers, holding that A.R.S. § 13-3620(J) confers qualified immunity conditioned on the initial reporter's reasonable belief that abuse occurred and that a counselor owes no duty to a third-party alleged abuser.
  Why here: States the exact condition — reasonable belief — on which a mandatory reporter's statutory immunity turns.
  Quote: “as the initial reporter of the alleged abuse, Sheets enjoys qualified immunity under the statute only if she "reasonably believe[d]" abuse had occurred”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2630890/ramsey-v-yavapai-family-advocacy-center/
- **L.A.R. v. Ludwig**, 170 Ariz. 24, 821 P.2d 291 (App. 1991)
  Holding: The court of appeals held that a person with reasonable grounds to report suspected child abuse has qualified statutory immunity defeasible only by proof of malice, but reversed and remanded because the immunity ruling did not dispose of the plaintiffs' separate negligent-treatment and consortium claims.
  Why here: Places the burden of proving malice on anyone suing a mandatory reporter, and confirms 'reasonable grounds' is a low threshold.
  Quote: “Malicious intent must be proved by the plaintiff to defeat the defense of qualified immunity.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1238265/lar-v-ludwig/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Calabretta v. Floyd**, 189 F.3d 808 (9th Cir. 1999)
  Holding: The Ninth Circuit affirmed the denial of qualified immunity, holding that a social worker and a police officer who coerced entry into a home and strip-searched a child during a child-abuse investigation, without a warrant, consent, or exigency, violated clearly established Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights.
  Why here: Holds that a police escort does not supply the authority a caseworker lacks to enter a home.
  Quote: “everyone knew that the government could not so enter houses, and White said that principle was well established, in the context of a child abuse investigation”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/765930/robert-calabretta-v-floyd/
- **Wallis ex rel. Wallis v. Spencer**, 202 F.3d 1126 (9th Cir. 2000)
  Holding: The Ninth Circuit reversed summary judgment for the city on the § 1983 claims, holding that absent risk of dissipating evidence or an urgent medical problem the state must notify parents and obtain judicial approval before subjecting children in protective custody to investigatory physical examinations, and that a jury must decide whether there was reasonable cause for the removal.
  Why here: Governs law enforcement's role in removing and examining children on the basis of a referral it has not corroborated.
  Quote: “The right to family association includes the right of parents to make important medical decisions for their children”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7079922/wallis-ex-rel-wallis-v-spencer/
- **In re Timothy C.**, 194 Ariz. 159, 978 P.2d 644 (App. 1998)
  Holding: The court of appeals held that a Child Protective Services caseworker who obtained a juvenile's confession was a state actor for due process purposes and that the State failed to overcome the presumption that the confession was involuntary.
  Why here: Governs CPS interviews that run parallel to or feed a criminal investigation.
  Quote: “We hold that the CPS caseworker was a state actor for purposes of a due process analysis.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1133636/in-re-timothy-c/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **In Re the Appeal in Cochise County Juvenile Action No. 5666-J**, 133 Ariz. 157, 650 P.2d 459 (1982)
  Holding: The Arizona Supreme Court vacated the court of appeals' opinion and affirmed the juvenile court's dismissal of the dependency petition, holding that Santosky's clear-and-convincing standard is confined to termination and that dependency must be proved only by a preponderance of the evidence, which the State had not done.
  Why here: The Arizona Supreme Court source of the evidentiary threshold a referral must ultimately meet once a judge sends a family into the juvenile court.
  Quote: “the weighing of the private and public interests convinces us that the preponderance of the evidence standard is the proper standard of proof in dependency proceedings”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1357609/in-re-the-appeal-in-cochise-county-juvenile-action-no-5666-j/
- **Stanley v. Illinois**, 405 U.S. 645 (1972)
  Holding: The Supreme Court reversed and remanded, holding that an unwed father was denied due process and equal protection when Illinois declared his children dependent and removed them on a presumption of unfitness without any hearing on his actual fitness as a parent.
  Why here: The federal constitutional floor when a court opens a dependency inquiry against a parent — presumed unfitness cannot substitute for an individualized hearing.
  Quote: “We have concluded that all Illinois parents are constitutionally entitled to a hearing on their fitness before their children are removed from their custody”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/108497/stanley-v-illinois/
- **Louis C. v. Department of Child Safety**, 237 Ariz. 484, 353 P.3d 364 (App. 2015)
  Holding: The court of appeals affirmed the dependency adjudication, holding that A.R.S. § 8-844(C) requires the juvenile court to adjudicate dependency on a preponderance of the evidence and that the Parents' Bill of Rights does not raise that burden.
  Why here: Sets the standard the juvenile court applies once a referral results in a dependency proceeding.
  Quote: “Section 8-844(C) not only permits a court to enter an order of dependency based on a preponderance of the evidence, but directs that a court "shall" do so.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2811466/louis-c-v-department-of-child-safety/
- **Oscar F. v. Department of Child Safety**, 235 Ariz. 266, 330 P.3d 1023 (App. 2014)
  Holding: The court of appeals affirmed the dependency adjudication, holding the juvenile court had statutory authority to find the children dependent as to one parent notwithstanding the petition's allegations or the failure to serve the other parent or guardian.
  Why here: Confirms the juvenile court's authority to proceed against one parent when a referral reaches the court.
  Quote: “The juvenile court was authorized by statute to adjudicate the children dependent as to Oscar, notwithstanding allegations in the petition or that another parent or guardian has not been served.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2689001/oscar-f-v-dcs-ef-mf-c-f-gf-and-lf/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **Lorenz v. State**, 238 Ariz. 556, 364 P.3d 475 (App. 2015)
  Holding: The court of appeals affirmed dismissal of grandparents' civil suit against DCS, holding the child-welfare statutes, rules, and policies they invoked create no civil duty running to relatives who seek placement of a dependent child.
  Why here: Establishes that a relative's request for involvement creates no enforceable civil duty on the agency.
  Quote: “Because the cited authorities reveal an intent to protect and benefit dependent children, not potential foster or adoptive placements, we affirm the superior court's judgment.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3160631/lorenz-v-state/
- **Mary Ellen C. v. Arizona Department of Economic Security**, 193 Ariz. 185, 971 P.2d 1046 (App. 1999)
  Holding: The court of appeals reversed the severance order because the State failed to make reasonable efforts to preserve the family, holding that the duty to provide rehabilitative services with a reasonable possibility of success has a constitutional basis.
  Why here: Frames the State's obligation to respond to a family that seeks or needs help with services that could actually work.
  Quote: “although the State is not obliged to undertake futile rehabilitative measures, it is obliged to undertake those which offer a reasonable possibility of success”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1420233/mary-ellen-c-v-arizona-department-of-economic-security/

  Quote: “Under any of the formulations of a public record discussed above, and based on its content, CHILDS is a public record.”

  Holding: The Arizona Supreme Court held that purely personal documents of a public officer are not public records but that the custodian's assertion is not conclusive, reversed the superior court, vacated the court of appeals' opinion, and remanded for in camera inspection.
  Why here: Supplies the presumption of disclosure that oversight bodies and the public rely on to obtain agency records.
  Quote: “Arizona law defines "public records" broadly and creates a presumption requiring the disclosure of public documents”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/867384/griffis-v-pinal-county/
- **Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community v. Rogers**, 168 Ariz. 531, 815 P.2d 900 (1991)
  Holding: The Arizona Supreme Court vacated the superior court's disclosure order, holding that a document is not a public record merely because a public officer possesses it and that it must bear some relation to the officer's official duties, and ordering the tribal check distribution list returned.
  Why here: The foundational Arizona Supreme Court definition of what counts as a public record, which fixes the outer boundary of an oversight body's or requester's reach into agency files.
  Quote: “a public record must also have some relation to the official duties of the public officer that holds the record”

  Why here: The source of the presumption of openness that lets oversight bodies and the public demand agency records without first proving entitlement.
  Quote: “all records required to be kept under A.R.S. § 39-121.01(B), are presumed open to the public for inspection as public records”

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Valerie M. v. Arizona Department of Economic Security**, 219 Ariz. 331, 198 P.3d 1203 (2009)
  Holding: The Arizona Supreme Court held that ICWA does not require Arizona's state-law termination grounds or the best-interests finding to be proved beyond a reasonable doubt; that standard under 25 U.S.C. § 1912(f) governs only the finding that continued custody is likely to cause serious emotional or physical damage.
  Why here: Controlling Arizona authority on how ICWA's heightened proof requirement interacts with state-law findings once a case is identified as involving an Indian child.
  Quote: “We do not believe that Congress intended to apply the reasonable doubt standard to state-law findings.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2512452/valerie-m-v-arizona-department-of-economic-security/
- **Steven H. v. Arizona Department of Economic Security**, 218 Ariz. 566, 190 P.3d 180 (2008)
  Holding: The Arizona Supreme Court vacated the court of appeals' opinion and remanded, holding that 25 U.S.C. § 1912(e) requires qualified expert testimony addressing the likelihood of future serious harm to the Indian child, but that the testimony need not recite the statutory language or be expressed in any particular form.
  Why here: The Arizona Supreme Court's controlling construction of ICWA's qualified-expert-witness requirement — what the expert must actually address before an Indian child may be placed in foster care.
  Quote: “ICWA requires qualified expert testimony that addresses the determination that the Indian child is at risk of future harm unless the child is removed from the parents' custody”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/867336/steven-h-v-arizona-department-of-economic-security/
- **Gila River Indian Community v. Department of Child Safety**, 242 Ariz. 277, 395 P.3d 286 (2017)
  Holding: The Arizona Supreme Court vacated the court of appeals' 2016 opinion and affirmed the denial of the tribe's transfer motion, holding that 25 U.S.C. § 1911(b) governs transfer only of foster-care-placement and termination proceedings, that it neither applies to nor prohibits transfer of preadoptive and adoptive placements, and declining to rest on waiver.
  Why here: The Arizona Supreme Court's controlling statement of when § 1911(b) transfer to tribal court is available, superseding the 2016 court of appeals decision this brief previously relied on and rejecting its implied-waiver rationale.
  Quote: “We hold that § 1911(b) addresses transfer only of foster care placement and termination-of-parental-rights actions”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4399866/gila-river-indian-community-v-department-of-child-safety/
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Supreme Court reversed and remanded, holding that 'domicile' under ICWA is a uniform federal standard, that twins born off-reservation to reservation-domiciled parents were themselves domiciled on the reservation, and that the tribal court therefore had exclusive jurisdiction under 25 U.S.C. § 1911(a) notwithstanding the mother's voluntary surrender.
  Why here: The controlling Supreme Court decision on how ICWA actually operates — it fixes exclusive tribal jurisdiction by federal-law domicile and forecloses parental workarounds, the threshold question in any Arizona case involving an Indian child.
  Quote: “Tribal jurisdiction under § 1911(a) was not meant to be defeated by the actions of individual members of the tribe”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl**, 570 U.S. 637 (2013)
  Holding: The Supreme Court reversed and remanded, holding that ICWA § 1912(f) does not apply where the parent never had custody of the child, that § 1912(d)'s remedial-services requirement applies only where termination would precipitate the breakup of an existing Indian family, and that § 1915(a)'s placement preferences do not apply when no preferred party has sought to adopt.
  Why here: The second controlling Supreme Court merits decision on ICWA's operation, marking the limits of §§ 1912(d), 1912(f) and 1915(a) once a case is identified as involving an Indian child.
  Quote: “Consistent with the statutory text, we hold that §1912(d) applies only in cases where an Indian family's "breakup" would be precipitated by the termination of the parent's rights”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/931616/adoptive-couple-v-baby-girl/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Supreme Court affirmed that Congress had constitutional authority to enact ICWA, reversed the anticommandeering holdings below, and vacated the equal protection and nondelegation rulings for want of standing, leaving ICWA's requirements and placement preferences operative in state proceedings.
  Why here: Settles that ICWA validly binds Arizona state courts once an Indian child is identified.
  Quote: “we affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals regarding Congress's constitutional authority to enact ICWA”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **Dep't of Child Safety v. Stocking-Tate**, 247 Ariz. 108, 446 P.3d 813 (App. 2019)
  Holding: The court of appeals granted special action relief and vacated the order striking the ex parte temporary custody order, holding that an ex parte removal order is not challenged by a motion to strike but is reviewed at the statutory temporary custody hearing, where DCS bears the probable-cause burden.

- **Demaree v. Pederson**, 887 F.3d 870 (9th Cir. 2018)
  Holding: In an Arizona case, the Ninth Circuit reversed summary judgment based on qualified immunity and remanded, holding that on the plaintiffs' version of the facts the social workers lacked reasonable cause to believe the children faced articulable, imminent, serious physical injury or molestation and so violated clearly established rights by removing them without a court order.
  Why here: States the exact quantum of risk required before Arizona caseworkers may take emergency custody without judicial authorization.
  Quote: “The clearly established case law requires articulable, imminent, and serious physical injury or physical abuse to children to justify removing them from their parents' home without a judicial order.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9022500/demaree-v-pederson/
- **Kirkpatrick v. County of Washoe**, 843 F.3d 784 (9th Cir. 2016) (en banc)
  Holding: The en banc Ninth Circuit held a jury could find the warrantless seizure of a newborn unconstitutional because there was time to obtain a warrant, granted the individual caseworkers qualified immunity because that obligation was not clearly established at the time, and reversed and remanded the municipal-liability claim.
  Why here: Establishes that exigency requires not only danger but insufficient time to obtain judicial authorization.
  Quote: “We hold that Kirkpatrick has raised a genuine dispute as to whether B.W. was in such imminent danger of serious bodily injury that no warrant was necessary.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4328788/jamie-kirkpatrick-v-county-of-washoe/
- **Wallis ex rel. Wallis v. Spencer**, 202 F.3d 1126 (9th Cir. 2000)
  Holding: The Ninth Circuit reversed summary judgment for the city on the § 1983 claims, holding that absent risk of dissipating evidence or an urgent medical problem the state must notify parents and obtain judicial approval before subjecting children in protective custody to investigatory physical examinations, and that a jury must decide whether there was reasonable cause for the removal.
  Why here: Confirms that protective custody does not by itself authorize intrusive examinations of the child.
  Quote: “The right to family association includes the right of parents to make important medical decisions for their children”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7079922/wallis-ex-rel-wallis-v-spencer/
- **Mabe v. San Bernardino County**, 237 F.3d 1101 (9th Cir. 2001)
  Holding: The Ninth Circuit affirmed in part and reversed in part, holding that a triable issue existed as to whether the social worker had reasonable cause to believe the child faced imminent danger of serious bodily injury justifying warrantless entry and seizure, and that probable cause supporting a warrant does not itself establish the exigency a warrantless removal requires.
  Why here: The source authority for the imminent-danger standard Arizona caseworkers must satisfy before taking protective custody without a court order, cited here directly rather than second-hand.
  Quote: “a showing of probable cause does not satisfy the conclusion that MD was in imminent danger of serious physical injury sufficient to justify a warrantless removal”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/771798/mabe-v-san-bernardino-county/
- **Rogers v. County of San Joaquin**, 487 F.3d 1288 (9th Cir. 2007)
  Holding: The Ninth Circuit reversed the grant of qualified immunity to the social worker and reversed the denial of the parents' partial summary judgment motion, holding it was clearly established that children may be removed without a warrant only in exigent circumstances and that no reasonable social worker could have found exigency on these facts of medical neglect.
  Why here: Confirms that neglect findings, even serious ones, do not supply the exigency that alone permits protective custody without judicial authorization.
  Quote: “it was clearly established that warrantless removal of children is permissible only in cases of exigency”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/797914/rogers-v-county-of-san-joaquin/

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6604368/american-civil-liberties-union-v-arizona-department-of-child-safety/
- **Griffis v. Pinal County**, 215 Ariz. 1, 156 P.3d 418 (2007)
  Holding: The Arizona Supreme Court held that purely personal documents of a public officer are not public records but that the custodian's assertion is not conclusive, reversed the superior court, vacated the court of appeals' opinion, and remanded for in camera inspection.
  Why here: Establishes that the burden of justifying withholding rests on the agency, not the requester.
  Quote: “Arizona law defines "public records" broadly and creates a presumption requiring the disclosure of public documents”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/867384/griffis-v-pinal-county/
- **Silverman v. Arizona Department of Economic Security**, No. CV-23-0181-PR (Ariz. June 3, 2024)
  Holding: The Arizona Supreme Court held that investigative journalists can qualify as persons conducting 'bona fide research' under the confidentiality statute's exception, vacated the court of appeals' opinion, and remanded for the agency to exercise its release discretion under the definition the Court supplied.
  Why here: Confirms that systematic analysis of agency records can constitute 'bona fide research' entitled to statutory access.
  Quote: “The primary issue before us is whether investigative journalists can engage in "bona fide research." We conclude they can.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9509831/amy-silverman-v-ades/
