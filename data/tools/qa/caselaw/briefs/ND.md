## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral

  Why here: Applies to joint police and social-services removals arising from a law-enforcement encounter.

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/792986/kd-a-minor-through-his-mother-michelle-deason/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **Worden v. Worden**, 434 N.W.2d 341 (N.D. 1989)
  Holding: The court reversed an award of a stepdaughter's custody to her stepfather and directed that custody be placed with her mother, holding that a court may not award custody to a third party under a best-interests test without first finding exceptional circumstances, which in every case upheld by the court had meant a psychological-parent relationship formed during the third party's actual physical custody.
  Why here: Instructs that where a parent's fitness to provide minimally adequate care is genuinely at issue, the route is a ch. 27-20 deprivation proceeding commenced for that purpose, not a fitness ruling inside a divorce custody case.
  Quote: “Absent exceptional circumstances the natural parent is entitled to custody of the child even though the third party may be able to offer more amenities.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1643981/worden-v-worden/
- **In the Interest of T.T.**, 2004 ND 138, 681 N.W.2d 779
  Holding: The court affirmed an adjudication that a ten-year-old was deprived by reason of parental alienation and psychological maltreatment, holding the finding of deprivation by clear and convincing evidence was not clearly erroneous and that the juvenile court had not improperly merged the divorce-related custody dispute with the deprivation proceeding.
  Why here: Addresses the case that enters the child-welfare system out of ongoing civil litigation: the district court deferred its visitation ruling to the deprivation proceeding, and the court held that ch. 27-20 hearings must be conducted separately from proceedings outside juvenile-court jurisdiction even though judicial notice of prior adjudications is permitted.
  Quote: “A juvenile court's finding of deprivation must be supported by clear and convincing evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/897465/in-re-tt/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In re Guardianship of Barros**, 2005 ND 122, 701 N.W.2d 402
  Holding: The court reversed the denial of a mother's motion to terminate a voluntary guardianship and remanded, holding the parent must first show by a preponderance that the impediments prompting the guardianship are gone, after which the non-parent bears a preponderance burden to overcome the parental presumption, a voluntarily created guardianship itself constituting exceptional circumstances that triggers best-interests review.
  Why here: Applies to families who voluntarily request a guardianship and later ask to have the child returned.
  Quote: “the natural parent must initially prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the impediments leading to the creation of the guardianship have been removed.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/897669/in-re-guardianship-of-barros/
- **Interest of G.L.**, 2018 ND 176, 915 N.W.2d 685
  Holding: The court reversed the order continuing a guardianship of a child and remanded, because the juvenile court applied a best-interests analysis without first expressly finding the exceptional circumstances required before a child is kept in a non-parent's custody.
  Why here: Arose from a parent's own request to the court to review the guardianship she had earlier agreed to.
  Quote: “The juvenile court's order does not expressly consider or mention exceptional circumstances.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4295331/interest-of-gl/
- **In the Matter of the Adoption of S.R.F.**, 2004 ND 150, 683 N.W.2d 913
  Holding: The court affirmed a judgment terminating a mother's parental rights for abandonment under N.D.C.C. §§ 14-15-06(1)(a) and 14-15-19(3)(a) and granting the guardians' adoption petition, holding the abandonment findings were not clearly erroneous and overruling prior cases that had applied de novo review to adoption-act appeals.
  Why here: Begins with a mother voluntarily placing her child with family friends and consenting to their guardianship, the front-end self-referral route, and traces what happens when she does not follow through.
  Quote: “A party seeking termination of the parent-child relationship in the context of an adoption proceeding must prove the elements necessary to support termination by clear and convincing evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1232354/in-the-matter-of-adoption-of-srf/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court held that the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's requirement that a state plan provide for 'reasonable efforts' to prevent removal and reunify families creates no right enforceable by beneficiaries under § 1983 or directly, leaving enforcement to the federal agency's plan-approval process.
  Why here: Identifies federal plan review as the oversight trigger when a state falls short.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Mickelson v. City of Rolla**, 2023 ND 128
  Holding: The court affirmed, holding that a requester alleging an open-records violation must give the public entity notice that describes, paraphrases, or cites the provision violated and the facts constituting the violation, and a letter merely asking the attorney general to decide whether a violation occurred did not do so.
  Why here: Open-records litigation is a principal external oversight trigger available to citizens.
  Quote: “the notice must describe, paraphrase, or cite to the statutory requirement alleged to have been violated and generally describe the facts alleged to constitute the violation”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9409727/mickelson-v-city-of-rolla/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In the Interest of A.B.**, 2003 ND 98, 663 N.W.2d 625
  Holding: The court affirmed transfer of a termination proceeding from state juvenile court to the Turtle Mountain Tribal Court under 25 U.S.C. § 1911(b), holding the county failed to carry its burden of showing good cause to deny transfer and that applying ICWA to the child was not unconstitutional.
  Why here: Governs ICWA transfer motions: absent parental objection, the state court must transfer unless the opposing party proves good cause not to.
  Quote: “The burden of establishing good cause to deny a transfer is upon the party opposing the transfer.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8244788/lsw-v-kb/
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that 'domicile' under ICWA is a matter of uniform federal law and that twins born off-reservation to parents domiciled on the reservation were themselves domiciled there, so the tribal court had exclusive jurisdiction and the state-court adoption decree had to be vacated.
  Why here: Controls the threshold jurisdictional question whenever an Indian child's domicile may place the case in tribal court under 25 U.S.C. § 1911(a).
  Quote: “We therefore think it beyond dispute that Congress intended a uniform federal law of domicile for the ICWA.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **Interest of K.S.D.**, 2017 ND 289, 904 N.W.2d 479
  Holding: Clear and convincing evidence supported the state-law grounds and the finding that active efforts had been made and failed, but because nothing in the record satisfied ICWA's requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt including qualified expert witness testimony, the court retained jurisdiction and remanded for a hearing limited to that ICWA requirement.
  Why here: States North Dakota's dual burden of proof whenever the child is an Indian child, tying state deprivation proof to ICWA's added federal proof.
  Quote: “When Indian children are involved in proceedings such as this, state and federal law create a dual burden of proof for the party seeking termination of parental rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8248639/gaddie-v-ksd/
- **Interest of A.C.**, 2022 ND 123, 975 N.W.2d 567
  Holding: Because the juvenile court's order contained inconsistent findings about whether ICWA applied and whether active efforts and the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt serious-damage element were established, the Supreme Court retained jurisdiction and remanded for clear findings before any termination could stand.
  Why here: Holds that a juvenile court must make explicit, internally consistent findings that ICWA (and N.D.C.C. ch. 27-20.3-19, North Dakota's codification) applies before proceeding on an Indian child.
  Quote: “Without consistent and clear factual findings supporting the application of ICWA and North Dakota heightened review for Indian families we are unable to determine the remaining issues raised by A.L.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6347947/interest-of-ac/
- **In the Interest of T.F.**, 2004 ND 126, 681 N.W.2d 786
  Holding: The court affirmed termination of an incarcerated father's parental rights, holding incarceration alone does not prove abandonment but prognostic evidence of a long-continuing inability to care for the children satisfied both the state clear-and-convincing standard and ICWA's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt requirement.
  Why here: Applies ICWA's dual burden where the children are tribal members, requiring proof beyond a reasonable doubt of likely serious emotional or physical damage.
  Quote: “While incarceration, by itself, does not establish abandonment of a child for purposes of terminating parental rights”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/897467/in-the-interest-of-tf-v-director-traill-county-social-services/
- **In the Interest of M.S.**, 2001 ND 68, 624 N.W.2d 678
  Holding: The court affirmed termination of a mother's rights to her Yankton Sioux daughter, holding that state and federal law impose a dual burden — the state statutory elements by clear and convincing evidence and ICWA's serious-damage element beyond a reasonable doubt — and holding for the first time that ICWA's active-efforts requirement under 25 U.S.C. § 1912(d) need be proven only by clear and convincing evidence.
  Why here: This is the source decision, relied on by both T.F. and K.S.D., that first stated North Dakota's ICWA dual burden of proof once a case involves an Indian child, and it also sets the standard for qualified expert witnesses where no cultural bias is implicated.
  Quote: “These state and federal provisions create a dual burden of proof for the party seeking parental termination of the parent of an Indian child”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/896766/in-re-ms/
- **In the Matter of the Adoption of C.D.**, 2008 ND 128, 751 N.W.2d 236
  Holding: Reversing the dismissal of a stepmother's termination petition, the court held that although an Indian tribe's determination of its own membership and eligibility is binding and conclusive on the state court, the party asserting ICWA must produce admissible evidence of that determination, and allegations in the tribe's motions plus proof of Indian heritage and a pending enrollment application did not establish that the child was an Indian child.
  Why here: Supplies the threshold question in every North Dakota ICWA case: the court must first find on admissible evidence that the child is an 'Indian child' under 25 U.S.C. § 1903(4), and the burden of producing that evidence falls on the party asserting ICWA's application.
  Quote: “An Indian tribe's determinations of its own membership and eligibility for membership are binding and conclusive in an ICWA proceeding.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/898301/in-the-matter-of-adoption-of-cd/
- **Interest of K.B.**, 2021 ND 106, 961 N.W.2d 293
  Holding: The court retained jurisdiction and remanded for detailed ICWA findings, holding that while a qualified expert witness's preference against termination does not itself bar termination, the juvenile court's vague paraphrase of the statutory language was not the specific finding, beyond a reasonable doubt, that continued parental custody was likely to result in serious emotional or physical damage that 25 U.S.C. § 1912(f) requires.
  Why here: The most current North Dakota authority on the qualified-expert-witness element of ICWA: the ultimate serious-damage finding belongs to the judge, not the expert, but the court must make it explicitly and will not have it inferred from a vague reference to the statute.
  Quote: “A qualified expert witness's expressed preference to deny termination of parental rights does not preclude the court from making findings sufficient to satisfy ICWA and ordering termination.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4894387/interest-of-kb/
- **Interest of K.B.**, 2021 ND 163
  Holding: On return from its own remand, the court affirmed the juvenile court's order denying the petition to terminate the mother's parental rights and instead removing the children from her custody for nine months, deferring to the finding that the qualified expert's testimony raised a reasonable doubt whether continued custody was likely to cause serious emotional or physical damage.
  Why here: The sequel that shows how ICWA's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard actually operates on remand: contrary expert testimony from the tribe's qualified expert can defeat the State's proof even where the court doubts the expert's assessment.
  Quote: “A finding relating to a likelihood of future harm requires some prognostic evidence providing "the basis for a reasonable prediction as to future action."”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5092497/interest-of-kb/

  Why here: Defines the constitutional interest that protective custody intrudes upon in North Dakota's federal circuit.
  Quote: “We have long recognized that parents have a liberty interest in familial relationships and have an important substantive due process right to control the care and custody of their children.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3039317/marybeth-dornheim-v-michael-sholes/
- **Stanley v. Illinois**, 405 U.S. 645 (1972)
  Holding: The Court held that an unwed father was entitled as a matter of due process to a hearing on his fitness before his children could be taken from him, and that Illinois's presumption of unfitness for unwed fathers, while affording a hearing to every other parent, denied him equal protection.
  Why here: The federal due-process predicate underlying every emergency or protective removal: the State must give the parent a hearing on fitness before permanently depriving him of his children, and cannot substitute a presumption for individualized proof.
  Quote: “We conclude that, as a matter of due process of law, Stanley was entitled to a hearing on his fitness as a parent before his children were taken from him”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/108497/stanley-v-illinois/

  Holding: The Court held that the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's requirement that a state plan provide for 'reasonable efforts' to prevent removal and reunify families creates no right enforceable by beneficiaries under § 1983 or directly, leaving enforcement to the federal agency's plan-approval process.
  Why here: Supports the role of federally required review bodies as the compliance check on state performance.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Mickelson v. City of Rolla**, 2023 ND 128
  Holding: The court affirmed, holding that a requester alleging an open-records violation must give the public entity notice that describes, paraphrases, or cites the provision violated and the facts constituting the violation, and a letter merely asking the attorney general to decide whether a violation occurred did not do so.
  Why here: Records requests of this kind are the practical tool for testing whether review bodies are operating and reporting.
  Quote: “the notice must describe, paraphrase, or cite to the statutory requirement alleged to have been violated and generally describe the facts alleged to constitute the violation”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9409727/mickelson-v-city-of-rolla/

## PMC-04 · Fatality Review Commission
- **James v. Friend**, 458 F.3d 726 (8th Cir. 2006)
  Holding: After a child died in foster care, the court affirmed summary judgment for the caseworkers, holding that although foster placement creates a custodial relationship, liability requires conscience-shocking conduct — deliberate indifference where deliberation is practical — which the record did not establish.
  Why here: Defines the legal fault standard against which fatality-review findings are measured in litigation.
  Quote: “a substantive due process violation will be found to have occurred only if the official conduct or inaction is so egregious or outrageous that it is conscience-shocking”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/795306/sidney-james-v-alysha-friend/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause imposed no constitutional duty on county caseworkers to protect a child from his father's violence where the State had not taken the child into its custody, so their failure to act was not actionable under § 1983.
  Why here: Explains why fatality review, rather than constitutional litigation, is usually the accountability route for deaths of children known to CPS.
  Quote: “nothing in the language of the Due Process Clause itself requires the State to protect the life, liberty, and property of its citizens against invasion by private actors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-cty-dss/
- **Mickelson v. City of Rolla**, 2023 ND 128
  Holding: The court affirmed, holding that a requester alleging an open-records violation must give the public entity notice that describes, paraphrases, or cites the provision violated and the facts constituting the violation, and a letter merely asking the attorney general to decide whether a violation occurred did not do so.
  Why here: Applies to requests for fatality-review material held by public entities.
  Quote: “the notice must describe, paraphrase, or cite to the statutory requirement alleged to have been violated and generally describe the facts alleged to constitute the violation”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9409727/mickelson-v-city-of-rolla/

## PMC-05 · FOIA / Transparency

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9409727/mickelson-v-city-of-rolla/
- **Raboin v. North Dakota Department of Human Services**, 552 N.W.2d 329 (N.D. 1996)
  Holding: The court held that a Department determination of probable cause to believe child abuse is indicated affects legal rights and is an appealable final order, and that on this record the determination was not supported by a preponderance of the evidence, reversing and remanding with directions to vacate the probable-cause finding.
  Why here: Notes that child-abuse reports filed with the court and index are made confidential and exempt from public access under the open records law.
  Quote: “We conclude there is a statutory right of appeal to the courts from the Department's finding of probable cause to believe child abuse or neglect is indicated.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1840875/raboin-v-north-dakota-department-of-human-services/
