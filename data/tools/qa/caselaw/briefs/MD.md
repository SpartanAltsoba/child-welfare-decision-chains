## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Horridge v. St. Mary's County Department of Social Services**, 382 Md. 170, 854 A.2d 1232 (2004)
  Holding: Reversing dismissal of a wrongful-death complaint, the Court held that FL § 5-706 and its regulations create a mandatory duty owed to the identified child named in an abuse report, so the public duty doctrine does not bar a negligence action against the department and its caseworkers.
  Why here: It establishes that the statutory response duty triggered by a mandated report runs to the specific child named, not merely to the public.
  Quote: “This is not an obligation that runs to everyone in general and no one in particular. It runs to an identified or identifiable child or discrete group of children.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2326632/horridge-v-st-marys-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Freed v. Worcester County Department of Social Services**, 69 Md. App. 447, 518 A.2d 159 (1986)
  Holding: The court held that parents investigated for neglect have no right to compel the department to disclose the identity of the person who reported them, rejecting due process and equal protection challenges to the confidentiality and good-faith immunity scheme of FL §§ 5-707 and 5-708.
  Why here: It explains the good-faith immunity in FL § 5-708 that underwrites mandated and permissive reporting alike.
  Quote: “no one has the right to obtain the name of the reporter from Social Services for the purpose of initiating litigation under 42 U.S.C. § 1983”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2361472/freed-v-worcester-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Wells v. State**, 100 Md. App. 693, 642 A.2d 879 (1994)
  Holding: In a suit by an abused child against the State and social workers, the court affirmed dismissal, holding that state personnel retained statutory immunity absent malice or gross negligence, that the tort claim failed for lack of the required notice, and that violation of state-mandated investigative procedures is not itself a federal due process deprivation.
  Why here: It recites the statutory purpose of mandated reporting, immunity, and prompt investigation that frames agency duties.
  Quote: “The violation of State-mandated procedures may well give rise to an action under State law, if the appropriate preconditions to such an action are followed”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2009429/wells-v-state/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In re Nicole B.**, 410 Md. 33, 976 A.2d 1039 (2009)
  Holding: The Court held that ICWA's 'active efforts' requirement is satisfied by substantive proof that the department made, and exhausted, remedial and rehabilitative efforts, and that the juvenile court need not recite the statute's wording; it reversed the intermediate court and directed that the circuit court judgment be affirmed.
  Why here: This is Maryland's only high-court decision applying the Indian Child Welfare Act in a CINA case and it defines what triggers and satisfies the federal standard.
  Quote: “what is important is whether the Department in substance made active efforts to prevent the breakup of the family, whether those efforts were unsuccessful”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2068663/in-re-nicole-b/
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Court reversed a Mississippi adoption decree, holding that 'domicile' under ICWA is a matter of uniform federal law, that children born off-reservation to reservation-domiciled parents were themselves domiciled on the reservation, and that the tribal court therefore had exclusive jurisdiction under 25 U.S.C. § 1911(a).
  Why here: It is the controlling federal decision on when an Indian child's case belongs exclusively to a tribal court, the jurisdictional question Maryland's sole ICWA decision leaves undecided.
  Quote: “In enacting the ICWA Congress confirmed that, in child custody proceedings involving Indian children domiciled on the reservation, tribal jurisdiction was exclusive as to the States.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **Weller v. Department of Social Services for the City of Baltimore**, 901 F.2d 387 (4th Cir. 1990)
  Holding: The court affirmed dismissal of most of a father's § 1983 claims against the Baltimore City department but held that his allegation that the State transferred custody of his son on an emergency basis and then denied him any hearing stated a procedural due process claim that could not be dismissed.
  Why here: It defines the constitutional floor for protective custody taken before judicial authorization.
  Quote: “Due process does not mandate a prior hearing in cases where emergency action may be needed to protect a child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/540031/weller-v-department-of-social-services-for-the-city-of-baltimore/
- **Martin v. Saint Mary's Department of Social Services**, 346 F.3d 502 (4th Cir. 2003)
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for Maryland caseworkers on qualified immunity, holding that the record showed at most negligent miscommunication causing an out-of-state removal, and that the mother's due process rights were not violated where a post-deprivation hearing occurred and the children were returned within 24 hours.
  Why here: It confirms the statutory basis in FL § 5-709(c) for emergency protective custody and the process that must follow.
  Quote: “Because a § 1983 claim cannot be sustained, it follows that the defendants are entitled to qualified immunity.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2967846/martin-v-saint-marys-department-of-social-services/
- **Wildauer v. Frederick County**, 993 F.2d 369 (4th Cir. 1993)
  Holding: The court affirmed judgment for Maryland officials, holding that investigative home visits by social workers are not measured by criminal-search standards and that the entry of the caseworker and accompanying deputies into a consenting foster mother's home was not unreasonable.
  Why here: It supplies the search-and-seizure framework governing protective action taken at the home.
  Quote: “investigative home visits by social workers are not subject to the same scrutiny as searches in the criminal context”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/606677/wildauer-v-frederick-county/
- **Jordan v. Jackson**, 15 F.3d 333 (4th Cir. 1994)
  Holding: The court affirmed rejection of facial and as-applied challenges to Virginia's 72-hour emergency-removal statute, holding that a 65-hour weekend delay in judicial review of a protective removal did not violate due process, while reversing dismissal of the parents' § 1983 claim against the county and its social services department arising from the removal itself.
  Why here: It fixes how long protective custody may last before judicial review without offending due process in this circuit.
  Quote: “we conclude that section 63.1-248.9, as it was applied in this case so as to permit such a delay, does not violate the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/661725/jordan-v-jackson/
- **In re Joseph N.**, 407 Md. 278, 965 A.2d 59 (2009)
  Holding: Reversing the Court of Special Appeals, the Court held that a mother could maintain an interlocutory appeal from the order awarding temporary custody of her CINA child to the father under departmental supervision, and that subsequent review-hearing orders that changed nothing did not render the appeal moot.
  Why here: It traces the Maryland sequence from emergency shelter care through CINA adjudication to custody orders, and secures review of the order that follows emergency action.
  Quote: “We shall hold that Petitioner had a right to an interlocutory appeal and that the appeal has not been rendered moot by orders issued at subsequent review hearings.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2370680/in-re-joseph-n/

## DEC-01 · Screen-Out / Unfounded at Intake
- **Montgomery County Department of Health & Human Services v. P.F.**, 137 Md. App. 243, 768 A.2d 112 (2001)
  Holding: The court affirmed an order requiring the agency to reclassify a sexual-abuse finding as 'ruled out' and to expunge its records and central registry entry, where the ALJ found no credible evidence that abuse occurred after a deficient investigation.
  Why here: It is the leading Maryland case on the 'ruled out' disposition and the expungement it compels.
  Quote: “MCHHS must comply with the order to reclassify the case as "ruled out," and expunge its records and registry.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1975443/montgomery-county-department-of-health-human-services-v-pf/
- **Department of Human Resources v. Cosby**, 200 Md. App. 54, 24 A.3d 199 (2011)
  Holding: The court held that collateral estoppel may be raised and applied in an FL § 5-706.1 appeal hearing, so an ALJ properly dismissed the appeal of an indicated finding already litigated in the CINA case.
  Why here: It sets out the § 5-706.1 notice and hearing scheme that follows indicated and unsubstantiated dispositions alike.
  Quote: “we hold that the doctrine of collateral estoppel may be raised and applied in a F.L. § 5-706.1 hearing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2317815/department-of-human-resources-v-cosby/
- **Hodge ex rel. Hodge v. Jones**, 31 F.3d 157 (4th Cir. 1994)
  Holding: The court held that Maryland's confidential retention of an 'unsubstantiated' and 'ruled out' child-abuse investigation file did not violate the family's constitutional privacy, familial or due process rights, because there was no cognizable stigma and no loss of a tangible interest.
  Why here: It governs what the agency may keep on file after a report is ruled out or unsubstantiated.
  Quote: “a conclusory allegation of reputational injury which, absent a cognizable stigma and the ensuing loss of a tangible interest, fails to state a cause of action under Sec. 1983”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/675711/hodge-v-jones/
- **McClanahan v. Washington County Department of Social Services**, 445 Md. 691, 129 A.3d 293 (2015)
  Holding: Reversing the Court of Special Appeals and remanding to the ALJ, the Court held that a person may be placed on the DHR central registry as responsible for child abuse — including abuse by mental injury — only if the person intended to injure the child or acted in reckless disregard of the child's welfare.
  Why here: It means that conduct that was neither intended to injure nor reckless cannot support an indicated finding, so such reports must end in an unsubstantiated or ruled-out disposition.
  Quote: “inclusion on the central registry as a child abuser is not permitted unless Mother intended to harm R or acted in reckless disregard of R's welfare”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3164662/mcclanahan-v-washington-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Taylor v. Harford County Department of Social Services**, 384 Md. 213, 862 A.2d 1026 (2004)
  Holding: The Court vacated an ALJ's finding of 'indicated' child physical abuse, holding that the ALJ erred by using tort foreseeability to infer intent and that an intentional act causing unintended injury supports an indicated finding only if the act was reckless or deliberately intended to harm the child.
  Why here: It construes the COMAR 'ruled out' criterion excluding injury that was accidental or unintentional and not reckless or deliberate.
  Quote: “We hold that, under the circumstances here present, the intentional act must be shown to have been either reckless in its nature or deliberately intended to harm the child”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2351525/taylor-v-harford-county-department-of-social-services/
