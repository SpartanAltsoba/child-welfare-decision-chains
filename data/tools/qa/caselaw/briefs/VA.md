## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Norfolk Department of Human Services v. Goldberg**, No. 1382-23-1 (Va. Ct. App. Aug. 20, 2024)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals of Virginia affirmed an order releasing CPS investigation records, holding that the bad faith disclosure exception in Code § 63.2-1514(D) reaches reports conveyed to the Department by an innocent mandatory reporter when the underlying allegation originated with someone acting maliciously or in bad faith.
  Why here: This is the controlling Virginia case on what happens when a mandatory reporter innocently relays an allegation that originated with a bad-faith accuser.
  Quote: “Consistent with the statute’s purpose, we find that Code § 63.2-1514(D) covers bad faith disclosures made to mandatory reporters.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10512685/norfolk-department-of-human-services-v-joshua-goldberg/
- **Carter v. Gordon**, 28 Va. App. 133, 502 S.E.2d 697 (1998)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals of Virginia reversed the circuit court and reinstated the Department's findings that an elementary school physical education teacher sexually abused students, holding that the trial court improperly substituted its own factfinding for the agency's and that Gordon suffered no deprivation cognizable under the Due Process Clause.
  Why here: School employees who observed and relayed the students' accounts are the mandated reporters whose reports drove this investigation.
  Quote: “Under the facts of this case, we hold the trial court improperly rejected the agency's findings and substituted its own.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1066741/carter-v-gordon/

## INP-02 · Voluntary / Third-Party Report
- **Norfolk Department of Human Services v. Goldberg**, No. 1382-23-1 (Va. Ct. App. Aug. 20, 2024)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals of Virginia affirmed an order releasing CPS investigation records, holding that the bad faith disclosure exception in Code § 63.2-1514(D) reaches reports conveyed to the Department by an innocent mandatory reporter when the underlying allegation originated with someone acting maliciously or in bad faith.
  Why here: It construes the statutory remedy available to the subject of an unfounded third-party report made maliciously.
  Quote: “Consistent with the statute’s purpose, we find that Code § 63.2-1514(D) covers bad faith disclosures made to mandatory reporters.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10512685/norfolk-department-of-human-services-v-joshua-goldberg/
- **Jackson v. W.**, 14 Va. App. 391, 419 S.E.2d 385 (1992)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals of Virginia reversed, upholding the Department's child protective services guidelines and holding that a 'founded' disposition that only threatens reputation and speculative future consequences does not deprive the alleged abuser of a liberty or property interest requiring additional procedural safeguards.
  Why here: A complaint from any source triggers the same three-stage investigate-conference-hearing process the Court validated here.
  Quote: “However, a person’s reputation alone is not a liberty or property interest and thus is not entitled to due process protection.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1338732/jackson-v-w/
- **Hodge v. Jones**, 31 F.3d 157 (4th Cir. 1994)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit reversed, holding that a state agency's continued retention of child abuse investigation records after a report is ruled out or unsubstantiated violates neither substantive nor procedural due process, because retention serves legitimate child welfare interests and confidentiality provisions prevent any cognizable stigma.
  Why here: It addresses the consequences for a family of a third-party report that was investigated and then ruled out.
  Quote: “The record before us presents no substantive due process violation of a familial privacy liberty interest by virtue of the retention of the Hodge investigation report.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/675711/hodge-v-jones/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Jones v. West**, 46 Va. App. 309, 616 S.E.2d 790 (2005)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals of Virginia affirmed dismissal of a founded sexual abuse complaint, holding that the local department's failure to tape record the child victim's interview, absent any specific determination that recording was impractical or inappropriate under 22 VAC 40-705-80(B)(1), violated required procedure and was not harmless error.
  Why here: The interview failure arose from the department's blanket deference to the police department's practice in a joint law enforcement investigation, which the Court held could not substitute for its own regulatory judgment.
  Quote: “It was essential, therefore, for the hearing officer to have access to the precise substance of S.J.’s statements.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1063431/jones-v-west/
- **Wildauer v. Frederick County**, 993 F.2d 369 (4th Cir. 1993)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the county, holding that a social worker's consented-to investigative home visit accompanied by deputies did not violate the Fourth Amendment because such visits are not measured by criminal search standards and consent to the caseworker's entry extended to the accompanying officials.
  Why here: The caseworker was accompanied by sheriff's deputies, and the Court held their presence did not convert the consented entry into an unreasonable search.
  Quote: “Furthermore, investigative home visits by social workers are not subject to the same scrutiny as searches in the criminal context.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/606677/wildauer-v-frederick-county/
- **Jordan v. Jackson**, 15 F.3d 333 (4th Cir. 1994)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit held that Virginia's emergency removal scheme, which permits a child to be taken into protective custody before judicial review provided an ex parte or adversarial removal order is obtained as soon as possible and in no event later than seventy-two hours, is constitutional facially and as applied.
  Why here: Removal in this case followed a police and hospital referral, and the opinion describes the parallel law enforcement custody authority under Code § 16.1-246.
  Quote: “at least ex parte judicial review to ratify the initial removal decision must be obtained “as soon as possible,” but in any event within seventy-two hours”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/661725/jordan-v-jackson/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **Copeland v. Todd**, 282 Va. 183, 715 S.E.2d 11 (2011)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Virginia reinstated the adoption decree, holding that Virginia's adoption statutes satisfy Fourteenth Amendment due process because the Code § 63.2-1205 factors require consideration of parental fitness and the effect on the child, so courts may not graft an additional judicially created 'detriment to the child' finding onto the statute.
  Why here: The circuit court acted on a court-ordered social services investigation and report, which is how a contested adoption brings the agency into a private proceeding.
  Quote: “Rather, for these statutes to pass constitutional due process scrutiny, they must provide for consideration of parental fitness and detriment to the child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1058003/copeland-v-todd/
- **Bonanno v. Quinn**, No. 200963 (Va. May 27, 2021)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Virginia affirmed dismissal of a grandmother's appeal from an adoption order, holding that the phrase 'aggrieved party' in Code § 17.1-405 confers appellate standing only on those actually joined as litigants below, so a non-party who never moved to intervene cannot appeal.
  Why here: The case turns on a court-ordered social worker investigation in a sealed adoption proceeding and on what notice that investigation gave an interested relative.
  Quote: “confers standing to bring an appeal to the Court of Appeals only on those who were litigants joined in the proceeding from which the judgment appealed from was taken”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4690583/bonanno-v-quinn/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **Weaver v. Roanoke Department of Human Resources**, 220 Va. 921, 265 S.E.2d 692 (1980)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Virginia reversed the termination of both parents' residual parental rights because the record contained no evidence that social agencies made the 'reasonable and appropriate efforts' that Code § 16.1-283(C)(2) requires before rights may be severed, and directed that termination statutes be read to preserve the parent-child relationship where possible.
  Why here: Virginia's foundational termination case arises from children placed in foster care partly at the parents' own request, and holds that the agency, not the family, bears the burden of showing services were offered.
  Quote: “Statutes terminating the legal relationship between parent and child should be interpreted consistently with the governmental objective of preserving, when possible, the parent-child relationship.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1246307/weaver-v-roanoke-department-of-human-resources/
- **Toombs v. Lynchburg Division of Social Services**, 223 Va. 225, 288 S.E.2d 405 (1982)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Virginia affirmed termination under Code § 16.1-283(C)(2), holding that whether parental visitation must continue after a termination petition is filed is decided case by case with the child's best interests as the guiding principle, and that clear and convincing evidence supported the statutory factors here.
  Why here: The children entered foster care through the parents' own approach to the agency, and the Court still required the Division to prove the statutory grounds by clear and convincing evidence.
  Quote: “The question must be resolved, however, in light of the facts of each case, with the best interests of the child as the guiding principle.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6823914/toombs-v-lynchburg-division-of-social-services/
- **Harris v. Lynchburg Division of Social Services**, 223 Va. 235, 288 S.E.2d 410 (1982)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Virginia affirmed termination as to the mother but reversed as to the father, holding that undisputed evidence that a parent was never offered or provided services overcomes the statutory prima facie case and puts the agency to full proof of the § 16.1-283(C)(2) factors.
  Why here: A parent who never asked for and was never offered help cannot be terminated on a prima facie showing alone.
  Quote: “where, as here, there is undisputed evidence that a parent has not been offered or provided services, the prima facie case is overcome”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6823915/harris-v-lynchburg-division-of-social-services/
- **Rocka v. Roanoke County Department of Public Welfare**, 215 Va. 515, 211 S.E.2d 76 (1975)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Virginia reversed an order granting a welfare board permanent custody with the right to place for adoption, holding that in a contest between a parent and a non-parent the rule of comparative best interests does not apply and the board must prove parental unfitness by clear and convincing evidence, and directing an interlocutory award of custody to the mother so she could demonstrate her present fitness.
  Why here: The mother herself asked the Board to take her children and place them in foster care until she could find work and a home, and the Court held that request could not be converted into permanent severance without proof of unfitness.
  Quote: “We believe that the evidence of parental unfitness in this record is less than clear and convincing and, thus, insufficient to divest a parent of custody of its child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1323663/rocka-v-roanoke-county-department-of-public-welfare/
- **Watson v. Shepard**, 217 Va. 538, 229 S.E.2d 897 (1976)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Virginia affirmed the denial of a mother's petition to regain custody, holding that because a prior juvenile court order entered after her voluntary relinquishment, an investigation and a hearing had altered her custody rights she bore the burden of showing changed circumstances, but reversed the interlocutory adoption decree because the prospective adoptive parents were separating.
  Why here: The mother's own voluntary written relinquishment of custody, acted on after a thorough investigation and a hearing, is what shifted the burden against her when she later asked for her child back.
  Quote: “the burden was not upon the Shepards, as the mother argues, to show parental unfitness”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1400294/watson-v-shepard/

## INP-07 · Medical Disclosure
- **Dawn Farrell v. Warren County Department of Social Services**, 59 Va. App. 342, 719 S.E.2d 313 (2012)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals of Virginia affirmed termination of the mother's rights to all three children under Code § 16.1-283(B), holding that the statutory definitions of an abused or neglected child do not require proof of actual harm to each child and that abuse of a sibling plus an unsafe home environment supports the finding as to the others.
  Why here: The case originated in a hospital's findings of non-accidental trauma to an infant, the paradigm medical disclosure that opens a Virginia CPS case.
  Quote: “Thus, we hold that the evidence was sufficient to support the trial court’s determination that E. and W. were abused or neglected.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1061784/dawn-farrell-v-warren-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Christopher Farrell v. Warren County Department of Social Services**, 59 Va. App. 375, 719 S.E.2d 329 (2012)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals of Virginia affirmed termination and rejected the father's due process challenge, holding that Code § 16.1-283(B) is constitutional because the Department must prove every element supporting termination by clear and convincing evidence even though the underlying abuse or neglect adjudication uses a preponderance standard.
  Why here: The medical evidence of the infant's injuries drove the adjudication, and the father's challenge to the standard of proof applied to it was rejected.
  Quote: “In the context of terminating a parent’s rights, the Due Process Clause requires that the Department prove each of the necessary allegations of parental unfitness by clear and convincing evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1061790/christopher-farrell-v-warren-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Adam Yafi v. Stafford Department of Social Services**, 69 Va. App. 539, 820 S.E.2d 884 (2018)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals of Virginia affirmed termination under Code § 16.1-283(E), holding that where a parent is convicted of felony child abuse causing serious bodily injury to a sibling or subjects a child to aggravated circumstances, the Department need not prove it offered reasonable and appropriate rehabilitative services.
  Why here: The case arose from a half-sibling's hospitalization for serious bodily injury, the medical disclosure pathway into a Virginia abuse case.
  Quote: “the termination of Yafi’s residual parental rights under Code § 16.1-283(E)(iii) and 16.1-283(E)(iv) did not require a predicate showing that DSS provided any services to Yafi”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4344682/adam-yafi-v-stafford-department-of-social-services/
- **Jenkins v. Winchester Department of Social Services**, 12 Va. App. 1178, 409 S.E.2d 16 (1991)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals of Virginia affirmed both an abuse-and-neglect finding as to a newborn removed days after birth and the termination of the mother's rights to an older child, holding that the statutory definitions require no proof of harm already suffered because 'substantial risk' speaks prospectively and Code § 16.1-251(A)(1) contemplates removal before the child enters the dangerous environment.
  Why here: The Department filed its petition the day after the child's birth at the Winchester Medical Center based on its prior experience with the mother, the paradigm hospital-based opening of a Virginia case on a newborn.
  Quote: “We agree with the Department that the statutory definitions of an abused or neglected child do not require proof of actual harm or impairment having been experienced by the child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1409371/jenkins-v-winchester-department-of-social-services/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger

  Why here: The Court upheld the Commissioner's authority to adopt the child protective services guidelines against a vagueness and ultra vires challenge, which is the framework any oversight review must apply.

- **Department of Corrections v. Surovell**, 290 Va. 255, 776 S.E.2d 579 (2015)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Virginia held that VFOIA must be liberally construed toward disclosure and its exemptions narrowly construed, with the public body bearing the burden to establish an exemption by a preponderance of the evidence, though it found the security exemption satisfied on the record before it.
  Why here: Systemic oversight of a Virginia agency begins with records access, and this case sets the presumption and burden that govern every VFOIA request to a public body.
  Quote: “Disclosure exemptions must be “narrowly construed” in favor of disclosure.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2959683/department-of-corrections-v-surovell/
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held that the 'reasonable efforts' clause of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act, 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15), creates no federal right enforceable by private individuals under § 1983 or directly under the Act.
  Why here: Because private enforcement is unavailable, federal review and audit are the operative checks on a state's reasonable-efforts performance.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Supreme Court rejected all challenges to the Indian Child Welfare Act, upholding Congress's authority to enact it and its application in state court proceedings on the merits, and dismissing the equal protection and remaining placement-preference challenges for lack of standing.
  Why here: This is the controlling authority that ICWA validly binds Virginia state courts whenever the child is an Indian child, including its placement preferences and evidentiary standards.
  Quote: “the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners’ challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9402136/haaland-v-brackeen/
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Supreme Court reversed, holding that 'domicile' in ICWA § 1911(a) is defined by uniform federal law, that children born off the reservation take the reservation domicile of their parents, and that the tribal court therefore had exclusive jurisdiction so the state adoption decree had to be vacated under 25 U.S.C. § 1914.
  Why here: This is the controlling federal authority on when ICWA strips a state court of jurisdiction altogether, and it holds that parents cannot defeat exclusive tribal jurisdiction by arranging an off-reservation birth and adoption.
  Quote: “We therefore think it beyond dispute that Congress intended a uniform federal law of domicile for the ICWA.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **Jordan v. Jackson**, 15 F.3d 333 (4th Cir. 1994)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit held that Virginia's emergency removal scheme, which permits a child to be taken into protective custody before judicial review provided an ex parte or adversarial removal order is obtained as soon as possible and in no event later than seventy-two hours, is constitutional facially and as applied.
  Why here: This is the controlling federal authority upholding Virginia's protective custody statute and fixing the constitutional outer limit on unreviewed state custody.
  Quote: “at least ex parte judicial review to ratify the initial removal decision must be obtained “as soon as possible,” but in any event within seventy-two hours”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/661725/jordan-v-jackson/
- **Lynchburg Division of Social Services v. Cook**, 276 Va. 465, 666 S.E.2d 361 (2008)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Virginia reversed the Court of Appeals and held that once the foster care statutory process has begun, a court may not transfer custody of the child to a relative other than the prior family without making, and stating in the order, the four specific findings required by Code §§ 16.1-278.2(A1), 16.1-281(C1), 16.1-282(D1) and 16.1-282.1(A1).
  Why here: It recites the § 16.1-251(A) emergency-removal showing: imminent threat of severe or irremediable injury, reasonable efforts to prevent removal, and no less drastic alternative.
  Quote: “Those findings, under the plain terms of the statutes, must be stated in the order transferring custody.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1058454/lynchburg-div-of-social-services-v-cook/
- **Dawn Farrell v. Warren County Department of Social Services**, 59 Va. App. 342, 719 S.E.2d 313 (2012)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals of Virginia affirmed termination of the mother's rights to all three children under Code § 16.1-283(B), holding that the statutory definitions of an abused or neglected child do not require proof of actual harm to each child and that abuse of a sibling plus an unsafe home environment supports the finding as to the others.
  Why here: It recites the Code § 16.1-251(A) emergency custody showing and holds that 'substantial risk' speaks prospectively, permitting removal before harm occurs.
  Quote: “Thus, we hold that the evidence was sufficient to support the trial court’s determination that E. and W. were abused or neglected.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1061784/dawn-farrell-v-warren-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Weller v. Department of Social Services for the City of Baltimore**, 901 F.2d 387 (4th Cir. 1990)
  Holding: The Fourth Circuit held that a father stated a procedural due process claim where the state deprived him of custody of his child for four months with no judicial action ratifying the deprivation, and that the domestic relations exception did not bar his § 1983 damages claim.
  Why here: It fixes the outer bound of lawful protective custody: emergency action must be followed promptly by judicial ratification.
  Quote: “an utter lack of judicial action ratifying the deprivation of custody for four months is clearly not the prompt due process that the Constitution requires”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/540031/weller-v-department-of-social-services-for-the-city-of-baltimore/
- **Jenkins v. Winchester Department of Social Services**, 12 Va. App. 1178, 409 S.E.2d 16 (1991)

  Why here: The newborn was taken under an emergency removal order entered two days after birth, and the Court held Code § 16.1-251(A)(1) authorizes exactly that pre-harm protective custody.
  Quote: “We agree with the Department that the statutory definitions of an abused or neglected child do not require proof of actual harm or impairment having been experienced by the child.”

- **Commissioner, Virginia Department of Social Services v. Fulton**, 55 Va. App. 69, 683 S.E.2d 837 (2009)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals of Virginia reversed a circuit court that had converted a founded disposition to unfounded and purged the registry, holding that under the Administrative Process Act the reviewing court may reject agency factfinding only if a reasonable mind would necessarily reach a different conclusion, and may not reweigh witness credibility.
