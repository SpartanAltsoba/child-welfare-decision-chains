# KY — 47 authorities, verified 2026-09-20

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Commonwealth v. Allen**, 980 S.W.2d 278 (Ky. 1998)
  Holding: The Court held that KRS 620.030(1) imposes an individual, non-delegable duty to report suspected child abuse directly to law enforcement, the Cabinet, or a prosecutor, so a teacher's and counselor's report to their school principal neither discharged that duty nor entitled them to KRS 620.050 immunity from misdemeanor prosecution.
  Why here: This is the controlling Kentucky Supreme Court construction of the mandatory-reporter duty in KRS 620.030(1) and confirms that the duty is individual and cannot be satisfied by telling a supervisor.
  Quote: “All individuals with firsthand knowledge or reasonable cause to believe that a child is abused have a mandatory duty to report the abuse.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1620232/commonwealth-v-allen/
- **Norton Hospitals, Inc. v. Peyton**, 381 S.W.3d 286 (Ky. 2012)
  Holding: The Court held that KRS 620.050(1) grants immunity in either of two independent situations - acting upon reasonable cause in making a report, or acting under KRS 620.030-620.050 in good faith - so a reporter's subjective good-faith belief that she was discharging the reporting duty confers immunity even if that belief was mistaken, and it reinstated summary judgment for the hospital and physician.
  Why here: This is Kentucky's leading statement of reporter immunity and sets the good-faith standard that protects mandated reporters who report in error.
  Quote: “a reporter may be "acting under KRS 620.030 to 620.050 in good faith" if the reporter subjectively believed he or she was discharging the duty imposed by KRS 620.030.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5283140/norton-hospitals-inc-v-peyton/
- **A.A. ex rel. Lewis v. Shutts**, 516 S.W.3d 343 (Ky. Ct. App. 2017)
  Holding: The court held that KRS 620.050(1) immunity is available only to a person who acts by making a report, so a physician who did not report suspected abuse of a foster child later murdered by his foster uncle was not immune, and it reversed summary judgment because a jury could find she breached her KRS 620.030 duty to report.
  Why here: This decision fixes the outer limit of reporter immunity: a mandated reporter who stays silent is exposed to civil liability for breach of the reporting duty.
  Quote: “we hold that the Immunity Statute only applies to those who act by making a report”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5448132/aa-ex-rel-lewis-v-kristy-shutts/
- **Nelson v. Turner**, 256 S.W.3d 37 (Ky. Ct. App. 2008)
  Holding: The court remanded for the trial court to determine whether KRS 620.030's mandatory reporting duty applied to a teacher, because if it did the duty is ministerial rather than discretionary and qualified official immunity would be unavailable for her failure to report a student's alleged sexual assault.
  Why here: The case supplies the immunity consequence of the reporting mandate: because the duty is absolute, it is ministerial and strips a public employee of qualified official immunity.
  Quote: “if the mandatory reporting laws apply, then Turner could not be afforded the protection of qualified official immunity”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2344501/nelson-v-turner/
- **White v. Norton Healthcare, Inc.**, 435 S.W.3d 68 (Ky. Ct. App. 2014)
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for hospital staff who reported suspected physical abuse, placed a 72-hour hold on the child, and photographed and examined her without parental consent, holding they acted in subjective good faith under KRS 620.050(1) and that the KRS 620.050(14) negligence exception reaches only negligently performed diagnostics, not the decision to perform them.
  Why here: The opinion applies Peyton's good-faith standard to hospital-based mandated reporters and confirms immunity survives even where the parent disputes the abuse.
  Quote: “We believe the negligence exception is intended to apply only where the diagnostics are administered negligently in some way so as to cause injury to the patient”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5444403/white-v-norton-healthcare-inc/
- **Garrison v. Leahy-Auer**, 220 S.W.3d 693 (Ky. Ct. App. 2006)
  Holding: The court affirmed governmental immunity for the University of Kentucky Medical Center and held that the newborn's attending physician was immune under KRS 620.050(1) for the reporting of a mislabeled meconium drug screen that led to the infant's removal, because she acted in good faith and neither the KRS 620.050(2) Cabinet-employee exception nor the subsection (14) negligent-diagnostics exception applied.
  Why here: It is the predecessor Kentucky construction of the KRS 620.030(1) reporting duty and KRS 620.050(1) immunity on which Norton Hospitals v. Peyton and White v. Norton Healthcare both build.
  Quote: “having concluded that Dr. Leahy-Auer has immunity from liability under KRS 620.030(1) and KRS 620.050(1), any error which may have occurred at the trial of this action is moot”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1756582/garrison-v-leahy-auer/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **Cabinet for Health & Family Services v. J.M.G.**, 475 S.W.3d 600 (Ky. 2015)

  Why here: The opinion describes the family court's supervisory authority to compel Cabinet compliance with reporting and scheduling duties in open DNA and termination cases.
  Quote: “the trial court had ample justification for finding that the Cabinet's foot dragging amounted to at least a lack of due diligence and a failure to make reasonable efforts”

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Cabinet for Health & Family Services v. Todd County Standard, Inc.**, 488 S.W.3d 1 (Ky. Ct. App. 2015)
  Holding: The court affirmed enforcement of an unappealed Attorney General open-records decision requiring the Cabinet to produce records about a child who died under circumstances suggesting abuse, held that statutory authority for a record's creation raises a rebuttable presumption that it exists, and upheld the finding that the Cabinet's denial was willful.
  Why here: The Attorney General's open-records review is a working external oversight mechanism over Cabinet conduct in child-fatality cases.
  Quote: “The Cabinet cannot benefit from intentionally frustrating the Attorney General's review of an open records request”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5446710/cabinet-for-health-family-services-v-todd-county-standard-inc/
- **Cabinet for Health & Family Services v. Courier-Journal, Inc.**, 493 S.W.3d 375 (Ky. Ct. App. 2016)
  Holding: The court held the production issue moot because the 140 child-fatality and near-fatality case files had already been released, and affirmed the finding that the Cabinet willfully withheld them, upholding roughly $300,000 in attorneys' fees and costs and $756,000 in statutory penalties calculated on a per-record, per-day basis under KRS 61.882(5).
  Why here: The litigation exposed the Cabinet's adoption of emergency regulations and wholesale redaction protocols to evade disclosure, a systemic failure surfaced by outside scrutiny.
  Quote: “Public entities must permit inspection of public records as required or risk meaningful punishment for noncompliance”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5447009/cabinet-for-health-family-services-v-courier-journal-inc/
- **Cabinet for Health & Family Services v. J.M.G.**, 475 S.W.3d 600 (Ky. 2015)
  Holding: The Court reversed criminal contempt sanctions imposed on the Cabinet for missing a KRS 620.240 case-progress report and an FCRPP 34 pretrial date because willfulness was not proved, but it recognized that the family court had ample justification for finding that the Cabinet's delay was a failure to make reasonable efforts to finalize the children's permanency plan.
  Why here: It is Kentucky's leading published account of judicial oversight of systemic Cabinet non-compliance with statutory and rule deadlines.
  Quote: “the trial court had ample justification for finding that the Cabinet's foot dragging amounted to at least a lack of due diligence and a failure to make reasonable efforts”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5446084/cabinet-for-health-family-v-jmg/
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court held that the 'reasonable efforts' requirement of 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15) creates no federally enforceable right for children or parents under § 1983 and no implied private right of action, leaving enforcement to the Secretary's power to reduce or eliminate federal payments to a non-compliant State.
  Why here: The Court identified the Secretary's audit and funding-reduction powers under § 671(b) as the intended enforcement mechanism for state plan failures.
  Quote: “the "reasonable efforts" language does not unambiguously confer an enforceable right upon the Act's beneficiaries”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/

## INP-10 · Agency Escalation (Screening → Investigation)
- **Schulkers v. Kammer**, 955 F.3d 520 (6th Cir. 2020)
  Holding: In a case against Kentucky CHFS social workers, the court held the Fourth Amendment governs a social worker's warrantless in-school interview of a child and requires at least reasonable suspicion of abuse (though that right was not clearly established, so those defendants received qualified immunity), and it affirmed the denial of qualified immunity on the parents' substantive and procedural due-process claims arising from a coerced 'prevention plan' that restricted a mother's unsupervised contact with her children.
  Why here: The opinion marks the constitutional boundary that attaches once a hotline report is escalated into an in-person investigation of the children.
  Quote: “a social worker must have a reasonable suspicion of child abuse before conducting an in-school interview when no other exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement applies”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4740438/holly-schulkers-v-elizabeth-kammer/
- **Kottmyer v. Maas**, 436 F.3d 684 (6th Cir. 2006)
  Holding: The court held that the constitutional right to familial association is circumscribed by the government's interest in protecting children from potential abuse and is not implicated by a governmental investigation into abuse allegations standing alone, and it affirmed dismissal of the parents' § 1983 claims against the social worker and the children's hospital.
  Why here: It marks the constitutional floor at the moment a screened report is escalated into a formal investigation, holding that escalation alone violates no right of the parent or child.
  Quote: “We conclude that the right to familial association is not implicated merely by governmental investigation into allegations of child abuse.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/793173/kottmyer-v-maas/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Rye v. Weasel**, 934 S.W.2d 257 (Ky. 1996)
  Holding: The Court adopted the 'Existing Indian Family Doctrine,' holding that the Indian Child Welfare Act does not divest a Kentucky court of jurisdiction where the child is not being removed from an existing Indian family or environment, and it reinstated the circuit court's custody award notwithstanding the tribe's claim of exclusive jurisdiction under 25 U.S.C. § 1911(a).
  Why here: This is Kentucky's only controlling high-court decision on when ICWA's tribal-jurisdiction and notice provisions apply to a child-custody proceeding in this Commonwealth.
  Quote: “The consistent thread that runs throughout the ICWA is a concern for the removal of Indian children from an existing family unit, and the subsequent disruption of an Indian family”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1657915/rye-v-weasel/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court rejected every constitutional challenge to the Indian Child Welfare Act, affirming Congress's authority to enact it and reversing the Fifth Circuit on the anticommandeering claims, while vacating the equal-protection and nondelegation rulings with instructions to dismiss for lack of standing, leaving ICWA fully operative in state child-custody proceedings.
  Why here: This is the controlling modern federal authority confirming that ICWA's placement preferences, notice, intervention and active-efforts requirements bind Kentucky courts, and it must be addressed before any reliance on Rye v. Weasel's Existing Indian Family Doctrine.
  Quote: “the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners' challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **Kovacic v. Cuyahoga County Department of Children & Family Services**, 724 F.3d 687 (6th Cir. 2013)

  Why here: The decision defines when protective custody may be taken without prior judicial authorization: only on genuine exigency demanding immediate action.
  Quote: “Fourth Amendment warrant requirements, including the exigent-circumstances exception, apply to the removal of children from their homes by social workers”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1035935/kovacic-v-cuyahoga-county-department-of-children-family-services/
- **Andrews v. Hickman County**, 700 F.3d 845 (6th Cir. 2012)
  Holding: The court held there is no child-welfare exception to the Fourth Amendment and that social workers are bound by its warrant requirement when entering a home to investigate abuse, but it reversed the denial of qualified immunity to the social workers because that rule was not clearly established in 2008, while affirming the denial of immunity to the deputy who entered.
  Why here: It establishes that a caseworker responding to an abuse allegation cannot enter a home without consent, exigent circumstances, or another recognized exception.
  Quote: “a social worker, like other state officers, is governed by the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement”

  Holding: The court vacated an abuse-and-neglect adjudication because the family court failed to decide the truth or falsity of the petition's allegations under KRS 620.100(3), relied on evidence outside the record, and used the DNA hearing to resolve custody.
  Why here: The court held that if the complainant fails to prove the allegations by a preponderance, the trial court must find for the parent - the judicial analogue of an unfounded closure.

  Holding: The court vacated the termination of an absent mother's parental rights because a fifteen-minute hearing with one conclusory social-worker witness was not clear and convincing evidence; the parent's failure to appear did not shift the Cabinet's burden, and the Cabinet never showed the home's condition was attributable to something other than poverty.
  Why here: The opinion shows what conclusory, unsupported agency evidence looks like, the same deficiency that should produce an unfounded determination at intake.
  Quote: “we observe that the Cabinet did not show that V.S.'s allegedly substandard home was due to factors other than poverty alone, as required by KRS 625.090(2)(g)”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1870719/vs-v-commonwealth-cabinet-for-health-family-services/

## OUT-02 · Termination of Parental Rights / Adoption
- **Santosky v. Kramer**, 455 U.S. 745 (1982)
  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a State to support its allegations by at least clear and convincing evidence before it may permanently terminate parental rights, and struck down New York's preponderance standard.
  Why here: This is the federal constitutional floor for the burden of proof in every Kentucky termination case and is expressly applied in KRS 625.090 jurisprudence.
  Quote: “due process requires that the State support its allegations by at least clear and convincing evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/110676/santosky-v-kramer/
- **N.S. v. C**, 642 S.W.2d 589 (Ky. 1982)
  Holding: Following Santosky v. Kramer, the Court held that the preponderance-of-the-evidence standard then prescribed by KRS 199.603(1) for involuntary termination of parental rights is unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment, and it reversed and remanded for a new trial applying the clear-and-convincing standard.
  Why here: This is Kentucky's own adoption of the clear-and-convincing standard for involuntary termination, the intervening Kentucky Supreme Court authority between the federal floor of Santosky and the modern KRS 625.090 case law that the brief otherwise skips over.
  Quote: “we declare that the provision of KRS 199.603(1) (1978) requiring only a "preponderance of the evidence" in involuntary termination cases is unconstitutional”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5233111/ns-v-c/
- **D.G.R. v. Commonwealth, Cabinet for Health & Family Services**, 364 S.W.3d 106 (Ky. 2012)
  Holding: The Court reinstated a trial court's refusal to terminate parental rights, holding that only a decision to terminate must rest on clear and convincing evidence, that parents may defeat termination by showing by a preponderance that the child will not be abused in the future, and that the KRS 625.090(3) best-interest factors are to be considered rather than treated as a checklist.
  Why here: This is the Kentucky Supreme Court's leading modern explication of the burdens and discretion built into KRS 625.090.
  Quote: “the Cabinet must prove the necessary statutory allegations by clear and convincing evidence in order for the trial court to terminate parental rights”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2541102/dgr-v-commonwealth-cabinet-for-health-family-services/
- **B.L. v. J.S.**, 434 S.W.3d 61 (Ky. Ct. App. 2014)
  Holding: The court affirmed an adoption granted without the biological parents' consent under KRS 199.502, holding that such an adoption is functionally a termination governed by the clear-and-convincing standard, that it does not require a prior abuse-or-neglect adjudication, and that the trial court adequately considered less drastic alternatives.
  Why here: This is the Kentucky rule that a KRS 199.502 adoption without consent must satisfy the same clear-and-convincing proof as an involuntary termination.
  Quote: “An adoption without the consent of a living biological parent is, in effect, a proceeding to terminate that parent's parental rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5444352/bl-v-js/
- **Moore v. Asente**, 110 S.W.3d 336 (Ky. 2003)
  Holding: The Court affirmed the trial court's jurisdiction and reinstated its finding that the birth parents' adoption consents were not knowingly given, but held that the prospective adoptive parents had standing to seek custody and that the birth parents had waived their superior right to custody, so on remand custody must be decided on the child's best interest.
  Why here: The Court's statement that a valid adoption judgment terminates parental rights exactly as a termination order does is the premise from which B.L. v. J.S. derives the rule that a KRS 199.502 adoption without consent requires clear and convincing proof.
  Quote: “Like the final order in a TPR proceeding, a valid adoption judgment terminates the parental rights of the birth parent.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1773531/moore-v-asente/

## OUT-03 · Guardianship / Kinship Permanency
- **L.D. v. J.H.**, 350 S.W.3d 828 (Ky. Ct. App. 2011)
  Holding: The court affirmed an award of permanent custody to grandparents in a dependency case, holding that KRS 620.027 confers standing on grandparents with whom the child is actually residing in a stable relationship, independent of the de facto custodian requirements of KRS 403.270.
  Why here: This is the controlling Kentucky decision on KRS 620.027 grandparent standing to obtain permanent custody as a permanency outcome.
  Quote: “Once the family court makes a finding of a stable relationship between grandparent(s) and child, the court is to utilize KRS Chapter 403 to determine custodial arrangements.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5281232/ld-v-jh/
- **N.L. v. W.F.**, 368 S.W.3d 136 (Ky. Ct. App. 2012)
  Holding: The court held that a permanent custody award may be entered within a KRS Chapter 620 dependency action if the proper procedures are followed, but reversed because the family court failed to make sufficient findings on the KRS 403.270 best-interest factors and did not account for the mother's progress on her case plan.
  Why here: It is the Kentucky authority on converting a dependency case into a permanent third-party custody disposition.
  Quote: “such a hearing and an award of custody are not precluded by the structure of KRS Chapter 620, so long as the proper procedures are followed”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5281839/nl-v-wf/
- **Baker v. Webb**, 127 S.W.3d 622 (Ky. 2004)
  Holding: The Court held that second cousins denied any consideration for relative placement, in derogation of the Cabinet's own standards, had a sufficient legal interest to intervene as of right in the foster parents' adoption proceeding; it vacated the adoption and ordered the Cabinet to evaluate the relatives.
  Why here: It establishes that relatives denied consideration may intervene to seek permanency for the child themselves.
  Quote: “Although the statute and regulations do not mandate that the Cabinet choose a relative placement over other options, they at the very least, require that the Cabinet consider relatives”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1652041/baker-v-webb/
- **Moore v. Asente**, 110 S.W.3d 336 (Ky. 2003)
  Holding: The Court affirmed the trial court's jurisdiction and reinstated its finding that the birth parents' adoption consents were not knowingly given, but held that the prospective adoptive parents had standing to seek custody and that the birth parents had waived their superior right to custody, so on remand custody must be decided on the child's best interest.
  Why here: It is the Kentucky Supreme Court's framework for a nonparent's standing to seek custody and for the waiver exception that permits a permanent third-party custody award decided on the child's best interest rather than on parental unfitness.
  Quote: “Like the final order in a TPR proceeding, a valid adoption judgment terminates the parental rights of the birth parent.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1773531/moore-v-asente/
- **London v. Collins**, 242 S.W.3d 351 (Ky. Ct. App. 2007)
  Holding: The court held that a permanent-custody order entered in a KRS Chapter 620 dependency action is a 'custody decree' subject to the modification restrictions of KRS 403.340 only if it was actually based on the best-interest standards of KRS 403.270(2); because the order here rested solely on a social worker's unsworn statement with no evidence taken, it was not, and the court vacated and remanded for a full custody hearing.
  Why here: It is the authority N.L. v. W.F. relies on for converting a dependency case into a permanent third-party custody disposition, and it specifies what a permanency order must contain to become a durable custody decree.
  Quote: “for a custody order to be a "custody decree," within the meaning of KRS Chapter 403, it must be based on the standards set out in KRS 403.270(2)”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1404177/london-v-collins/

## OUT-04 · Extended Foster Care / Aging Out
- **J.S. v. Commonwealth**, 304 S.W.3d 67 (Ky. Ct. App. 2009)
  Holding: The court affirmed the commitment of a habitual runaway to the Cabinet, holding that the absence of written findings about the inadequacy of less restrictive alternatives is not fatal where the record and the court's statements show it considered and rejected those alternatives and found commitment necessary to protect the child from physical harm.
  Why here: The case governs commitment of an older youth to Cabinet custody, the disposition that carries a youth toward an extended-care or aging-out posture.
  Quote: “The failure to make written findings regarding the inadequacy of available alternatives is not fatal to commitment”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5280967/js-v-commonwealth/
- **N.L. v. W.F.**, 368 S.W.3d 136 (Ky. Ct. App. 2012)
  Holding: The court held that a permanent custody award may be entered within a KRS Chapter 620 dependency action if the proper procedures are followed, but reversed because the family court failed to make sufficient findings on the KRS 403.270 best-interest factors and did not account for the mother's progress on her case plan.
  Why here: The court emphasized that KRS 620.140 permits continued out-of-home placement only where remaining home is contrary to the child's welfare, the standard for prolonged commitment.
  Quote: “such a hearing and an award of custody are not precluded by the structure of KRS Chapter 620, so long as the proper procedures are followed”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5281839/nl-v-wf/

## OUT-05 · Case Dismissal
- **Commonwealth, Cabinet for Health & Family Services v. C.V.**, 192 S.W.3d 703 (Ky. Ct. App. 2006)
  Holding: The court affirmed dismissal of the Cabinet's termination petition because the circuit court made no findings that would support termination, while agreeing with the Cabinet that a district-court change of the permanency goal from reunification to adoption is not a prerequisite to filing a termination petition.
  Why here: This is Kentucky's published example of a termination petition properly dismissed for want of findings, with leave to refile.
  Quote: “Under KRS 625.090(3)(c), the court can make its own concurrent finding as to reasonable efforts”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5279269/commonwealth-cabinet-for-health-family-services-v-cv/
- **S.R. v. J.N.**, 307 S.W.3d 631 (Ky. Ct. App. 2010)
  Holding: The court vacated an abuse-and-neglect adjudication because the family court failed to decide the truth or falsity of the petition's allegations under KRS 620.100(3), relied on evidence outside the record, and used the DNA hearing to resolve custody.
  Why here: The opinion explains that unproven allegations require a ruling in the parent's favor rather than a custody disposition.
  Quote: “The family court did not follow the statutory requirement that it determine the truth or falsity of the allegations in the complaint”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5280984/sr-v-jn/
- **Commonwealth, Cabinet for Health & Family Services ex rel. M.H. v. R.H.**, 199 S.W.3d 201 (Ky. Ct. App. 2006)
  Holding: The court held that issue preclusion did not bar the Cabinet from introducing evidence identifying a parent as the perpetrator of prior sexual abuse of the child's seven siblings, because the earlier court had not actually decided the perpetrator's identity and that identity is not material to a finding that a parent created a risk of sexual abuse; it vacated the dismissal and ordered an expedited adjudication.
  Why here: The court vacated a dismissal that had been entered after the family court excluded the sibling-abuse evidence, showing that evidentiary error cannot be the basis for closing a case.
  Quote: “The identity of the perpetrator of the abuse is not material to that finding.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2358367/commonwealth-cabinet-for-health-family-services-ex-rel-mh-v-rh/

## OUT-06 · Child Fatality / Serious Injury
- **A.A. ex rel. Lewis v. Shutts**, 516 S.W.3d 343 (Ky. Ct. App. 2017)
  Holding: The court held that KRS 620.050(1) immunity is available only to a person who acts by making a report, so a physician who did not report suspected abuse of a foster child later murdered by his foster uncle was not immune, and it reversed summary judgment because a jury could find she breached her KRS 620.030 duty to report.
  Why here: It defines the civil remedy available to a deceased foster child's estate against professionals who failed to report the abuse that killed him.
  Quote: “we hold that the Immunity Statute only applies to those who act by making a report”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5448132/aa-ex-rel-lewis-v-kristy-shutts/
- **Barnette v. Evans**, No. 2023-CA-1159-MR (Ky. Ct. App. Aug. 16, 2024)
  Holding: In a mother's negligence and wrongful-death action against DCBS social workers after her child's death, the court reversed summary judgment on qualified official immunity because the circuit court made no findings identifying the source or nature of the workers' duties, and remanded for the specific findings Meinhart requires on the ministerial/discretionary question.
  Why here: It sets out the findings a Kentucky court must make before dismissing a civil claim arising from a child fatality under agency supervision.
  Quote: “the circuit court's order granting summary judgment did not contain any findings identifying the nature or source of the defendants' duties and why it believed these duties were discretionary”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10069651/ashley-v-barnette-individually-and-in-her-capacity-as-administratrix-of/
- **Cabinet for Health & Family Services v. Todd County Standard, Inc.**, 488 S.W.3d 1 (Ky. Ct. App. 2015)
  Holding: The court affirmed enforcement of an unappealed Attorney General open-records decision requiring the Cabinet to produce records about a child who died under circumstances suggesting abuse, held that statutory authority for a record's creation raises a rebuttable presumption that it exists, and upheld the finding that the Cabinet's denial was willful.
  Why here: KRS 620.050(12)(a) makes records of a fatality caused by abuse or neglect subject to disclosure, and the court enforced that provision.
  Quote: “The Cabinet cannot benefit from intentionally frustrating the Attorney General's review of an open records request”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5446710/cabinet-for-health-family-services-v-todd-county-standard-inc/
- **Cabinet for Health & Family Services v. Courier-Journal, Inc.**, 493 S.W.3d 375 (Ky. Ct. App. 2016)
  Holding: The court held the production issue moot because the 140 child-fatality and near-fatality case files had already been released, and affirmed the finding that the Cabinet willfully withheld them, upholding roughly $300,000 in attorneys' fees and costs and $756,000 in statutory penalties calculated on a per-record, per-day basis under KRS 61.882(5).
  Why here: The withheld records were the Cabinet's own internal reviews and case files for children who died or nearly died of abuse or neglect.
  Quote: “Public entities must permit inspection of public records as required or risk meaningful punishment for noncompliance”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5447009/cabinet-for-health-family-services-v-courier-journal-inc/
