## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **Ray v. Department of Human Resources**, 155 Ga. App. 81, 270 S.E.2d 303 (1980)
  Holding: The court reversed, holding that a juvenile court may order disclosure of otherwise confidential DHR child-abuse records when it finds disclosure necessary to resolve an issue before it, and that parental rights may not be terminated on a bare 'welfare of the child' test without a showing of parental unfitness.
  Why here: It is the Georgia authority for a court compelling production of agency investigative records in a proceeding it is hearing.
  Quote: “it is not proper to consider the question of termination of parental rights based solely upon a "welfare of the child" test, without some required showing of parental unfitness”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1315311/ray-v-department-of-human-resources/
- **Floyd County Grand Jury v. Department of Family & Children Services**, 218 Ga. App. 832, 463 S.E.2d 519 (1995)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals affirmed an order quashing grand jury subpoenas to seven DFACS employees, holding that county departments of family and children services are instrumentalities of the state Department of Human Resources rather than county offices, so a grand jury has no power under OCGA 15-12-71(b)(2) or any other statute to conduct a civil investigation of DFACS.
  Why here: It marks the outer boundary of compulsory process directed at DFACS by a body other than the court hearing a child's case.
  Quote: “these departments are instrumentalities of the Department of Human Resources (DHR), a state agency, independent of the county, and no evidence has been presented to the contrary”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5792460/floyd-county-grand-jury-v-department-of-family-children-services/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In the Interest of M. F., a Child**, 298 Ga. 138, 780 S.E.2d 291 (2015)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Georgia reversed dismissal of a father's petition, holding that under OCGA 15-11-244(c) a change in a parent's circumstances sufficient to make a previously unfit parent fit can be a material change in the child's circumstances warranting modification, vacatur or revocation of a permanent guardianship.
  Why here: It is the vehicle by which a rehabilitated parent may ask the juvenile court to restore custody.
  Quote: “We hold only that a change in parental circumstances sufficient to render a previously unfit parent fit may constitute a material change in the circumstances of the child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3156735/in-the-interest-of-mf-a-child/
- **In re M. A. F.**, 254 Ga. 748, 334 S.E.2d 668 (1985)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Georgia reversed, holding that a caregiver who had received parental power by a valid relinquishment contract and stood in loco parentis was entitled to the notice, hearing and juvenile-code safeguards that the juvenile court denied her before the child was removed and custody given to the agency.
  Why here: It recognizes the standing of a private caregiver who took the child in at the parents' request.
  Quote: “The juvenile court failed to acknowledge the appellant's rights, and undoubtedly failed to provide her with the safeguards and procedures of the juvenile code”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5746226/in-re-m-a-f/
- **In the Interest of K. M., a Child**, 811 S.E.2d 505 (Ga. Ct. App. 2018)
  Holding: The Court of Appeals reversed, holding that a juvenile court deciding a parent's petition to end a third party's temporary guardianship must apply the clear and convincing evidence standard for physical or significant long-term emotional harm, not a probable-cause standard, and that the burden rests on the third party seeking to keep custody.
  Why here: It is the route by which a parent who earlier consented to a guardianship asks for the child's return.
  Quote: “the burden of satisfying this stringent standard rests on the third party who is seeking to obtain or maintain custody of the child”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4477633/in-the-interest-of-km-a-child/
- **Clark v. Wade**, 273 Ga. 587, 544 S.E.2d 99 (2001)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Georgia reversed and remanded two grandparent-versus-parent custody cases, upholding the best-interest standard of OCGA 19-7-1(b.1) as constitutional only under a narrowing construction requiring the third party first to prove by clear and convincing evidence that parental custody would harm the child, and only then that third-party custody would best promote the child's welfare and happiness.
  Why here: It governs the reverse path, where a parent who let relatives take the child comes forward to reclaim custody and the third party bears the burden of defeating the parental presumption.
  Quote: “the third party must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the child will suffer physical or emotional harm if custody were awarded to the biological parent”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1266755/clark-v-wade/
- **Boddie v. Daniels**, 288 Ga. 143, 702 S.E.2d 172 (2010)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Georgia reversed and remanded, holding that Clark v. Wade's narrowing construction controls the 'best interest' standard in OCGA 29-2-8(b), so a juvenile court may not deny a parent's petition to terminate a third party's temporary guardianship without finding by clear and convincing evidence that termination would cause the child physical or significant long-term emotional harm.
  Why here: It is the Supreme Court of Georgia authority protecting the parent who voluntarily consented to a guardianship during a crisis and later asks the court to give the child back.
  Quote: “Therefore, the juvenile court erred by denying the petition to terminate the temporary guardianship without finding by clear and convincing evidence that such termination would harm the child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2396467/boddie-v-daniels/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held that 'domicile' in ICWA is a matter of uniform federal law, that children born off-reservation to parents domiciled on the reservation are themselves domiciled there, and that the tribal court therefore had exclusive jurisdiction, voiding the state adoption decree.
  Why here: It is the controlling authority on exclusive tribal jurisdiction and on transfer under ICWA sections 1911(a) and (b).
  Quote: “We therefore think it beyond dispute that Congress intended a uniform federal law of domicile for the ICWA.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl**, 570 U.S. 637 (2013)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held that ICWA section 1912(f) does not apply where the Indian parent never had custody, that section 1912(d)'s remedial-efforts requirement applies only where the termination would precipitate the breakup of an Indian family, and that section 1915(a) preferences do not bar an adoption where no preferred placement has come forward.
  Why here: It defines when ICWA's heightened termination and active-efforts requirements actually attach in a state proceeding.
  Quote: “we hold that §1912(d) applies only in cases where an Indian family's "breakup" would be precipitated by the termination of the parent's rights”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/931616/adoptive-couple-v-baby-girl/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Supreme Court rejected all challenges to ICWA, holding that Congress had authority to enact it, that the anticommandeering challenges failed, and that the equal protection and nondelegation challenges could not be reached for lack of standing, leaving ICWA and its placement preferences fully operative in state courts.
  Why here: It confirms that Georgia juvenile courts remain fully bound by ICWA's requirements and placement preferences.
  Quote: “the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners' challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/

## PMC-04 · Fatality Review Commission
- **Georgia Department of Human Services v. Spruill**, 294 Ga. 100, 751 S.E.2d 315 (2013)
  Holding: The Supreme Court of Georgia reversed the Court of Appeals and held that the Georgia Tort Claims Act's discretionary function exception preserved sovereign immunity for a DFCS caseworker's choices in investigating a report of child neglect, barring the tort suit brought after the children were severely injured.
  Why here: It is the Georgia decision a fatality or near-fatality review must reckon with on accountability for investigative choices.
  Quote: “But when such agents act as representatives of the sovereign, they exercise a different sort of discretion, one that may be informed in significant part by considerations of public policy.”

- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Supreme Court held that a state's failure to protect a child from violence by his father, even after repeated reports and agency monitoring, is not a deprivation of liberty under the Due Process Clause, because the Clause imposes no general affirmative duty to protect.
  Why here: It is the case any fatality review must confront on the limits of legal accountability for non-removal.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause.”
