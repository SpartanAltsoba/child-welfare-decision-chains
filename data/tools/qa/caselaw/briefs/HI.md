# HI — 41 authorities, verified 2026-09-20

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Kaho'ohanohano ex rel. Morales-Kaho'ohanohano v. Department of Human Services**, 117 Haw. 262, 178 P.3d 538 (2008)
  Holding: The court affirmed a negligence judgment against DHS, holding that once DHS receives a report of suspected child abuse, HRS chapter 587 and its implementing rules create a special relationship and an affirmative legal duty to investigate reasonably and to protect the reported child from further harm.
  Why here: This is the controlling Hawai'i authority that a mandated report triggers a legally enforceable DHS duty, so a mandatory reporter's report is not merely informational but starts a duty-bearing process.
  Quote: “Accordingly, we conclude that DHS had a duty to protect Minor under the circumstances of this case.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6615449/kahoohanohano-ex-rel-morales-kahoohanohano-v-department-of-human/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause imposes no general affirmative federal duty on a State to protect a child from private violence, even where caseworkers received and recorded repeated reports of abuse, while noting that States may impose such duties through their own tort law.
  Why here: A report alone does not create a federal constitutional duty, which is why Hawai'i's state-law duty under Kaho'ohanohano is the operative one.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/
- **In the Interest of Doe**, 91 Haw. 166, 981 P.2d 723 (Haw. Ct. App. 1999)
  Holding: The ICA affirmed an order of family supervision, holding that a chapter 587 petition is verified by a declaration under penalty of perjury, that threatened harm alone suffices to sustain the petition, and that DHS's disclosure of case information to the police was not a ground for dismissal absent particularized objection.
  Why here: It confirms that a report describing risk rather than completed injury is legally sufficient to start and sustain protective proceedings.
  Quote: “Thus, allegations of actual harm are not required to sustain a petition for family supervision and allegations of threatened harm would suffice.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1160891/in-the-interest-of-doe/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In re Guardianship of I.W. and T.E.**, No. SCWC-23-0000356 (Haw. June 16, 2026)
  Holding: The court held that the presumption that a fit parent acts in the child's best interest survives the parent's consent to a guardianship, and that article I, sections 5 and 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution require clear and convincing evidence of unfitness before that presumption may be set aside.
  Why here: A parent who voluntarily asks for help by consenting to a guardianship does not thereby waive the constitutional presumption in their favor.
  Quote: “the rebuttable presumption that a fit parent acts in the best interest of their child extends to guardianship proceedings”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10875980/in-re-guardianship-of-iw/
- **In the Interest of Doe**, 96 Haw. 272, 30 P.3d 878 (2001)
  Holding: The court held that the family court lacked jurisdiction to order the Department of Health to fund services where IDEA remedies were unexhausted, but that the child's status as a ward of the State created an independent state-law basis obligating the State, with DHS ultimately accountable, to pay for his mental health services.
  Why here: Foster families who seek help for a child in their care have a state-law route to funded services independent of federal education law.
  Quote: “we hold that the family court correctly concluded that an independent state basis exists under which John is entitled to receive payment for the mental health services”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2622598/in-the-interest-of-doe/
- **In re the Guardianship of Doe**, 93 Haw. 374, 4 P.3d 508 (Haw. Ct. App. 2000)
  Holding: The ICA held that in deciding whether to terminate or continue a non-parent's guardianship of a minor the family court must consider the parental preference of HRS § 571-46(1) within the best-interests inquiry, adopting for Hawai'i a rebuttable custody presumption in favor of parents, but affirmed continuation of the grandparents' guardianship because the family court implicitly considered that preference and the evidence supported its order.
  Why here: This is the origin of the rebuttable parental presumption in Hawai'i guardianship practice that In re Guardianship of I.W. and T.E. later grounded in article I, sections 5 and 6, so a parent who seeks or consents to a guardianship starts from that presumption.
  Quote: “we believe our jurisdiction is similar to the majority of jurisdictions which adopt a custody presumption in favor of parents subject to rebuttal”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2595363/in-re-the-guardianship-of-doe/
- **In re the Guardianship of Doe**, 106 Haw. 75, 101 P.3d 684 (Haw. Ct. App. 2004)
  Holding: The ICA held that the heightened clear and convincing standard of proof required to terminate parental rights does not apply to the parental-fitness question in a minor guardianship proceeding, because a guardianship of the person of a minor is neither absolute nor irrevocable, and it vacated the denial of the grandparents' co-guardianship petition and remanded for reconsideration.
  Why here: It held that parental fitness in a guardianship need not be proved by clear and convincing evidence — precisely the rule In re Guardianship of I.W. and T.E. displaced on state constitutional grounds in 2026 — so anyone relying on pre-2026 guardianship authority must check this holding first.
  Quote: “We conclude the family court erred in applying a heightened standard of proof to the question of the fitness of the natural father.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6614269/in-re-the-guardianship-of-doe/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Peer News LLC v. City & County of Honolulu**, 143 Haw. 472, 431 P.3d 1245 (2018)
  Holding: The court held that the Office of Information Practices palpably erred in reading a categorical deliberative process privilege into HRS § 92F-13(3), because that exception requires an individualized showing that disclosure would frustrate a legitimate government function.
  Why here: Oversight bodies and the public can reach agency deliberative records absent a record-specific frustration showing.
  Quote: “OIP palpably erred in adopting a deliberative process privilege pursuant to the HRS § 92F-13(3) exception for documents that would frustrate a legitimate government function if disclosed”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4576246/peer-news-llc-v-city-of-honolulu-dept-of-budget-fiscal-servs/
- **Honolulu Civil Beat Inc. v. Department of the Attorney General**, 146 Haw. 285, 463 P.3d 942 (2020)
  Holding: The court applied the UIPA's presumption of openness in reviewing the Attorney General's refusal to release an investigative report, holding that the agency bears the burden of establishing that a claimed exemption, including one resting on privilege, actually applies.
  Why here: Records generated by state investigations of agency conduct are presumptively public, which is how oversight findings become actionable.
  Quote: “The UIPA contains a strong presumption in favor of public disclosure of government records.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4734772/honolulu-civil-beat-inc-v-department-of-the-attorney-general/
- **Civil Beat Law Center for the Public Interest, Inc. v. City and County of Honolulu**, 144 Haw. 466, 445 P.3d 47 (2019)
  Holding: The court held that the circuit court erred in dismissing the Sunshine Law complaint, ruling that the law permits but does not require a board to close a meeting concerning personnel matters and does not expose members to criminal penalty for meeting in the open.
  Why here: Boards that oversee agencies cannot use personnel confidentiality as a mandatory reason to exclude the public.
  Quote: “The Sunshine Law does not require that meetings related to personnel matters be closed to the public”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4634801/civil-beat-law-center-for-the-public-interest-inc-v-city-and-county-of/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In the Interest of "A" Children**, 119 Haw. 28, 193 P.3d 1228 (Haw. Ct. App. 2008)
  Holding: The ICA rejected a Native Hawaiian father's claim that ICWA's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard and qualified-expert requirement applied to him, holding that ICWA reaches only proceedings involving an 'Indian child' and does not include Native Hawaiians, and affirmed termination under the ordinary clear and convincing standard.
  Why here: This is the controlling Hawai'i holding that Native Hawaiian ancestry does not trigger ICWA, which is the single most misunderstood jurisdictional point in Hawai'i child welfare practice.
  Quote: “The ICWA clearly does not include Native Hawaiians within its purview and applies only to state-child-protective proceedings involving an "Indian child."”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2514722/in-the-interest-of-a-children/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court rejected the Article I, anticommandeering and recordkeeping challenges to the Indian Child Welfare Act and held the petitioners lacked standing to press the equal protection and nondelegation challenges to its placement preferences, leaving ICWA in force.
  Why here: Where a child is an Indian child, ICWA governs the state proceeding from start to finish and its preferences bind Hawai'i courts.
  Quote: “Courts must adhere to the placement preferences absent "good cause" to depart from them.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **In re J.H.**, 152 Haw. 373 (2023)
  Holding: The court reaffirmed that counsel must be appointed at the onset of a parental rights case, but held that a later break in representation caused by the parent's own voluntary absence is not structural error so long as the proceeding remains fundamentally fair.
  Why here: It quotes and applies the statutory grounds for warrantless police protective custody and the department's assumption of temporary foster custody.
  Quote: “A family court must timely appoint counsel in parental rights cases.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9384020/in-re-jh/
- **Kirkpatrick v. County of Washoe**, 843 F.3d 784 (9th Cir. 2016) (en banc)
  Holding: Sitting en banc, the court restated that officials must ordinarily obtain judicial authorization before removing a child and may seize a child without a warrant only on reasonable cause to believe the child faces imminent danger of serious bodily injury, though it found the exception satisfied on those facts.
  Why here: This is the circuit's en banc statement of the warrant baseline that governs Hawai'i emergency custody practice.
  Quote: “Under the Fourth Amendment, government officials are ordinarily required to obtain prior judicial authorization before removing a child from the custody of her parent.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4328788/jamie-kirkpatrick-v-county-of-washoe/
- **Demaree v. Pederson**, 880 F.3d 1066 (9th Cir. 2018)
  Holding: The court held that a caseworker was not entitled to qualified immunity for removing children without a court order where the record showed no articulable imminent risk of serious physical injury, and that the availability of time to obtain a warrant is irrelevant when no such risk exists.
  Why here: Generalized concern or suspicion is legally insufficient to support warrantless protective custody.
  Quote: “The clearly established case law requires articulable, imminent, and serious physical injury or physical abuse to children to justify removing them from their parents' home without a judicial order.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4461266/lisa-demaree-v-john-krause/
- **Stanley v. Illinois**, 405 U.S. 645 (1972)
  Holding: The Court held that an unwed father was entitled as a matter of due process to a hearing on his fitness before his children were taken from him, and that denying him the hearing given other parents also violated equal protection.
  Why here: Emergency custody must be followed promptly by a hearing on the parent's fitness; presumed unfitness is unconstitutional.
  Quote: “We have concluded that all Illinois parents are constitutionally entitled to a hearing on their fitness before their children are removed from their custody.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/108497/stanley-v-illinois/

## OUT-06 · Child Fatality / Serious Injury

  Holding: The court affirmed a negligence judgment against DHS, holding that once DHS receives a report of suspected child abuse, HRS chapter 587 and its implementing rules create a special relationship and an affirmative legal duty to investigate reasonably and to protect the reported child from further harm.
  Why here: This is Hawai'i's leading case on agency liability where a child suffers catastrophic injury after a report to DHS.
  Quote: “Accordingly, we conclude that DHS had a duty to protect Minor under the circumstances of this case.”

  Holding: The court held that foster children have a Fourteenth Amendment liberty interest in supervision and protection from harm by their foster parents, and denied qualified immunity to licensors and social workers who placed and left children with a foster parent despite warning signs.
  Why here: It is the circuit authority for constitutional claims when a child is seriously harmed while in a licensed foster home.
  Quote: “The Fourteenth Amendment substantive due process clause protects a foster child's liberty interest in social worker supervision and protection from harm inflicted by a foster parent.”

  Why here: It marks the boundary between federal constitutional claims and state tort claims after a child dies following agency contact.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause.”

  Why here: Where a foster child dies or suffers a near fatality, the confidentiality of the CPA and adoption files yields, and the record of how the agency and courts handled the case becomes publicly obtainable.
  Quote: “We hold that the standard for whether a legitimate purpose exists for disclosure of CPA records is not grounded solely in the best interest of the child standard.”

## FAIL-02 · Unlawful Removal
- **Kirkpatrick v. County of Washoe**, 843 F.3d 784 (9th Cir. 2016) (en banc)
  Holding: Sitting en banc, the court restated that officials must ordinarily obtain judicial authorization before removing a child and may seize a child without a warrant only on reasonable cause to believe the child faces imminent danger of serious bodily injury, though it found the exception satisfied on those facts.
  Why here: It is the controlling Ninth Circuit authority against which an unlawful-removal § 1983 claim in Hawai'i is measured.
  Quote: “Under the Fourth Amendment, government officials are ordinarily required to obtain prior judicial authorization before removing a child from the custody of her parent.”

  Holding: The court held that a caseworker was not entitled to qualified immunity for removing children without a court order where the record showed no articulable imminent risk of serious physical injury, and that the availability of time to obtain a warrant is irrelevant when no such risk exists.
  Why here: It is the clearest circuit statement of the elements a plaintiff must negate to establish an unlawful warrantless removal.
  Quote: “The clearly established case law requires articulable, imminent, and serious physical injury or physical abuse to children to justify removing them from their parents' home without a judicial order.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4461266/lisa-demaree-v-john-krause/

  Why here: It is the foundational circuit case that a child-welfare investigation is not an exception to ordinary search and seizure law.
  Quote: “In our circuit, a reasonable official would have known that the law barred this entry.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7078750/calabretta-v-floyd/
- **Rieman v. Vazquez**, 96 F.4th 1085 (9th Cir. 2024)
  Holding: The court affirmed denial of absolute and qualified immunity to social workers who failed to give a mother notice of the detention hearing and then told the juvenile court her whereabouts were unknown, holding that giving notice is a mandatory, non-discretionary act and that the right against judicial deception was clearly established.
  Why here: Removal orders obtained through misstatements to the juvenile court are actionable notwithstanding absolute or qualified immunity.
  Quote: “An individual has a well-established constitutional right to be free from deception in the presentation of evidence during juvenile dependency proceedings.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9481186/sydney-rieman-v-gloria-vasquez/
- **Mann v. County of San Diego**, 907 F.3d 1154 (9th Cir. 2018)
  Holding: The court held that the County violated parents' Fourteenth Amendment rights by subjecting removed children to invasive medical examinations without notifying the parents and without either parental consent or judicial authorization, absent a medical emergency or risk that evidence would dissipate.
  Why here: Examining a removed child without parental notice or a court order is an independent constitutional violation beyond the removal itself.
  Quote: “the County is required to notify the parents and obtain parental consent (or a court order) in advance of performing the Polinsky medical examinations”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4548912/mark-mann-v-county-of-san-diego/
