## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **In the Interest of L.V.**, 127 A.3d 831 (Pa. Super. 2015)
  Holding: The court affirmed the dependency adjudication of a child certified as a near fatality, holding the trial court did not abuse its discretion where medical evidence established extensive inflicted injuries and the mother failed to seek treatment.
  Why here: A hospital child-abuse team's findings supplied the report and the proof supporting intervention.
  Quote: “In order to adjudicate a child dependent, the court must determine that the above definition has been met by clear and convincing evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3154267/in-the-interest-of-lv-a-minor/
- **Heinrich v. Conemaugh Valley Memorial Hospital**, 648 A.2d 53, 436 Pa. Super. 465 (1994)
  Holding: Affirming dismissal on preliminary objections, the court held that hospital staff and physicians who reported suspected child abuse were immune under 23 Pa.C.S. § 6318 because general averments of malice failed to plead the specific facts needed to rebut the statutory presumption of a reporter's good faith.
  Why here: The published Pennsylvania authority on a mandated reporter's own exposure: § 6318 immunity attaches to the act of reporting and the presumption of good faith must be rebutted with specific facts.
  Quote: “We cannot fault Appellees, given the criminal penalty imposed for failing to report suspected child abuse, from making a report under these circumstances.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1523464/heinrich-v-conemaugh-valley-memorial-hospital/
- **K.H. v. Kumar**, 122 A.3d 1080 (Pa. Super. 2015)
  Holding: Reversing summary judgment and remanding, the court held that although the CPSL creates no private cause of action for failure to report suspected child abuse, a child may pursue an ordinary medical-malpractice claim where expert testimony would let a jury find that the physicians' standard of care required them to recognize and report the abuse.
  Why here: The leading Pennsylvania decision on the consequences of not reporting: no CPSL cause of action, but the reporting obligation can form part of a professional's common-law standard of care.
  Quote: “the CPSL does not expressly preclude civil liability for a failure to report abuse, nor immunize those who fail in their reporting obligations”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2830539/kh-v-kumar-s-md/

## INP-02 · Voluntary / Third-Party Report
- **In the Interest of Y.W.-B.**, No. 1 EAP 2021 (Pa. Dec. 23, 2021)
  Holding: The court reversed the Superior Court, holding that an order compelling a parent to admit child-welfare workers into the home requires probable cause evaluated under ordinary search-and-seizure principles, and that a single stale, uncorroborated anonymous report did not supply it.
  Why here: A third-party report is the starting point, not the end point; its veracity and reliability must be assessed before entry is compelled.
  Quote: “We expressly hold that there is no "social worker exception" to compliance with constitutional limitations on an entry into a home without consent or exigent circumstances.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5311733/in-the-interest-of-yw-b-apl-of-jb/
- **Good v. Dauphin County Social Services for Children & Youth**, 891 F.2d 1087 (3d Cir. 1989)
  Holding: The court reversed summary judgment on qualified immunity, holding that on the mother's version of the facts a caseworker and police officer entered the home and strip-searched the child without valid consent, a warrant, or exigent circumstances, violating clearly established law.
  Why here: A single anonymous report of unspecified bruises did not justify forced entry or a body search.
  Quote: “under these circumstances a reasonable law enforcement officer could not have believed Ms. Good had given legally effective consent to either the entry or the subsequent strip search”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/533797/good-v-dauphin-county-social-services-for-children-and-youth/
- **Croft v. Westmoreland County Children & Youth Services**, 103 F.3d 1123 (3d Cir. 1997)
  Holding: Reversing summary judgment for the caseworker, the court held that forcing a father from his home on the basis of an uncorroborated anonymous tip violated the family's substantive due process rights because the caseworker lacked objectively reasonable grounds to suspect abuse.
  Why here: A third-party allegation must be corroborated before it can justify coercive interference with the family.
  Quote: “we hold that she lacked objectively reasonable grounds to believe the child had been sexually abused or was in imminent danger of sexual abuse”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/732428/croft-v-westmoreland-county-children-and-youth-services/
- **Heinrich v. Conemaugh Valley Memorial Hospital**, 648 A.2d 53, 436 Pa. Super. 465 (1994)
  Holding: Affirming dismissal on preliminary objections, the court held that hospital staff and physicians who reported suspected child abuse were immune under 23 Pa.C.S. § 6318 because general averments of malice failed to plead the specific facts needed to rebut the statutory presumption of a reporter's good faith.
  Why here: Confirms that the immunity and good-faith presumption protect the report itself, whatever its ultimate accuracy, so long as bad faith is not specifically pleaded.
  Quote: “We cannot fault Appellees, given the criminal penalty imposed for failing to report suspected child abuse, from making a report under these circumstances.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1523464/heinrich-v-conemaugh-valley-memorial-hospital/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In re Adoption of K.L.R.F.**, 515 A.2d 33, 356 Pa. Super. 555 (1986)
  Holding: The court set aside the termination of an Indian mother's parental rights and ordered the child returned, holding that 25 U.S.C. § 1913(b) of the Indian Child Welfare Act allowed her to withdraw her consent to the foster placement at any time.
  Why here: Governs a guardian's voluntary placement of a child and the statutory right to revoke that consent.
  Quote: “appellant had the right and ability to withdraw her consent to the placement and to effect an immediate return of the child”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2329951/in-re-adoption-of-klrf/
- **In re M.L.**, 757 A.2d 849, 562 Pa. 646 (2000)
  Holding: The court reversed the Superior Court, holding that a child whose non-custodial parent is ready, willing and able to provide proper care does not meet the Juvenile Act definition of a dependent child and may not be adjudicated dependent.
  Why here: A guardian's request for intervention cannot produce dependency jurisdiction if that guardian can provide proper care.
  Quote: “a child is not dependent if the child has a parent who is willing and able to provide proper care to the child”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2157096/in-re-ml/

## INP-07 · Medical Disclosure
- **In the Matter of L.Z.**, 111 A.3d 1164, 631 Pa. 343 (2015)
  Holding: Reversing the Superior Court and reinstating the trial court, the court held that the rebuttable presumption of 23 Pa.C.S. § 6381(d) identifying the caregiver as the abuser applies even where a child was in the care of multiple responsible adults.
  Why here: Medical findings of injuries not ordinarily sustained absent abuse are the evidentiary engine of the § 6381(d) presumption.
  Quote: “when a child is in the care of multiple parents or other persons responsible for care, those individuals are accountable for the care and protection of the child”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2789257/in-the-matter-of-lz-appeal-of-lz/
- **In the Interest of L.V.**, 127 A.3d 831 (Pa. Super. 2015)
  Holding: The court affirmed the dependency adjudication of a child certified as a near fatality, holding the trial court did not abuse its discretion where medical evidence established extensive inflicted injuries and the mother failed to seek treatment.
  Why here: Illustrates how a medical encounter documenting inflicted injuries drives the entire child-protection sequence.
  Quote: “In order to adjudicate a child dependent, the court must determine that the above definition has been met by clear and convincing evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3154267/in-the-interest-of-lv-a-minor/
- **In the Interest of N.B.-A.**, No. 11 EAP 2019 (Pa. Jan. 22, 2020)
  Holding: The court reversed, holding the evidence insufficient to find the mother a perpetrator of child abuse by omission and that the § 6381(d) presumption does not apply absent evidence the caregiver knew or should have known of the abuse or risk and disregarded it.
  Why here: Hospital presentation and medical findings alone did not establish the non-abusing parent's knowledge.
  Quote: “we hold there was insufficient evidence to support the juvenile court's finding that Mother was a perpetrator of child abuse under 23 Pa.C.S. § 6303(b.1)”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4714156/in-the-interest-of-nb-a-appeal-of-ea/
- **Heinrich v. Conemaugh Valley Memorial Hospital**, 648 A.2d 53, 436 Pa. Super. 465 (1994)

  Why here: An emergency-room presentation of unexplained swelling in a seven-month-old was enough to make the medical report a good-faith one as a matter of law.
  Quote: “We cannot fault Appellees, given the criminal penalty imposed for failing to report suspected child abuse, from making a report under these circumstances.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1523464/heinrich-v-conemaugh-valley-memorial-hospital/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **In re Fortieth Statewide Investigating Grand Jury**, 190 A.3d 560 (Pa. 2018)
  Holding: The court ordered interim release of the clergy-abuse grand jury report with the challengers' identities redacted, holding that Pennsylvania's constitutional right to reputation requires enhanced procedural protections before a grand jury report publicly condemns named individuals.
  Why here: Shows how a statewide investigative body's findings about institutional child abuse are produced and constrained.
  Quote: “the lines between a grand jury "investigation" and an "adjudication" are blurred when the grand jury renders wide-scale, individualized, condemnatory findings”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6485493/in-re-fortieth-statewide-investigating-grand-jury/
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court reversed, holding that the 'reasonable efforts' state-plan requirement of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act, 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15), confers no privately enforceable right and cannot be sued upon under § 1983 or an implied right of action.
  Why here: Places enforcement of the federal plan requirements with the Secretary, which is why administrative review and audit are the operative oversight tools.
  Quote: “the "reasonable efforts" language does not unambiguously confer an enforceable right upon the Act's beneficiaries”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Allegheny County Department of Administrative Services v. A Second Chance, Inc.**, 13 A.3d 1025 (Pa. Commw. Ct. 2011)
  Holding: The court affirmed in part and vacated in part, holding that the Right-to-Know Law's definition of 'record' is not confined to documents the agency itself created or holds, that agency policy bulletins could not create an exemption, and remanding for findings on the contractor's duties.
  Why here: Public access to contractor records is a practical oversight lever over privatized child-welfare services.
  Quote: “This language does not limit "record" to only those documents "created, received or retained" by the agency.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5144420/allegheny-county-department-of-administrative-services-v-a-second-chance/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **Croft v. Westmoreland County Children & Youth Services**, 103 F.3d 1123 (3d Cir. 1997)
  Holding: Reversing summary judgment for the caseworker, the court held that forcing a father from his home on the basis of an uncorroborated anonymous tip violated the family's substantive due process rights because the caseworker lacked objectively reasonable grounds to suspect abuse.
  Why here: Protective action requires reasonable and articulable evidence of abuse or imminent danger.
  Quote: “we hold that she lacked objectively reasonable grounds to believe the child had been sexually abused or was in imminent danger of sexual abuse”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/732428/croft-v-westmoreland-county-children-and-youth-services/
- **Good v. Dauphin County Social Services for Children & Youth**, 891 F.2d 1087 (3d Cir. 1989)
  Holding: The court reversed summary judgment on qualified immunity, holding that on the mother's version of the facts a caseworker and police officer entered the home and strip-searched the child without valid consent, a warrant, or exigent circumstances, violating clearly established law.
  Why here: Defines the emergency exception: imminent danger of serious bodily injury with intrusion reasonably necessary to avert it.
  Quote: “under these circumstances a reasonable law enforcement officer could not have believed Ms. Good had given legally effective consent to either the entry or the subsequent strip search”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/533797/good-v-dauphin-county-social-services-for-children-and-youth/
- **B.S. v. Somerset County**, 704 F.3d 250 (3d Cir. 2013)
  Holding: The court held that caseworkers' ex parte presentations to the judge that stripped the mother of custody violated procedural due process, but that the individual caseworkers were absolutely immune, and remanded for trial on the county's liability.
  Why here: Distinguishes genuine emergencies, where ex parte action may be tolerated, from non-emergency ex parte contacts.
  Quote: “We agree with Mother that her procedural due process rights were violated by the Appellees, though the individual defendants are protected by absolute immunity.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/814981/bs-ex-rel-ts-v-somerset-county/
- **Bayer v. Monroe County Children & Youth Services**, 577 F.3d 186 (3d Cir. 2009)
  Holding: The court reversed the denial of qualified immunity, holding that even assuming a constitutional right to a post-removal hearing within seventy-two hours, that right was not clearly established such that the Pennsylvania caseworkers' conduct was objectively unreasonable.
  Why here: Addresses the timing of the post-custody hearing that must follow a Pennsylvania protective-custody seizure.
  Quote: “we consider it objectively reasonable for defendants to have believed, under the law existing at the time, that their particular conduct in this case was lawful”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1232403/bayer-v-monroe-county-children-and-youth-services/
- **Miller v. City of Philadelphia**, 174 F.3d 368 (3d Cir. 1999)
  Holding: Affirming judgment for the city and its caseworker, the court held that procedural due process does not require a parent's participation in an emergency ex parte custody hearing, and that a social worker's conduct in separating parent and child violates substantive due process only where it exceeds negligence and deliberate indifference and reaches gross negligence or arbitrariness that shocks the conscience.
  Why here: Explains why the protective-custody process may proceed ex parte without building in a pre-deprivation hearing whenever a parent happens to be available.
  Quote: “the standard of culpability for substantive due process purposes must exceed both negligence and deliberate indifference, and reach a level of gross negligence or arbitrariness that indeed "shocks the conscience."”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/763470/miller-v-city-of-philadelphia/
- **Mulholland v. Government County of Berks, Pennsylvania**, 706 F.3d 227 (3d Cir. 2013)
  Holding: Affirming judgment as a matter of law for the county, the court held that the CPSL's appeal process supplies all the process due to a person listed as an indicated perpetrator, so a county policy of not forwarding exculpatory information to ChildLine is not a constitutional violation, and that the removal rested on objectively reasonable suspicion.
  Why here: Holds that an unrebutted ChildLine listing can supply the reasonable suspicion needed to take children into care from the listed perpetrator's household.
  Quote: “That standard is met if the child is removed without "an objectively reasonable suspicion of abuse," based on the information available at the time.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/816127/randy-mulholland-v-government-county-of-berks/

  Why here: An allegation resting only on uncorroborated hearsay cannot support an indicated finding and must be treated as unproven.
  Quote: “Although less process is due in an administrative proceeding than where criminal charges have been brought, an administrative adjudication of suspected child abuse is of the most serious nature.”

  Holding: The court affirmed in part and vacated in part, holding that the Right-to-Know Law's definition of 'record' is not confined to documents the agency itself created or holds, that agency policy bulletins could not create an exemption, and remanding for findings on the contractor's duties.
  Why here: Holds that a county may not shield child-welfare contractor information by pointing to the contractor's private possession or to internal bulletins.
  Quote: “This language does not limit "record" to only those documents "created, received or retained" by the agency.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5144420/allegheny-county-department-of-administrative-services-v-a-second-chance/
- **In re Fortieth Statewide Investigating Grand Jury**, 190 A.3d 560 (Pa. 2018)
  Holding: The court ordered interim release of the clergy-abuse grand jury report with the challengers' identities redacted, holding that Pennsylvania's constitutional right to reputation requires enhanced procedural protections before a grand jury report publicly condemns named individuals.
  Why here: Balances the public's interest in disclosure of abuse findings against the due-process rights of those named.
  Quote: “the lines between a grand jury "investigation" and an "adjudication" are blurred when the grand jury renders wide-scale, individualized, condemnatory findings”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6485493/in-re-fortieth-statewide-investigating-grand-jury/
- **Support Center for Child Advocates v. Department of Human Services**, 189 A.3d 497 (Pa. Commw. Ct. 2018)
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of the child's guardian ad litem's petition to intervene in the alleged perpetrator's ChildLine expunction appeal, holding the GAL had no statutory right to intervene and no interest not already represented by the Department.
  Why here: Limits third-party access to and participation in the confidential expunction process.
  Quote: “H.M.'s G.A.L. has no right to intervene "conferred by statute," nor has he stated an interest such that his participation "is in the public interest."”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4509917/support-center-for-child-advocates-as-gal-for-the-minor-child-hm-and-hm/
- **Pennsylvania v. Ritchie**, 480 U.S. 39 (1987)
  Holding: Affirming in part and reversing in part, the Court held that due process entitles a criminal defendant to have Pennsylvania's confidential CYS child-abuse file reviewed in camera by the trial court for material exculpatory evidence, but does not entitle defense counsel to search the file itself.
  Why here: The governing decision on when Pennsylvania's confidentiality of CYS child-abuse records must yield to a competing constitutional right of access, and on the in camera mechanism for resolving that clash.
  Quote: “Although we recognize that the public interest in protecting this type of sensitive information is strong, we do not agree that this interest necessarily prevents disclosure in all circumstances.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/111822/pennsylvania-v-ritchie/
- **R. v. Department of Public Welfare**, 535 Pa. 440, 636 A.2d 142 (1994)
  Holding: Affirming the Commonwealth Court, the court held that an alleged perpetrator was not denied due process at a ChildLine expunction hearing where the hearing examiner who decided the case had not observed all the witnesses and the child testified in camera subject to cross-examination, because the CPSL narrowly limits who may learn of an indicated report.
  Why here: Grounds the constitutionality of the indicated-report scheme in the statute's narrow limits on who may receive registry information.
  Quote: “we conclude that R. received all the process he was due under the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2207723/r-v-com-dept-of-public-welfare/
- **Mulholland v. Government County of Berks, Pennsylvania**, 706 F.3d 227 (3d Cir. 2013)
  Holding: Affirming judgment as a matter of law for the county, the court held that the CPSL's appeal process supplies all the process due to a person listed as an indicated perpetrator, so a county policy of not forwarding exculpatory information to ChildLine is not a constitutional violation, and that the removal rested on objectively reasonable suspicion.
  Why here: Rejects a constitutional duty on a county agency to forward recantations and other exculpatory information to the statewide registry after its investigation closes, leaving correction to the CPSL appeal.
  Quote: “That standard is met if the child is removed without "an objectively reasonable suspicion of abuse," based on the information available at the time.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/816127/randy-mulholland-v-government-county-of-berks/

## PMC-01 · CFSR Monitoring
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court reversed, holding that the 'reasonable efforts' state-plan requirement of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act, 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15), confers no privately enforceable right and cannot be sued upon under § 1983 or an implied right of action.
  Why here: Explains why the CFSR and the Secretary's funding leverage, rather than lawsuits, drive federal performance monitoring.
  Quote: “the "reasonable efforts" language does not unambiguously confer an enforceable right upon the Act's beneficiaries”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **In the Interest of D.C.D.**, 105 A.3d 662, 629 Pa. 325 (2014)
  Holding: Reversing the Superior Court and reinstating termination, the court held that an agency's failure to provide reasonable efforts toward reunification does not bar the granting of an otherwise properly proven petition to terminate parental rights, although courts may enforce reasonable efforts by other means.
  Why here: Illustrates the gap between federal reasonable-efforts monitoring and enforceable rights in individual Pennsylvania cases.
  Quote: “refusing a properly proven termination of parental rights petition, and thus harming an innocent child, is not among them”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2760970/in-the-int-of-dcd-appeal-of-clinton-co-cys/
- **In the Interest of R.J.T.**, 9 A.3d 1179, 608 Pa. 9 (2010)
  Holding: The court reversed the Superior Court and reinstated the trial court's denial of a goal change, holding that dependency rulings are reviewed only for abuse of discretion and that fifteen months in placement under 42 Pa.C.S. § 6351(f)(9) is one factor, not a litmus test requiring a goal change.
  Why here: Shows how federal permanency timelines were absorbed into Pennsylvania practice as discretionary factors subject to judicial review.
  Quote: “(f)(9) is merely one of a number of factors a trial court must consider in ultimately determining whether the current placement is appropriate”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5144340/in-the-interest-of-rjt/
- **In re Adoption of S.E.G.**, 901 A.2d 1017, 587 Pa. 568 (2006)
  Holding: Affirming the Superior Court, the court held that a court-ordered change of the permanency goal is not a condition precedent to an agency's filing of a petition to terminate parental rights, consistent with ASFA's endorsement of concurrent planning.
  Why here: Traces the federal permanency framework into Pennsylvania's statutory scheme reviewed in the CFSR.
  Quote: “We conclude that Section 6351 does not require that a goal change precede the filing of a termination petition.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1998401/in-re-adoption-of-seg/

- Missing (for a later pass): In re Adoption of C.M., 255 A.3d 343 (Pa. 2021) — the Supreme Court's controlling restatement of the § 2511(a)(1) parental-duties analysis and the clear-and-convincing burden. - Missing (for a later pass): In re Adoption of Charles E.D.M., II, 708 A.2d 88 (Pa. 1998) — the canonical Pennsylvania formulation of 'clear, direct, weighty and convincing' evidence in involuntary termination, and the source C.M. and L.A.K. both cite for it. It is the PA-law counterpart to Santosky's federal floor at OUT-02, which currently has no PA case stating the standard in its own terms.
- Missing (for a later pass): Lassiter v. Department of Social Services, 452 U.S. 18 (1981) — the Supreme Court's holding that due process does not categorically require appointed counsel for an indigent parent in a termination proceeding, with case-by-case determination. OUT-02 carries an elaborate treatment of the CHILD's right to counsel (L.B.M., T.S., K.M.G.) and only In re Adoption of T.M.F. on the parent's side; Lassiter is the constitutional baseline against which Pennsylvania's broader statutory right to parental counsel (42 Pa.C.S. § 6337; 23 Pa.C.S. § 2313(a.1)) operates, and its absence leaves the parent-counsel line of the node without a foundation.
- Missing (for a later pass): Commonwealth v. Lynn, 114 A.3d 796 (Pa. 2015) — the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania's decision that a supervisory official who is not the child's direct caregiver may be convicted of endangering the welfare of children under 18 Pa.C.S. § 4304. It is the leading Pennsylvania authority on institutional and supervisory failure to protect children from a known abuser, and is directly relevant to INP-01 (consequences of non-reporting by professionals), INP-09 (institutional investigations) and FAIL-04. The brief flags an unclosed gap on the criminal consequences of failing to act on suspected abuse; Lynn is the closest Pennsylvania Supreme Court authority in that space.
- Missing (for a later pass): Mammaro v. New Jersey Division of Child Protection & Permanency, 814 F.3d 164 (3d Cir. 2016) — binding Third Circuit authority holding that the right to familial integrity was not clearly established so as to defeat qualified immunity for a temporary removal, and that temporary removals are analyzed differently from the permanent separations in Croft. It is the most recent circuit word on qualified immunity in removal cases and materially qualifies how Croft, B.S. and Miller are applied at DEC-04, INP-12 and FAIL-02, all of which currently present pre-2013 authority as the operative law.
