## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Myers v. Lashley**, 2002 OK 14, 44 P.3d 553
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for a psychologist who reported suspected child abuse, holding the statutory good-faith reporting privilege bars every theory of recovery for harm flowing from the report unless the plaintiff pierces it with proof of bad faith.
  Why here: Myers is the controlling Oklahoma authority on the scope of the mandatory reporter's statutory immunity and on what a plaintiff must show to overcome it.
  Quote: “The broad statutory privilege extends across all cognizable theories of liability.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2543380/myers-v-lashley/
- **West v. Jane Phillips Memorial Medical Center**, 2017 OK CIV APP 52, 404 P.3d 896
  Holding: The court affirmed statutory reporting immunity for all harm flowing from a hospital's mandated report of a false-positive drug screen after childbirth, but reversed as to claims for harm unconnected to the report, such as negligent pre-testing protocols and alleged harassment.
  Why here: It draws the operative line for mandatory reporters: immunity covers post-reporting harm only, not independently negligent conduct that produced the underlying information.
  Quote: “The grant of summary judgment in Defendants' favor for harm not connected with the report to DHS was in error and is REVERSED.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5345649/west-v-jane-phillips-memorial-medical-center/
- **Kremeier v. Transitions, Inc.**, 2015 OK CIV APP 18, 345 P.3d 1126
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for a counselor who reported that her own patient posed a threat of sexual abuse to his daughter, holding negligence or even gross negligence in making the report does not pierce the statutory good-faith immunity, which also covers reports of threatened rather than completed abuse.
  Why here: Kremeier is the Oklahoma decision holding that 10A O.S. § 1-2-104 immunity covers reports of predicted or threatened abuse, not only abuse already occurring.
  Quote: “Although Dr. Kishur's affidavit may support a finding of negligence or even gross negligence, we conclude it fails to provide support for a finding of bad faith.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2825583/kremeier-v-transitions-inc/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Daniels v. State (In re C.D.P.F.)**, 2010 OK 81, 243 P.3d 21
  Holding: The court reinstated a jury verdict terminating a mother's parental rights, holding that clear and convincing evidence showed she failed to correct the substance-abuse conditions and that termination served the child's best interests.
  Why here: The case began with a joint DHS and police entry into a suspected drug house, the classic law-enforcement referral pathway into an Oklahoma deprived case.
  Quote: “In parental termination cases, the State must show by clear and convincing evidence that the child's best interest is served by the termination of parental rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5326214/daniels-v-state/
- **Halley v. Huckaby**, 902 F.3d 1136 (10th Cir. 2018)
  Holding: In a suit arising from Oklahoma DHS's seizure and questioning of a child at school, the Tenth Circuit affirmed the denial of qualified immunity to the DHS specialist and the sheriff's deputy on the Fourth Amendment claim, reversed as to the officer who merely transported the child, and reversed on the familial-association claim as not clearly established.
  Why here: The seizure was executed jointly by a DHS worker and law enforcement, and the court parses each actor's independent duty to assess suspicion.
  Quote: “Unless officials have judicial authorization, however, they cannot seize a child without at least having reasonable suspicion of imminent danger.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4530346/halley-v-huckaby/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **State v. Gurley**, 2012 OK CIV APP 52, 276 P.3d 1114
  Holding: The court affirmed a deprived adjudication, holding the State could re-remove and refile after the first case failed for want of a petition within seven days, that holding the emergency custody hearing on the third judicial day violated the two-day rule but did not divest jurisdiction or deny due process, and that competent evidence supported the adjudication.
  Why here: The case maps the sequence of court orders, from ex parte emergency custody order through show-cause hearing, that judicial actors control.
  Quote: “The burden of proof is on the State to demonstrate the factual basis for the deprived status adjudication by a preponderance of the evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5327142/state-v-gurley/

  Why here: The report here arose out of a contested custody matter, and Myers holds that the litigation context does not itself supply bad faith.

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In the Matter of the Guardianship of M.R.S.**, 1998 OK 38, 960 P.2d 357
  Holding: The court reversed the denial of a never-unfit natural father's application to end a non-relative guardianship, holding the post-decree change-of-custody test does not apply and that custody may be withheld from a parent only on clear and convincing proof of unfitness.
  Why here: When a parent voluntarily hands care to a guardian rather than to the agency, M.R.S. governs the terms on which the parent can get the child back.
  Quote: “for custody to be taken from the parent there must be a showing, by clear and convincing evidence, of unfitness of the parent”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5335633/in-the-matter-of-the-guardianship-of-mrs/
- **Ovalle v. Salinas-Cardosi**, 2003 OK 1, 65 P.3d 587
  Holding: The court reversed the termination of a maternal grandmother's guardianship, holding the mother had not carried her burden of showing both that the impediment to her custody was gone and that ending the guardianship would not be inimical to the child's welfare.
  Why here: Ovalle is the Oklahoma template for the parent-initiated placement with kin and for what the parent must later prove to undo it.
  Quote: “When the impediment to the natural parent's custody has been removed, the guardianship is no longer necessary unless its termination would be inimical to the welfare of the child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5321903/ovalle-v-salinas-cardosi/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Vandelay Entertainment, LLC v. Fallin**, 2014 OK 109, 343 P.3d 1273
  Holding: The court held the Governor holds a constitutionally grounded deliberative-process privilege that can withhold records from an Open Records Act request, but that the privilege is qualified and enforceable only through in camera judicial review with the burden on the executive.
  Why here: Oversight bodies seeking executive-branch child-welfare deliberations will meet this privilege and must invoke in camera review.
  Quote: “This privilege is not absolute, however, and is subject to the check and balance of in camera judicial review, in lieu of legislatively-mandated public disclosure.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2761882/vandelay-entertainment-llc-v-fallin/
- **Deal v. Brooks**, 2016 OK CIV APP 81, 389 P.3d 375
  Holding: In an action arising from the murder of a child DHS had placed with her biological father, the court affirmed that the Governmental Tort Claims Act's child-placement exemption bars tort claims against DHS, but reversed and remanded for trial on a state constitutional due-process claim that DHS employees acted in reckless disregard of a known risk.
  Why here: The case is the clearest Oklahoma appellate record of what an after-the-fact review of a fatal placement decision looks like.
  Quote: “the Legislature, under 155(29), has exempted DHS from tort liability under the GTCA”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5345433/deal-v-brooks/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that "domicile" in the Indian Child Welfare Act has a uniform federal meaning, that children born off-reservation to reservation-domiciled parents are domiciled on the reservation, and that exclusive tribal jurisdiction under § 1911(a) therefore applied notwithstanding the parents' voluntary off-reservation surrender.
  Why here: Holyfield is the controlling federal decision on when ICWA strips a state court of jurisdiction altogether, a live question in Oklahoma given its many tribes.
  Quote: “Tribal jurisdiction under § 1911(a) was not meant to be defeated by the actions of individual members of the tribe”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **In the Guardianship of C.H.S.**, 2016 OK CIV APP 72, 394 P.3d 278
  Holding: The court reversed the denial of the Cherokee Nation's motion to transfer a guardianship of an Indian child to tribal court and ordered the transfer, because the guardians opposing transfer did not carry their clear-and-convincing burden of proving good cause to keep the case in state court.
  Why here: C.H.S. is the current Oklahoma statement of the burden and standard governing tribal transfer motions, including in guardianship cases.
  Quote: “We conclude the Guardians did not provide clear and convincing evidence of good cause for the trial court to decline Cherokee Nation's request to transfer jurisdiction to tribal court.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4324117/in-the-guardianship-of-chs/
- **State v. Givens (In re S.F.)**, 2010 OK CIV APP 5, 230 P.3d 911
  Holding: The court reversed an order transferring a deprived proceeding to tribal court as to one child, holding ICWA § 1911(b) gives either parent an unconditional veto over transfer and that the objecting father need not show good cause for his objection.
  Why here: This is the controlling Oklahoma decision on parental objection to transfer of jurisdiction to a tribal court under ICWA § 1911(b).
  Quote: “The trial court erred in directing the transfer of the case as to S.F. over Wood's objection.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5326019/state-v-givens/
- **Clark v. State (In re J.C.)**, 2018 OK CIV APP 32, 417 P.3d 1218
  Holding: The court vacated a deprived adjudication as to a father because the trial court treated the mother's no-contest stipulation as binding on him and denied the non-jury trial he requested, and remanded for a trial meeting ICWA standards.
  Why here: On remand the adjudication must satisfy ICWA's clear-and-convincing, qualified-expert-witness standard for foster care placement of an Indian child.
  Quote: “We therefore find that the trial court committed reversible error in denying Father's request for a non-jury trial”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6656490/clark-v-state/
- **Smith v. State (In re T.S.)**, 2013 OK CIV APP 108, 315 P.3d 1030
  Holding: The court held that placing Indian children with their mothers after emergency removal from the father was a foster care placement triggering ICWA § 1912(d), and affirmed the finding that the State made active efforts by clear and convincing evidence before adjudication.
  Why here: The opinion construes ICWA's definitions to reach placements with a non-custodial parent, expanding when tribal protections attach.
  Quote: “We find no reason why the same case-by-case review should not be applied when deciding if State has made the required active efforts in this foster care placement.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5328985/smith-v-state/
- **In the Matter of M.H.C.**, 2016 OK 88
  Holding: The court affirmed the transfer of a deprived case to Cherokee Nation tribal court, holding ICWA became applicable prospectively when the mother enrolled and that the State and foster mother failed to prove good cause by clear and convincing evidence to keep the case in state court.
  Why here: M.H.C. is the controlling Oklahoma Supreme Court authority on ICWA transfer motions, including when ICWA attaches mid-case, and is the decision C.H.S. applies.
  Quote: “When ruling on a motion to transfer jurisdiction to tribal court, the party opposing transfer has to prove good cause to keep the case in state court by clear-and-convincing evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4258132/in-the-matter-of-mhc/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **In the Matter of K.G. v. Department of Human Services**, 2002 OK CIV APP 65, 48 P.3d 131
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of foster grandparents' objection to DHS's emergency removal of their foster child, holding that the reasonable-suspicion standard governs whether an emergency justified physically removing a child from a foster home.
  Why here: The opinion adopts a lesser, reasonable-suspicion showing for emergency action precisely because a prompt hearing follows.
  Quote: “the trial court is faced with an inquiry into whether an emergency exists to justify physical removal of the child from an existing foster home, the reasonable suspicion standard applies”

  Why here: Gomes fixes the substantive standard and the post-removal hearing duty that govern Oklahoma protective custody as a matter of federal due process.
  Quote: “even in these instances in which emergency removal is justified, the state must afford the parents a prompt post-removal hearing”

## DEC-01 · Screen-Out / Unfounded at Intake
- **Deal v. Brooks**, 2016 OK CIV APP 81, 389 P.3d 375
  Holding: In an action arising from the murder of a child DHS had placed with her biological father, the court affirmed that the Governmental Tort Claims Act's child-placement exemption bars tort claims against DHS, but reversed and remanded for trial on a state constitutional due-process claim that DHS employees acted in reckless disregard of a known risk.
  Why here: It shows the consequences when DHS possesses risk information and does not act on it before finalizing a placement.
  Quote: “the Legislature, under 155(29), has exempted DHS from tort liability under the GTCA”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5345433/deal-v-brooks/
- **T.D. v. Patton**, 868 F.3d 1209 (10th Cir. 2017)
  Holding: The Tenth Circuit affirmed the denial of qualified immunity to a caseworker who recommended placing and keeping a child in his father's custody and failed to investigate mounting evidence that the father was abusing him, holding this violated clearly established state-created-danger law.
  Why here: Screening out or closing on known abuse indicators can itself be the constitutional violation where the agency has already placed the child.
  Quote: “Ms. Patton's conduct violated T.D.'s clearly established substantive due process right under Currier to be free from state-created danger.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4422214/td-v-patton/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that a state child-protection agency that knew of and investigated abuse but left the child in his father's home did not violate the Due Process Clause, because the Clause imposes no affirmative duty to protect a person from private violence absent a custodial relationship.
  Why here: DeShaney is the reason a screen-out or unfounded closure, standing alone, rarely produces federal liability no matter how foreseeable the later harm.
  Quote: “nothing in the language of the Due Process Clause itself requires the State to protect the life, liberty, and property of its citizens against invasion by private actors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-cty-dss/
- **Mathews v. Eldridge**, 424 U.S. 319 (1976)
  Holding: The Court held that an evidentiary hearing is not required before termination of Social Security disability benefits, and announced that what process is due is determined by weighing the private interest affected, the risk of erroneous deprivation and the value of added safeguards, and the Government's interest including fiscal and administrative burdens.
  Why here: Whether a screening or unfounded-closure decision must carry any process at all is answered by the Mathews balance, not by any Oklahoma decision.
  Quote: “identification of the specific dictates of due process generally requires consideration of three distinct factors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/109382/mathews-v-eldridge/

## DEC-03 · Substantiated / Founded Finding
- **In re C.G.**, 1981 OK 131, 637 P.2d 66
  Holding: The court reversed a termination of parental rights because the trial court never prescribed written norms of parental conduct the father was expected to meet, and it announced prospectively that a termination-seeking party must prove parental potential for harm by clear and convincing evidence.
  Why here: A substantiated finding and deprived adjudication alone do not give a parent notice of what must change; C.G. requires judicially specified conduct norms before that finding can support later action.
  Quote: “Judicial clarity in the prescribed norms of parental conduct is essential to the preservation of the procedural safeguards mandated by state and federal due process.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5327033/in-re-c-g/
- **Daniels v. State (In re C.D.P.F.)**, 2010 OK 81, 243 P.3d 21
  Holding: The court reinstated a jury verdict terminating a mother's parental rights, holding that clear and convincing evidence showed she failed to correct the substance-abuse conditions and that termination served the child's best interests.
  Why here: Daniels states the standard of proof and the appellate canvass that a substantiated-conditions theory must ultimately satisfy.
  Quote: “In parental termination cases, the State must show by clear and convincing evidence that the child's best interest is served by the termination of parental rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5326214/daniels-v-state/
- **Gordon v. State (In re G.G.)**, 2004 OK CIV APP 71, 97 P.3d 1155
  Holding: The court reversed a termination judgment and remanded for new trial, holding that a parent's stipulation entered at the preponderance-level adjudication stage cannot be given preclusive effect on the elements the State must later prove by clear and convincing evidence.
  Why here: Gordon holds that an agency-substantiated, parent-stipulated adjudication is not an admission that survives into the higher-burden termination phase.
  Quote: “we view the recited stipulation as reflecting only Mother's concession that State could prove the allegations of the deprived petition by a preponderance of the evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5322863/gordon-v-state/

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2380270/in-re-btw/

## OUT-02 · Termination of Parental Rights / Adoption
- **In re C.G.**, 1981 OK 131, 637 P.2d 66
  Holding: The court reversed a termination of parental rights because the trial court never prescribed written norms of parental conduct the father was expected to meet, and it announced prospectively that a termination-seeking party must prove parental potential for harm by clear and convincing evidence.
  Why here: C.G. is the source of Oklahoma's clear-and-convincing standard for termination and of the pre-termination notice requirement that governs every TPR appeal.
  Quote: “Judicial clarity in the prescribed norms of parental conduct is essential to the preservation of the procedural safeguards mandated by state and federal due process.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5327033/in-re-c-g/
- **A.E. v. State**, 1987 OK 76, 743 P.2d 1041
  Holding: The court held that a parent whose child has been adjudicated deprived is entitled to a jury trial in a State-initiated proceeding to terminate parental rights under Okla. Const. art. 2, § 19, overruling contrary prior decisions.
  Why here: A.E. is the foundational Oklahoma holding that a parent facing State-sought termination has a constitutional right to trial by jury.
  Quote: “We find that parental rights are too precious to be terminated without the full panoply of protections afforded by the Oklahoma Constitution.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5331066/ae-v-state/
- **Daniels v. State (In re C.D.P.F.)**, 2010 OK 81, 243 P.3d 21
  Holding: The court reinstated a jury verdict terminating a mother's parental rights, holding that clear and convincing evidence showed she failed to correct the substance-abuse conditions and that termination served the child's best interests.
  Why here: Daniels is the modern Oklahoma Supreme Court restatement of the clear-and-convincing best-interests standard for termination.
  Quote: “In parental termination cases, the State must show by clear and convincing evidence that the child's best interest is served by the termination of parental rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5326214/daniels-v-state/
- **In the Matter of the Adoption of M.A.S.**, 2018 OK 1, 419 P.3d 204
  Holding: The court reversed an order declaring a child eligible for adoption without the biological father's consent because the record lacked clear and convincing evidence that his partial nonpayment of support was willful or that he had the ability to pay in full.
  Why here: M.A.S. is the current Oklahoma Supreme Court statement of the proof required to sever a parent's consent right and clear the path to a stepparent adoption.
  Quote: “unless the trial court's determination rests on clear and convincing evidence, that determination will be reversed”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4459990/in-the-matter-of-the-adoption-of-mas/
- **State v. Khan**, 2008 OK CIV APP 104, 198 P.3d 415
  Holding: The court reversed the termination of an incarcerated father's parental rights because the record contained no clear and convincing evidence supporting the statutory grounds alleged against him.
  Why here: Khan is the Oklahoma authority for the proposition that incarceration alone, without record proof of the statutory elements, cannot terminate parental rights.
  Quote: “We hold that the record does not include clear and convincing evidence supporting termination in this case and reverse.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5325571/state-v-khan/
- **Santosky v. Kramer**, 455 U.S. 745 (1982)
  Holding: The Court held that New York's fair-preponderance standard for permanent neglect proceedings violates the Due Process Clause, and that before a State may irrevocably sever the rights of parents in their natural child it must support its allegations by at least clear and convincing evidence.
  Why here: It is the federal constitutional floor for every termination of parental rights, including those litigated under Oklahoma's Children's Code.
  Quote: “due process requires that the State support its allegations by at least clear and convincing evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/110676/santosky-v-kramer/
- **In the Matter of A.M. and R.W.**, 2000 OK 82, 13 P.3d 484
  Holding: The court affirmed a jury termination verdict, reviewing the procedural due process claim de novo and holding that a parent may be excluded from the courtroom during a child's testimony only on a showing at a hearing of significant emotional harm, but that this mother showed no impairment of her defense.
  Why here: A.M. and R.W. supplies the Oklahoma Supreme Court's de novo standard of review for procedural due process challenges to a termination trial and the limits on excluding a parent from the child's testimony.
  Quote: “a parent should not be excluded from the courtroom absent a showing at a hearing that the child would suffer significant emotional harm caused by the parent's presence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2585339/in-re-am/
- **In re Adoption of K.P.M.A.**, 2014 OK 85, 341 P.3d 38
  Holding: The court reversed the termination of an unwed father's parental rights, holding he was denied due process because he was never given adequate notice of the pregnancy and birth and there was no clear and convincing evidence supporting termination under the adoption statute.
  Why here: K.P.M.A. is the controlling Oklahoma Supreme Court authority on the notice a biological father is constitutionally owed before his consent right can be severed to clear an adoption.
  Quote: “Father had a right, under the Due Process Clause, to notice of the fact that Mother was pregnant with his child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2742197/in-re-adoption-of-kpma/
- **In the Matter of the Adoption of R.W.S.**, 1997 OK 148, 951 P.2d 83
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of a non-relative couple's adoption petition, holding that where a court order deferred the father's child support there was no clear and convincing evidence of a willful failure to support that would make the child eligible for adoption without his consent.
  Why here: R.W.S. is part of the controlling Oklahoma Supreme Court line fixing the clear-and-convincing burden and the willfulness element before a father's consent right can be severed for nonsupport.
  Quote: “The standard of proof in determining a child's eligibility for adoption without parental consent is clear and convincing evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2611413/matter-of-adoption-of-rws/

## OUT-03 · Guardianship / Kinship Permanency
- **In the Matter of the Guardianship of M.R.S.**, 1998 OK 38, 960 P.2d 357
  Holding: The court reversed the denial of a never-unfit natural father's application to end a non-relative guardianship, holding the post-decree change-of-custody test does not apply and that custody may be withheld from a parent only on clear and convincing proof of unfitness.
  Why here: M.R.S. is the controlling Oklahoma statement of the burden and presumption that apply when guardianship is used as a permanency arrangement.
  Quote: “for custody to be taken from the parent there must be a showing, by clear and convincing evidence, of unfitness of the parent”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5335633/in-the-matter-of-the-guardianship-of-mrs/
- **Ovalle v. Salinas-Cardosi**, 2003 OK 1, 65 P.3d 587
  Holding: The court reversed the termination of a maternal grandmother's guardianship, holding the mother had not carried her burden of showing both that the impediment to her custody was gone and that ending the guardianship would not be inimical to the child's welfare.
  Why here: Ovalle supplies the two-part test that decides whether a kinship guardianship stays in place as the child's permanency outcome.
  Quote: “When the impediment to the natural parent's custody has been removed, the guardianship is no longer necessary unless its termination would be inimical to the welfare of the child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5321903/ovalle-v-salinas-cardosi/
- **In the Matter of the Adoption of S.A.H.**, 2022 OK 10, 503 P.3d 1190
  Holding: The court affirmed the grandparents' adoption and the dismissal of a cousin's competing guardianship claim, holding the cousin had no statutory or constitutional right to custody of, or to notice of the adoption of, the child.
  Why here: It holds that an adoption decree extinguishes a pending guardianship claim by a relative, which determines how kinship permanency competitions end.
  Quote: “Cousin had no right--by statute or constitution--to notice of Grandparents' adoption.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8242628/in-the-matter-of-the-adoption-of-sah/
- **In the Guardianship of C.H.S.**, 2016 OK CIV APP 72, 394 P.3d 278
  Holding: The court reversed the denial of the Cherokee Nation's motion to transfer a guardianship of an Indian child to tribal court and ordered the transfer, because the guardians opposing transfer did not carry their clear-and-convincing burden of proving good cause to keep the case in state court.
  Why here: It holds that a guardianship of an Indian child is subject to ICWA transfer even after the guardianship has been in place for years.
  Quote: “We conclude the Guardians did not provide clear and convincing evidence of good cause for the trial court to decline Cherokee Nation's request to transfer jurisdiction to tribal court.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4324117/in-the-guardianship-of-chs/
- **Katherine E. v. State, Department of Human Services**, 2011 OK CIV APP 3, 246 P.3d 1115
  Holding: The court reversed the dismissal of out-of-state grandparents' adoption petition, holding they had standing after the parents' rights were terminated and that DHS's statutory power to consent to an adoption does not displace the court's duty to hear them on the child's best interests.
  Why here: Katherine E. defines grandparents' standing to seek kinship permanency once parental rights have been terminated.
  Quote: “the trial court erred when it dismissed Grandparents' adoption petition based on the best interests of the child, without providing Grandparents an opportunity to be heard”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5326259/katherine-e-v-state-department-of-human-services/
- **Muggenborg v. Kessler**, 1981 OK 66, 630 P.2d 1276
  Holding: The court granted a writ prohibiting enforcement of an adoption decree obtained ex parte by one grandparent, holding that grandparents whose custodial claims stand co-equal with the petitioner's were affected or interested parties constitutionally entitled to notice and an opportunity to be heard.
  Why here: It establishes that once the parental bond is gone a grandparent has standing to claim custody and must be heard before kinship permanency is awarded to someone else.
  Quote: “Our view is, and we hold, that by the inexorable command of both the state and federal fundamental law notice to these petitioners was indeed their constitutional due.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1167062/muggenborg-v-kessler/

## OUT-04 · Extended Foster Care / Aging Out
- **In the Matter of B.T.W.**, 2008 OK 80, 195 P.3d 896
  Holding: The court affirmed orders continuing visitation with a reunification goal and directing a change of foster placement, finding no abuse of discretion in the trial court's weighing of conflicting evidence about the child's best interests.
  Why here: The case illustrates the court's continuing supervisory power over placement and visitation while the youth remains in agency custody.
  Quote: “In making such decisions, the paramount consideration remains the best interests of the child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2636500/in-the-matter-of-btw/
- **In the Matter of K.G. v. Department of Human Services**, 2002 OK CIV APP 65, 48 P.3d 131
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of foster grandparents' objection to DHS's emergency removal of their foster child, holding that the reasonable-suspicion standard governs whether an emergency justified physically removing a child from a foster home.
  Why here: It defines the court's role in policing placement stability for a child who remains in long-term agency custody.
  Quote: “the trial court is faced with an inquiry into whether an emergency exists to justify physical removal of the child from an existing foster home, the reasonable suspicion standard applies”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5321404/kg-v-department-of-human-services/
- **In the Matter of B.T.W.**, 2010 OK 69, 241 P.3d 199
  Holding: In the second B.T.W. appeal the court affirmed a post-appeal permanency order that kept the child with her foster mother, limited the mother to supervised visitation and changed the permanency plan to long-term out-of-home placement, finding reasonable efforts had been made and no abuse of discretion or due process violation.
  Why here: The case is the Oklahoma Supreme Court's approval of long-term out-of-home placement as a permanency plan when reunification is no longer realistic.
  Quote: “A trial court's findings concerning visitation and placement of a child previously adjudicated as deprived are matters of equitable cognizance.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2380270/in-re-btw/

## OUT-05 · Case Dismissal
- **State v. Khan**, 2008 OK CIV APP 104, 198 P.3d 415
  Holding: The court reversed the termination of an incarcerated father's parental rights because the record contained no clear and convincing evidence supporting the statutory grounds alleged against him.
  Why here: The reversal ends the State's termination case against this parent for want of proof.
  Quote: “We hold that the record does not include clear and convincing evidence supporting termination in this case and reverse.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5325571/state-v-khan/
- **State v. Gurley**, 2012 OK CIV APP 52, 276 P.3d 1114

## OUT-06 · Child Fatality / Serious Injury
- **Deal v. Brooks**, 2016 OK CIV APP 81, 389 P.3d 375
  Holding: In an action arising from the murder of a child DHS had placed with her biological father, the court affirmed that the Governmental Tort Claims Act's child-placement exemption bars tort claims against DHS, but reversed and remanded for trial on a state constitutional due-process claim that DHS employees acted in reckless disregard of a known risk.
  Why here: Deal is the leading Oklahoma authority on civil liability following a child fatality connected to an agency placement decision.
  Quote: “the Legislature, under 155(29), has exempted DHS from tort liability under the GTCA”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5345433/deal-v-brooks/
- **T.D. v. Patton**, 868 F.3d 1209 (10th Cir. 2017)
  Holding: The Tenth Circuit affirmed the denial of qualified immunity to a caseworker who recommended placing and keeping a child in his father's custody and failed to investigate mounting evidence that the father was abusing him, holding this violated clearly established state-created-danger law.
  Why here: The case sets the Tenth Circuit framework for serious-injury claims arising out of an agency-directed placement.
  Quote: “Ms. Patton's conduct violated T.D.'s clearly established substantive due process right under Currier to be free from state-created danger.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4422214/td-v-patton/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that a state child-protection agency that knew of and investigated abuse but left the child in his father's home did not violate the Due Process Clause, because the Clause imposes no affirmative duty to protect a person from private violence absent a custodial relationship.
  Why here: It is the starting point for any claim arising from a fatality or serious injury to a child the agency knew about but did not remove.
  Quote: “nothing in the language of the Due Process Clause itself requires the State to protect the life, liberty, and property of its citizens against invasion by private actors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-cty-dss/
- **Bosh v. Cherokee County Building Authority**, 2013 OK 9, 305 P.3d 994
  Holding: Answering certified questions, the court held that Okla. Const. art. 2, section 30 supplies a private damages action for excessive force notwithstanding the Governmental Tort Claims Act, that the action applies retrospectively to cases then in the pipeline, and that respondeat superior governs municipal liability for it.
  Why here: It is the origin of the only Oklahoma damages route around governmental immunity for a child seriously harmed or killed in state custody or state-directed placement.
  Quote: “The Okla. Const. art 2, § 30 provides a private cause of action for excessive force, notwithstanding the limitations of the Oklahoma Governmental Tort Claims Act”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5328543/bosh-v-cherokee-county-building-authority/
- **Barrios v. Haskell County Public Facilities Authority**, 2018 OK 90, 432 P.3d 233
  Holding: Answering certified questions, the court declined to extend Bosh and held that because the Legislature amended the Governmental Tort Claims Act in 2014 to define constitutional violations as torts subject to the Act, article II sections 7 and 9 do not allow an inmate to sue the State for denial of medical care.
  Why here: It shows the Legislature's power to close the damages remedy for harm suffered in state custody, the same power that produced the child-placement exemption in Deal.
  Quote: “these "constitutional" torts are now clearly "torts" governed by the GTCA”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6657866/barrios-v-haskell-cnty-pub-facilities-auth/

- **Burrell v. State**, 2013 OK CIV APP 13, 295 P.3d 603
  Holding: The court remanded a termination appeal for an evidentiary hearing on the mother's claim that trial counsel failed to interview or subpoena witnesses, adopting a record-supplementation procedure for ineffective-assistance claims in parental-rights cases.

## FAIL-02 · Unlawful Removal
- **State v. Gurley**, 2012 OK CIV APP 52, 276 P.3d 1114
  Holding: The court affirmed a deprived adjudication, holding the State could re-remove and refile after the first case failed for want of a petition within seven days, that holding the emergency custody hearing on the third judicial day violated the two-day rule but did not divest jurisdiction or deny due process, and that competent evidence supported the adjudication.
  Why here: Gurley is the Oklahoma decision squarely holding that a statutory timing violation in emergency custody does not itself make the ensuing custody unlawful.
  Quote: “The burden of proof is on the State to demonstrate the factual basis for the deprived status adjudication by a preponderance of the evidence.”

  Why here: This is the leading § 1983 wrongful-removal precedent binding on Oklahoma federal courts.

  Why here: A removal that fails the reasonable-suspicion test, or that is not followed by a prompt hearing, is actionable under § 1983 in this circuit.
  Quote: “even in these instances in which emergency removal is justified, the state must afford the parents a prompt post-removal hearing”

  Why here: Halley confirms that an Oklahoma DHS worker who seizes a child without reasonable suspicion of imminent danger is not entitled to qualified immunity.
  Quote: “Unless officials have judicial authorization, however, they cannot seize a child without at least having reasonable suspicion of imminent danger.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4530346/halley-v-huckaby/

## FAIL-03 · Federal Compliance Failure
- **Martinez-Mendoza v. State (In re C.M.)**, 2018 OK 93, 432 P.3d 763
  Holding: The court affirmed termination, holding DHS made reasonable reunification efforts despite the absence of visitation the children refused, that clear and convincing evidence supported the jury's verdict, and that trial counsel was effective.

- **Smith v. State (In re T.S.)**, 2013 OK CIV APP 108, 315 P.3d 1030
  Holding: The court held that placing Indian children with their mothers after emergency removal from the father was a foster care placement triggering ICWA § 1912(d), and affirmed the finding that the State made active efforts by clear and convincing evidence before adjudication.
  Why here: Failing to apply § 1912(d) to a parent placement is the kind of ICWA compliance gap this decision corrects.

  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of a new trial, holding ICWA's active-efforts requirement did not apply because no existing Indian family relationship would be broken up, and alternatively that clear and convincing evidence showed active efforts were made and had failed.
  Why here: It applies Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl in Oklahoma and defines when an active-efforts omission is not a compliance failure at all.

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5345125/simmers-v-state/
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that "domicile" in the Indian Child Welfare Act has a uniform federal meaning, that children born off-reservation to reservation-domiciled parents are domiciled on the reservation, and that exclusive tribal jurisdiction under § 1911(a) therefore applied notwithstanding the parents' voluntary off-reservation surrender.
  Why here: A state court that proceeds where exclusive tribal jurisdiction exists has entered a void order, the most serious form of ICWA non-compliance.
  Quote: “Tribal jurisdiction under § 1911(a) was not meant to be defeated by the actions of individual members of the tribe”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **In the Matter of Baby Boy L.**, 2004 OK 93, 103 P.3d 1099
  Holding: The court reversed an order declaring an Indian child eligible for adoption without the father's consent, abandoning the judicially created existing Indian family exception in light of amendments to the Oklahoma ICWA and expressly overruling In re S.C., Baby Boy D and D.M.J.
  Why here: It is the controlling Oklahoma authority that a state court may not decline to apply ICWA on the theory that no Indian family existed, the compliance failure Simmers flirts with.
  Quote: “the "existing Indian family exception" is no longer a viable doctrine applicable in Oklahoma insofar as Indian child custody proceedings are concerned”

Both the federal ICWA and the Oklahoma Indian Child Welfare Act (10 O.S. §§ 40-40.9) apply, and OICWA § 40.6 extends the 25 U.S.C. § 1915 placement preferences to all pre-adjudicatory placements as well as preadoptive, adoptive and foster care placements - broader than the federal text. - In re C.G. (1981) remains the leading Oklahoma authority on two distinct points: (1) due process requires judicially prescribed, written norms of parental conduct before a failure-to-correct termination, and (2) the clear-and-convincing standard for termination. Its clear-and-convincing holding is now codified at 10A O.S. § 1-4-904, but C.G. is still the cited source. In re T.T.S. (2015) is the modern procedural counterpart, requiring the specific uncorrected conditions to appear in the application, instructions, verdict forms and final order - a point on which Court of Civil Appeals panels had openly split.
- Reporter-immunity practice in Oklahoma is governed by Myers v. Lashley (Okla. 2002): the 10A O.S. § 1-2-104 privilege bars all theories of recovery for reporting-caused harm, good faith is statutorily presumed, and only scienter-level bad faith pierces it. Kremeier (threatened rather than ongoing abuse still immunized; gross negligence insufficient) and West v. Jane Phillips (immunity covers post-reporting harm only, not independently negligent conduct upstream of the report) are the two published COCA refinements.
- State v. Gurley is important and cuts against parents: the Court of Civil Appeals held that holding the emergency custody hearing on the third judicial day violated 10A O.S. § 1-4-203's two-judicial-day rule but that the statute supplies no remedy and the court retains jurisdiction, and that the State may re-remove on identical facts after a case is dismissed for missing the seven-day petition deadline. Counsel relying on statutory deadlines in Oklahoma emergency-custody practice should plan around this.
- Deal v. Brooks is the key Oklahoma decision on agency liability after a child's death: 51 O.S. § 155(29) exempts DHS from all GTCA tort liability for 'any claim based upon an act or omission of an employee in the placement of children,' but the Court of Civil Appeals held that exemption does not extinguish a Bosh-type damages action directly under the Oklahoma Constitution where DHS employees acted in reckless disregard of a known or obvious risk of serious harm. Both Bosh and Barrios are now carried in the brief, and Barrios must be read before relying on Deal. Compare the federal route in T.D. v. Patton.
