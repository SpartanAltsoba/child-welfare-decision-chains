# WA — 47 authorities, verified 2026-09-21

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Tyner v. Department of Social & Health Services, Child Protective Services**, 141 Wn.2d 68, 1 P.3d 1148 (2000)
  Holding: The court held that RCW 26.44.050 impliedly creates a negligent-investigation cause of action in favor of a child's parents, including parents themselves suspected of abuse, and that a later judicial no-contact order does not automatically break legal causation unless all material information was placed before the court.
  Why here: Establishes that the reporting-and-investigation scheme of chapter 26.44 RCW, which begins with a mandated report, runs to the benefit of parents as well as children.
  Quote: “We conclude that under RCW 26.44.050, CPS owes a duty of care to a child's parents, even those suspected of abusing their own children, when investigating allegations of child abuse.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2552284/tyner-v-dshs-child-protective-serv/
- **Wrigley v. State**, 195 Wn.2d 65, 455 P.3d 1138 (2020)
  Holding: The court held that the statutory duty to investigate is triggered only by a report of abuse or neglect that has already occurred, so a parent's prediction of future harm from a proposed placement did not invoke DSHS's duty under former RCW 26.44.050.
  Why here: Defines what a mandated reporter must communicate before the agency's investigative duty attaches: past or ongoing conduct, not a prediction.
  Quote: “Generally, a report initiates the dependency process. The duty to investigate cannot be invoked until receipt of a report.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4714773/wrigley-v-state/
- **Dunning v. Paccerelli**, 63 Wn. App. 232, 818 P.2d 34 (1991)
  Holding: The court held that caseworkers and DSHS have only qualified, not absolute, immunity for reporting and investigating child abuse and for placing names on the central registry, and that the good-faith condition of the RCW 26.44.060 immunity is a question of fact.
  Why here: Defines the scope of the reporter immunity mandated reporters rely on: it is qualified and conditioned on good faith.
  Quote: “The determination of good faith under RCW 26.44.060 necessarily presents issues of fact.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1227028/dunning-v-paccerelli/
- **Yonker v. Department of Social & Health Services**, 85 Wn. App. 71, 930 P.2d 958 (1997)
  Holding: The court held that the public duty doctrine does not shield DSHS from a negligence claim by a mother and child whose reports of possible abuse the Department declined to investigate, because they fall within the particular and circumscribed class chapter 26.44 RCW was enacted to protect, and reversed summary judgment for the State.
  Why here: The origin of the enforceable investigative duty under chapter 26.44 RCW, on facts where caseworkers agreed the report indicated possible abuse and still did nothing.
  Quote: “If there is a question whether a report is sufficient to invoke the duty to investigate, that issue will be resolved on a case-by-case basis.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6993314/yonker-v-department-of-social-health-services/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Devereaux v. Abbey**, 263 F.3d 1070 (9th Cir. 2001) (en banc)
  Holding: Sitting en banc in a case arising from the Wenatchee, Washington child sex abuse investigations, the court held there is no constitutional right to have child witnesses interviewed in a particular manner, and that a deliberate-fabrication claim requires evidence that investigators knew or should have known the accused was innocent or used techniques so coercive they knew would yield false information.
  Why here: Arose from a joint police and CPS investigation in Washington and defines the constitutional limits on that joint interviewing practice.
  Quote: “an allegation that an interviewer disbelieved an initial denial and continued with aggressive questioning of the child cannot, without more, support a deliberate-fabrication-of-evidence claim”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7099058/devereaux-v-abbey/
- **Miles v. Child Protective Services Department**, 102 Wn. App. 142, 6 P.3d 112 (2000)
  Holding: The court affirmed dismissal of claims arising from an emergency removal, holding the caseworker was entitled to the good-faith immunity of RCW 26.44.056(3) where she relied on the opinions of treating physicians, and that the parents identified no clearly established constitutional right to support their section 1983 claim.
  Why here: Illustrates the joint hospital, law-enforcement and CPS pathway into protective custody and the immunity that attaches to it.
  Quote: “We conclude that a rational trier could not find causation, and that a rational trier would be obligated to find good faith.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4930248/miles-v-child-protective-services-department/
- **Rogers v. County of San Joaquin**, 487 F.3d 1288 (9th Cir. 2007)
  Holding: The court held that warrantless removal of children is permissible only in cases of exigency, meaning reasonable cause to believe the child is likely to experience serious bodily harm in the time required to obtain a warrant, and reversed qualified immunity for the social worker because no reasonable social worker could have believed an exigency existed on this record.
  Why here: Applies the same exigency requirement to social workers and to the police officer who accompanied the removal, so a referral between them does not lower the constitutional threshold.
  Quote: “we hold that it was clearly established that warrantless removal of children is permissible only in cases of exigency”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/797914/rogers-v-county-of-san-joaquin/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **Tyner v. Department of Social & Health Services, Child Protective Services**, 141 Wn.2d 68, 1 P.3d 1148 (2000)
  Holding: The court held that RCW 26.44.050 impliedly creates a negligent-investigation cause of action in favor of a child's parents, including parents themselves suspected of abuse, and that a later judicial no-contact order does not automatically break legal causation unless all material information was placed before the court.
  Why here: Holds that the agency's statutory investigative duty exists independently of any court involvement, so a judicially generated referral does not displace the agency's own duty of care.
  Quote: “We conclude that under RCW 26.44.050, CPS owes a duty of care to a child's parents, even those suspected of abusing their own children, when investigating allegations of child abuse.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2552284/tyner-v-dshs-child-protective-serv/
- **Babcock v. State**, 116 Wn.2d 596, 809 P.2d 143 (1991)
  Holding: The court held that DSHS caseworkers have no absolute immunity for foster placement decisions made outside an adversarial judicial process, that they may claim only the Guffey qualified immunity, which requires carrying out a statutory duty by statutory procedures and acting reasonably, and that the State itself has no immunity from suit.
  Why here: Holds that a court order procured ex parte and without any adversarial evaluation of the placement does not confer absolute immunity on the caseworkers who obtained it.
  Quote: “Caseworkers cannot claim even a qualified immunity when they fail to follow statutory procedures.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1196412/babcock-v-state/
- **Petcu v. State**, 121 Wn. App. 36, 86 P.3d 1234 (2004)
  Holding: The court affirmed summary judgment for DSHS, holding that a dependency court's order is a superseding intervening cause cutting off caseworker liability for negligent investigation unless the faulty investigation deprived that court of material information, and that Petcu identified no material information withheld from the dependency judge.
  Why here: Defines when judicial involvement in a case breaks the causal chain between the agency's own investigation and the resulting separation of parent and child.
  Quote: “Otherwise, court intervention operates as a superseding intervening cause that cuts off the caseworker's liability.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4937237/petcu-v-state/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In re Dependency of A.L.K.**, 196 Wn.2d 686, 478 P.3d 63 (2020)
  Holding: The court held that the Department failed to make active efforts under ICWA and WICWA where the social worker offered a case plan and one phone call but did not actively help the parent access services, and that declining voluntary services before a dependency finding cannot substitute for active efforts.
  Why here: Holds that a parent's refusal of voluntary pre-dependency services cannot later be used against her as proof of agency effort.
  Quote: “We hold that the Department in the present case did not engage in active efforts as is required under ICWA and WICWA.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4842714/in-re-dependency-of-alk-lrcks-dbcks/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Braam v. State**, 150 Wn.2d 689, 81 P.3d 851 (2003)
  Holding: In a foster-care class action the court recognized that foster children hold a substantive due process right to be free from unreasonable risk of harm and to reasonable safety, but held the jury was misinstructed by treating the professional-judgment standard of care as the right itself, and it narrowed the systemwide injunction.
  Why here: The leading Washington example of systemic litigation producing court-supervised reform of the child welfare agency.
  Quote: “We hold that foster children have a constitutional substantive due process right to be free from unreasonable risks of harm and a right to reasonable safety.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4908273/braam-v-state/
- **H.B.H. v. State**, 429 P.3d 484 (Wash. 2018)
  Holding: The court held that DSHS stands in a Restatement section 315(b) protective special relationship with dependent foster children based on entrustment and vulnerability rather than physical custody, and that the children's negligence claims for failure to protect them from their foster parents must go to a jury.
  Why here: Holds the agency answerable in tort for failing to perform required health and safety checks, the very practice oversight bodies audit.
  Quote: “Consistent with our precedent, we hold that the special relationship between DSHS and foster children gives rise to a protective duty under Restatement § 315(b).”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6657514/hbh-v-state/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In re Dependency of Z.J.G.**, 196 Wn.2d 152, 471 P.3d 853 (2020)
  Holding: The court adopted a broad reading of the ICWA and WICWA inquiry threshold, holding that a court has reason to know a child is an Indian child whenever any participant in the proceeding indicates the child has tribal heritage, and that the trial court had reason to know at the shelter care hearing.
  Why here: Sets the trigger for ICWA and WICWA jurisdiction and notice in Washington dependency cases.
  Quote: “We hold that a trial court has "reason to know" that a child is an Indian child when a participant in the proceeding indicates that the child has tribal heritage.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4782391/in-re-dependency-of-zjg/
- **In re Dependency of A.L.K.**, 196 Wn.2d 686, 478 P.3d 63 (2020)
  Holding: The court held that the Department failed to make active efforts under ICWA and WICWA where the social worker offered a case plan and one phone call but did not actively help the parent access services, and that declining voluntary services before a dependency finding cannot substitute for active efforts.
  Why here: Applies the heightened ICWA and WICWA active-efforts duty owed before an Indian child may be removed.
  Quote: “We hold that the Department in the present case did not engage in active efforts as is required under ICWA and WICWA.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4842714/in-re-dependency-of-alk-lrcks-dbcks/
- **In re Adoption of T.A.W.**, 186 Wn.2d 828, 383 P.3d 492 (2016)
  Holding: The court held that ICWA and WICWA protect the parental rights of a non-Indian parent of an Indian child, apply to stepparent adoptions, require any party seeking termination including a private petitioner to prove active efforts, and that WICWA legislatively abrogated the existing Indian family exception.
  Why here: Defines the reach of ICWA and WICWA by the child's status, not the parent's, in every child custody proceeding.
  Quote: “We hold that the plain language of ICWA and WICWA apply to and protect the parental rights of a non-Indian parent of an Indian child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4910797/rb-v-cw/
- **In re Dependency of A.M.**, 106 Wn. App. 123, 22 P.3d 828 (2001)
  Holding: The court held that the ICWA active-efforts showing under 25 U.S.C. section 1912(d) need be proved only by clear, cogent and convincing evidence rather than beyond a reasonable doubt, and that a parent's voluntary unavailability excuses the State from offering further remedial services.
  Why here: Fixes the evidentiary standard governing the federal active-efforts element in Washington ICWA cases.
  Quote: “We conclude that the trial court properly applied the clear, cogent and convincing standard to the requirements contained in 25 U.S.C. § 1912(d).”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4931548/applebee-v-department-of-social-health-services/
- **In re Dependency of T.L.G.**, 126 Wn. App. 181, 108 P.3d 156 (2005)
  Holding: The court held that a parent's assertion of Cherokee heritage gave the trial court reason to know an Indian child might be involved and triggered the ICWA and state notice duties, that the burden of ensuring notice rests on the court and the State rather than the parent, and that remand for proper notice is the ordinary remedy.
  Why here: Holds that tribal enrollment is not required to trigger ICWA notice and that only the tribe can decide membership.
  Quote: “Dunlavy's assertion of Cherokee heritage gave the court "reason to know," and triggered the notice requirements of 25 U.S.C. § 1912(a) and RCW 13.34.070(10).”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4938817/department-of-social-health-services-v-gilfillen/

  Holding: Sitting en banc the court reaffirmed that officials must ordinarily obtain prior judicial authorization before removing a child, warrantless seizure being excusable only on reasonable cause to believe the child is likely to suffer serious bodily harm in the time needed to get a warrant, and held the facts there met that standard.
  Why here: States the controlling Fourth Amendment rule for protective custody in the circuit that governs Washington.
  Quote: “Under the Fourth Amendment, government officials are ordinarily required to obtain prior judicial authorization before removing a child from the custody of her parent.”

  Holding: The court held that subjecting children in protective custody to invasive investigatory medical examinations without notifying the parents and without parental consent or judicial authorization violates the parents' Fourteenth Amendment right to family association and the children's Fourth Amendment rights, absent exigency.
  Why here: Limits what the State may do to a child after taking protective custody, not merely how it may take custody.

  Why here: States the reasonable-cause-plus-imminence rule and the scope limitation governing warrantless protective custody in the circuit that governs Washington.
  Quote: “the state is required to notify parents and to obtain judicial approval before children are subjected to investigatory physical examinations”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7079922/wallis-ex-rel-wallis-v-spencer/

  Why here: The source of the 'likely to experience serious bodily harm in the time that would be required to obtain a warrant' formulation that Keates quotes and Kirkpatrick applies.

## DEC-01 · Screen-Out / Unfounded at Intake
- **M.W. v. Department of Social & Health Services**, 149 Wn.2d 589, 70 P.3d 954 (2003)
  Holding: The court held that the implied negligent-investigation action under RCW 26.44.050 is a narrow exception limited to biased or faulty investigations producing a harmful placement decision, and does not reach other investigative harms such as an intrusive physical examination of the child.
  Why here: Because only harmful placement decisions are actionable, a screen-out or unfounded closure standing alone will rarely support the statutory claim.
  Quote: “such a claim is available only when DSHS conducts a biased or faulty investigation that leads to a harmful placement decision”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4907932/mw-v-department-of-social-health-services/
- **Ducote v. Department of Social & Health Services**, 167 Wn.2d 697, 222 P.3d 785 (2009)
  Holding: The court held that stepparents are not within the class protected by RCW 26.44.050 and therefore lack standing to bring a negligent-investigation claim, limiting the protected class to children, parents, guardians and custodians.
  Why here: Defines who may challenge an agency investigative decision at all, which determines who can litigate a screening or unfounded determination.
  Quote: “they are not included in the categories of persons who may sue for negligent investigation under RCW 26.44.050”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4909012/ducote-v-department-of-social-health-services/
- **Wrigley v. State**, 195 Wn.2d 65, 455 P.3d 1138 (2020)
  Holding: The court held that the statutory duty to investigate is triggered only by a report of abuse or neglect that has already occurred, so a parent's prediction of future harm from a proposed placement did not invoke DSHS's duty under former RCW 26.44.050.
  Why here: Directly governs screen-out liability: without a qualifying report there is no investigative duty and no negligent-investigation claim.
  Quote: “Generally, a report initiates the dependency process. The duty to investigate cannot be invoked until receipt of a report.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4714773/wrigley-v-state/
- **Roberson v. Perez**, 156 Wn.2d 33, 123 P.3d 844 (2005)
