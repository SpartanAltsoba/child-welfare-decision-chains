## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Geraci v. Hamilton**, No. 22-CV-0749 (D.C. Feb. 5, 2026)
  Holding: D.C. Code § 4-1321.04 confers on good-faith mandatory reporters immunity from liability rather than immunity from suit, so the denial of that immunity is not effectively unreviewable after final judgment and cannot be appealed under the collateral order doctrine; the interlocutory appeal was dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.
  Why here: It is the most recent D.C. decision construing the mandatory reporter immunity that accompanies the reporting duty and its criminal penalty for failure to report.
  Quote: “mandatory reporters are shielded from liability, both civil and criminal, for reports made in good faith, even if those reports ultimately turn out to be mistaken”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10784541/geraci-v-hamilton/
- **Turner v. District of Columbia**, 532 A.2d 662 (D.C. 1987)
  Holding: When CPS receives a report identifying particular children as abused, the Child Abuse Prevention Act creates a special relationship imposing on the District a duty to take the steps the Act prescribes for those children, so summary judgment for the District was error.
  Why here: It is the D.C. case holding that a mandated report to CPS about identified children triggers a legally enforceable duty to act.
  Quote: “From that moment on, the District had a duty to take certain steps prescribed by the Act for the protection of those children.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2377127/turner-v-district-of-columbia/
- **V.K. v. Child & Family Services Agency**, 14 A.3d 628 (D.C. 2011)

  Why here: It is the case that shows how a report by a professional becomes a substantiated Register entry naming the reported adult.

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **Troxel v. Granville**, 530 U.S. 57 (2000)
  Holding: As applied, Washington's broad third-party visitation statute violated the mother's due process rights because the trial court gave no special weight to the presumption that a fit parent acts in her children's best interests.
  Why here: It protects the decisions of a parent who seeks help voluntarily from being second-guessed absent a finding of unfitness.
  Quote: “there is a presumption that fit parents act in the best interests of their children”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1087668/troxel-v-granville/
- **Stanley v. Illinois**, 405 U.S. 645 (1972)
  Holding: An unwed father was constitutionally entitled to a hearing on his fitness before his children were taken from him, and Illinois' presumption that unwed fathers are unfit denied him due process and equal protection.
  Why here: It establishes that a parent who comes forward seeking his children may not be treated as presumptively unfit by category.

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/108497/stanley-v-illinois/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **LaShawn A. v. Dixon**, 762 F. Supp. 959 (D.D.C. 1991)
  Holding: After trial the court found the District's child welfare system violated federal and District statutory and constitutional requirements at nearly every stage, including a consistent failure to provide services or make reasonable efforts to prevent placement, and ordered systemic relief.
  Why here: It is the D.C. class action in which court-supervised oversight of the entire child welfare system originated.
  Quote: “the Court finds that defendants have consistently failed to provide services or otherwise use “reasonable efforts” to prevent placement”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1619416/lashawn-a-v-dixon/
- **Vining v. Council of the District of Columbia**, 140 A.3d 439 (D.C. 2016)
  Holding: The Council may not invoke the Legislative Privilege Act through D.C. FOIA Exemption 6 (or, on this record, Exemption 4) to withhold documents responsive to a FOIA request, and summary judgment for the Council was reversed.
  Why here: It keeps legislative oversight records about District agencies reachable by the public rather than sealed by privilege.
  Quote: “We conclude that the Legislative Privilege Act cannot be invoked through Exemption 6 so as to permit the Council to withhold documents responsive to a FOIA request”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3211615/kirby-vining-v-council-of-the-district-of-columbia/
- **LaShawn A. v. Barry**, 87 F.3d 1389 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc)
  Holding: Sitting en banc the court held there is no 'jurisdictional question' exception to the law-of-the-case doctrine, so the later panel could not revisit the earlier panel's direction that the district court exercise pendent jurisdiction over the local-law claims in the foster care class action, and it remitted the District's remaining arguments to the panel.
  Why here: It is the appellate history that kept the LaShawn class action's systemic oversight intact, and it must travel on the face of any brief citing the 1991 merits findings.
  Quote: “the law-of-the-case and law-of-the-circuit doctrines precluded the panel in LaShawn II from revisiting the Gibbs step two decision reached in LaShawn I”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/721294/lashawn-a-v-marion-s-barry-jr/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: Domicile under ICWA is a matter of uniform federal law; because the twins took their mother's reservation domicile at birth the tribal court had exclusive jurisdiction under 25 U.S.C. § 1911(a) and the state adoption decree had to be vacated.
  Why here: It is the controlling authority on when an Indian child's case must go to tribal rather than District court at the threshold.
  Quote: “the twin babies in this case were domiciled on the reservation when adoption proceedings were begun, the Choctaw tribal court possessed exclusive jurisdiction”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court rejected every challenge to the Indian Child Welfare Act, upholding Congress's Article I power and rejecting the anticommandeering claims on the merits while dismissing the equal protection and nondelegation claims for lack of standing.
  Why here: It confirms that ICWA's active-efforts, expert-witness and placement-preference commands remain binding on courts handling Indian child custody proceedings.
  Quote: “we reject all of petitioners' challenges to the statute”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10049675/haaland-v-brackeen/
- **Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl**, 570 U.S. 637 (2013)
  Holding: The Court held that ICWA § 1912(f)'s continued-custody standard does not apply where the Indian parent never had custody of the child and that § 1912(d)'s active-efforts requirement applies only where termination would precipitate the breakup of an existing Indian family, so neither provision barred termination of the biological father's rights.
  Why here: It is the Supreme Court's construction of ICWA's active-efforts and continued-custody commands, which Holyfield (jurisdiction) and Brackeen (constitutionality) leave undefined, and it tells a District practitioner when those heightened duties actually attach.
  Quote: “Consistent with the statutory text, we hold that §1912(d) applies only in cases where an Indian family's "breakup" would be precipitated by the termination of the parent's rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/931616/adoptive-couple-v-baby-girl/

  Holding: Civil Rule 36 admissions deemed against parents who failed to respond were conclusively established and, with the rest of the evidence, sufficed to prove neglect by a preponderance, so the adjudication was affirmed.
  Why here: It is the current D.C. statement of the burden of proof at the neglect factfinding hearing after a petition is filed.
  Quote: “The government’s burden at trial was to prove that K.M. was neglected by a preponderance of the evidence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5155985/in-re-km/
- **In re M.L.**, 28 A.3d 520 (D.C. 2011)
  Holding: The Improved Child Abuse Investigations Amendment Act of 2002 does not bar Superior Court judges from admitting the results of court-ordered mental evaluations in neglect hearings, such examinations under D.C. Code § 16-2315(e)(4) are not shielded by the doctor-patient privilege, and the neglect adjudication was supported by sufficient evidence.
  Why here: It addresses the admissibility and privilege questions that recur at the neglect factfinding hearing.
  Quote: “we hold that the trial court's neglect adjudication was supported by sufficient evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2321544/in-re-ml/
- **In re Jam.J.**, 825 A.2d 902 (D.C. 2003)
  Holding: The trial court has power to protect a child from the harmful effects of compelled testimony in a neglect case, but ordinarily must exercise it through conditions on the examination rather than by barring the child's testimony outright; excluding the children's testimony and the parent's expert required a new trial.
  Why here: It is the D.C. rule on child testimony and expert evidence at the neglect factfinding hearing.
  Quote: “We hold that the trial court does have power to protect a child from the harmful effects of forced testimony.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1495654/in-re-jam-j/
- **In re Na.H.**, 65 A.3d 111 (D.C. 2013)
  Holding: A parent who does not timely move for review of a magistrate judge's neglect and disposition order by a Superior Court associate judge forfeits appellate review, and the Court of Appeals affirmed the dismissal of the untimely motion without reaching the merits.
  Why here: It fixes the procedural step a parent must take after a magistrate judge's neglect ruling to preserve any challenge.
  Quote: “We therefore affirm the Superior Court’s order dismissing the motion for review as untimely, and conclude that the merits of the neglect ruling are not properly before us.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5146398/in-re-nah/
- **In re Ti.B.**, 762 A.2d 20 (D.C. 2000)
  Holding: Orders barring a father in a neglect proceeding from conferring with his criminal defense counsel about his privilege against self-incrimination, and excluding that lawyer from the courtroom, were immediately appealable under the collateral order doctrine and were an abuse of discretion.
  Why here: It protects a parent's access to counsel of choice once a neglect petition places him at risk of parallel criminal exposure.
  Quote: “the trial court abused its discretion in excluding Mr. Rapping from the courtroom, and in prohibiting T.B. and Mr. Harris from conferring with Mr. Rapping about the neglect proceeding”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2092391/in-re-ti-b/
- **Mathews v. Eldridge**, 424 U.S. 319 (1976)
  Holding: The Court held that an evidentiary hearing is not required before disability benefits are terminated and that what process is due is determined by weighing three factors: the private interest affected, the risk of erroneous deprivation under the existing procedures and the value of added safeguards, and the government's interest including administrative burden.
  Why here: It is the balancing framework In re S.J. applies in holding that denying a parent an immediate appeal from a shelter care order does not violate due process because the factfinding hearing follows.
  Quote: “identification of the specific dictates of due process generally requires consideration of three distinct factors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/109382/mathews-v-eldridge/

## ACT-02 · Court-Ordered Services
- **In re A.I.**, 211 A.3d 1116 (D.C. 2019)
  Holding: Reasonable efforts require CFSA to identify the barriers to reunification and to refer the parent to appropriate services addressing them; CFSA satisfied that duty here and the change of permanency goal from reunification to adoption was affirmed.
  Why here: It is the leading post-Ta.L. D.C. decision defining the content of a court-ordered case plan and the reasonable efforts that must accompany it.

  Why here: It conditions any court-ordered service plan and goal change on a preponderance finding that the government made reasonable efforts consistent with the neglect plan.
  Quote: “a change of the presumptive goal of a neglect proceeding from reunification to adoption is an appealable final order”

  Holding: The ADA and the Rehabilitation Act protect parents with disabilities from discriminatory curtailment of parental rights and require reasonable accommodations, but the trial court did not violate them where the intellectually disabled mother received extensive services and still could not safely parent, so the goal change to adoption was affirmed.
  Why here: It requires that the service plan itself be accommodated to a parent's disability rather than set at an unreachable standard.
  Quote: “The ADA and the Rehabilitation Act do protect parents with disabilities, including intellectual and psychiatric disabilities, from discriminatory curtailment of their parental rights.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4514250/in-re-hc-kc/
- **LaShawn A. v. Dixon**, 762 F. Supp. 959 (D.D.C. 1991)
  Holding: After trial the court found the District's child welfare system violated federal and District statutory and constitutional requirements at nearly every stage, including a consistent failure to provide services or make reasonable efforts to prevent placement, and ordered systemic relief.
  Why here: It found chronic failures in case planning and in the timely preparation of plans required by federal and District law.
  Quote: “the Court finds that defendants have consistently failed to provide services or otherwise use “reasonable efforts” to prevent placement”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1619416/lashawn-a-v-dixon/
- **In re K.C.**, 200 A.3d 1216 (D.C. 2019)
  Holding: The court affirmed the suspension of visitation, the change of permanency goal to adoption and the termination of parental rights, holding that the agency's case plan and reasonable efforts were adequate where the mother herself refused to participate in case planning and in the mental health services offered to her.
  Why here: It holds that a parent who refuses to take part in case planning cannot later attack the court-ordered service plan as untailored or lacking benchmarks.
  Quote: “She cannot now argue, well after the fact, that the Agency should have done even more than it already did to overcome her uncooperativeness.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4586153/in-re-kc-dc/
- **In re J.M. & D.M.**, 193 A.3d 773 (D.C. 2018)
  Holding: The court affirmed retention of adoption as the children's sole permanency goal, holding that the case plan was appropriate and reasonable even though the mother never signed the plan documents and the later plan assigned no action components to the agency, and that the reasonable-efforts and inadequate-progress findings were supported by the record.
  Why here: It is the post-Ta.L. decision holding that a court-ordered case plan binds the parent without her signature because its requirements come from the court's own disposition order.
  Quote: “Thus, the case plan mirrored what the magistrate judge had ordered; it did not set out requirements that became effective only upon the Mother's signature.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4536550/in-re-jm-dm-sm/

## ACT-03 · Foster Care Placement
- **In re S.J.**, 632 A.2d 112 (D.C. 1993)
  Holding: Only the child, not the parent, may take an interlocutory appeal from a shelter care order under D.C. Code § 16-2328(a), and that limitation does not violate due process because the custody deprivation is temporary and followed by a factfinding hearing at which the government must prove its allegations by a preponderance.
  Why here: It defines the limited interlocutory review available once the child is placed in shelter care.
  Quote: “Because it is not the child who has brought this appeal (but rather her mother), we lack jurisdiction to entertain it under the plain language of the statute.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5130392/in-re-sj/
- **J.C. v. District of Columbia**, 199 A.3d 192 (D.C. 2018)
  Holding: Probable cause supported the medical hold and shelter care of an infant with suspected shaken baby syndrome and the parents' Child Protection Register listing did not violate due process, but the warrantless seizure of the sibling from inside the family home was remanded for a determination whether exigent circumstances existed.
  Why here: It ties the lawfulness of the initial shelter care placement to the same probable cause showing.
  Quote: “under the Fourth Amendment, a lawful seizure of children from their parents' custody requires a court order, e.g., a warrant, probable cause, or exigent circumstances”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6486612/jc-v-dist-of-columbia/
- **In re L.M.**, No. 23-FS-0386 (D.C. Dec. 21, 2023)
  Holding: The District failed to carry its burden of showing that shelter care was necessary for a healthy five-month-old removed after her mother's hospitalization, even under the less demanding probable cause standard, so the shelter care order was reversed.
  Why here: It reverses the shelter care placement that followed the emergency custody decision.
  Quote: “Because we conclude that the District failed to carry its burden even under a less-demanding probable-cause standard, we assume without deciding that a probable-cause standard applies.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9454123/in-re-lm/
- **Smith v. District of Columbia**, 413 F.3d 86 (D.C. Cir. 2005)
  Holding: A youth in the District's legal custody and placed by it in a group program was in state custody for DeShaney purposes, so the District owed him a substantive due process duty of care and the jury's deliberate-indifference verdict was sustained.
  Why here: It establishes that once the District places a child it assumes a constitutional duty to select and monitor the placement with care.
  Quote: “Because the District, rather than Tron's family, had primary legal control over him, the District had legal responsibility for his daily care.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/186512/smith-v-district-of-columbia/
- **LaShawn A. v. Dixon**, 762 F. Supp. 959 (D.D.C. 1991)
  Holding: After trial the court found the District's child welfare system violated federal and District statutory and constitutional requirements at nearly every stage, including a consistent failure to provide services or make reasonable efforts to prevent placement, and ordered systemic relief.
  Why here: It found that children were consistently not placed in the least restrictive placement consistent with their needs.
  Quote: “the Court finds that defendants have consistently failed to provide services or otherwise use “reasonable efforts” to prevent placement”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1619416/lashawn-a-v-dixon/
- **Youngberg v. Romeo**, 457 U.S. 307 (1982)
  Holding: A person involuntarily committed to a state institution retains substantive due process liberty interests in reasonably safe conditions, freedom from undue bodily restraint and minimally adequate training, and officials are liable only where their decision is a substantial departure from accepted professional judgment.
  Why here: It is the affirmative-duty case DeShaney relies on and the doctrinal basis for Smith v. District of Columbia: once the District takes a child into its custody and places him, it owes a constitutional duty of reasonable safety.
  Quote: “The State also has the unquestioned duty to provide reasonable safety for all residents and personnel within the institution.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/110746/youngberg-v-romeo-ex-rel-romeo/

  Why here: It requires the trial court to give weighty consideration to the parents' preferred relative caregiver before approving a competing stranger adoption.

## PMC-04 · Fatality Review Commission
- **District of Columbia v. Hampton**, 666 A.2d 30 (D.C. 1995)
  Holding: A plaintiff suing the District for negligent placement and monitoring of a foster child who died must present expert testimony on the applicable standard of care, and the foster mother was not the District's agent for respondeat superior purposes.
  Why here: It explains why fatality review findings alone do not establish negligence without expert standard-of-care proof.
  Quote: “Mrs. Hampton should have presented expert testimony on the standard of care, and that the absence of such testimony was fatal to her case”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2381519/district-of-columbia-v-hampton/
- **Doe ex rel. Fein v. District of Columbia**, 697 A.2d 23 (D.C. 1997)
  Holding: Police reports of a child's injuries did not satisfy the six-month written notice requirement of D.C. Code § 12-309, and the statute does not permit equitable tolling, so the abused child's claims against the District and its employees were barred.
  Why here: It demonstrates why delayed fatality or serious-injury review can leave a family with no remedy at all.
  Quote: “the police reports filed after Jane Doe suffered her physical injuries were insufficient under D.C.Code § 12-309 to provide the District with notice of her potential claims”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2361058/doe-ex-rel-fein-v-district-of-columbia/
- **Smith v. District of Columbia**, 413 F.3d 86 (D.C. Cir. 2005)
  Holding: A youth in the District's legal custody and placed by it in a group program was in state custody for DeShaney purposes, so the District owed him a substantive due process duty of care and the jury's deliberate-indifference verdict was sustained.
  Why here: It shows what a fatality review's findings about placement selection and monitoring can support in litigation.
  Quote: “Because the District, rather than Tron's family, had primary legal control over him, the District had legal responsibility for his daily care.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/186512/smith-v-district-of-columbia/

- Missing (for a later pass): In re S.J., 772 A.2d 247 (D.C. 2001) — holds that an order waiving a birth parent's consent to adoption is not final and appealable because it is only a step toward the final act of adoption. Verified from the text of In re K.M.T., which calls it 'dispositive.' It is the authority K.M.T. rests on and is therefore load-bearing for the brief's FAIL-01/PMC-06 appealability story, and it must be cited with care because the brief already maps a different In re S.J., 632 A.2d 112 (D.C. 1993), at four nodes.
- Missing (for a later pass): Baker v. District of Columbia, 326 F.3d 1302 (D.C. Cir. 2003) — the D.C. Circuit's statement of the Monell policy-or-custom and deliberate-indifference standards as applied to the District specifically. FAIL-02 now maps Monell itself but nothing showing how this circuit applies it to the District, which is the actual question on the Doe v. District of Columbia remand.
- Missing (for a later pass): Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158 (1944) — the foundational statement of the State's parens patriae authority and its limits. It is the necessary counterweight to Troxel at INP-05 and ACT-01, where the brief currently presents parental autonomy without the state-interest side of the balance.
