# OR — 45 authorities, verified 2026-09-21

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Pamplin Media Group v. City of Salem**, 293 Or. App. 755, 429 P.3d 1019 (2018)
  Holding: The court held that ORS 419B.035's exclusion of child-abuse reports and records from disclosure does not sweep in an arrest record merely because the underlying investigation began as a child-abuse report, and the city failed to carry its burden of proving exemption.
  Why here: Construes ORS 419B.010 to 419B.050 — the mandatory reporting and investigation provisions — to define what is and is not a confidential 'report of child abuse.'
  Quote: “On both parties' motions for summary judgment, the city bore the burden of proving that the requested records were exempt from disclosure.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6657546/pamplin-media-grp-v-city-of-salem/
- **State v. Judd**, 301 Or. App. 549, 457 P.3d 316 (2019)
  Holding: The court held Oregon's mandatory abuse-reporting statutes abrogate the psychotherapist-patient privilege only far enough to permit the required report, not to permit the reporter to testify about the privileged communication in a later judicial proceeding.
  Why here: Defines how far a mandatory reporting duty overrides a professional privilege — the report is compelled, courtroom testimony is not, and the court expressly compares the parallel child-abuse reporting statute.
  Quote: “the elder abuse reporting statutes only abrogate privileged communications to the extent that a report be submitted”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10133262/state-v-judd/
- **Querbach v. Dept. of Human Services**, 369 Or. 786, 512 P.3d 432 (2022)
  Holding: Reviewing a DHS "founded" child-abuse determination as an order in other than a contested case, the court held that the governing threshold is the rule's "reasonable cause to believe" standard rather than probable cause, that review is for substantial evidence on the record developed in the circuit court, and that the circuit court's criticisms of DHS's investigative process were not relevant to that review.
  Why here: Ties the "reasonable cause to believe" founding standard to the identical standard that triggers a mandatory reporter's duty to report under ORS 419B.010.
  Quote: “the issue is whether the record developed in the circuit court, when viewed as a whole, would permit a reasonable person to make the determinations that the agency did here.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10160897/querbach-v-dept-of-human-services/

  Why here: Applies equally to records generated by non-mandated reports routed into the same investigative stream.
  Quote: “On both parties' motions for summary judgment, the city bore the burden of proving that the requested records were exempt from disclosure.”

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10144019/kasliner-v-dept-of-human-services/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Sjurset v. Button**, 810 F.3d 609 (9th Cir. 2015)
  Holding: In an Oregon case, the court held city police officers who assisted an ORS 419B.150 removal at the direction of Oregon DHS were entitled to qualified immunity because their reliance on the agency's protective-custody determination was objectively reasonable.
  Why here: This is the controlling Ninth Circuit decision on Oregon police assisting a DHS removal, and on the limits of an officer's reliance on the agency's call.
  Quote: “Sjurset correctly points out, however, that an officer cannot blindly rely on the existence of a protective-custody determination to avoid liability.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3160113/stephen-sjurset-v-charles-button/
- **Calabretta v. Floyd**, 189 F.3d 808 (9th Cir. 1999)
  Holding: The court held that a social worker and police officer were not entitled to qualified immunity for a coerced warrantless entry into a home to investigate suspected child abuse, or for the strip search of a three-year-old, because the Fourth Amendment applies to child-abuse investigations.
  Why here: Governs joint law-enforcement and caseworker entries during an abuse investigation.
  Quote: “It does not hold that the social worker may enter the home despite the absence of consent or exigency.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7078750/calabretta-v-floyd/
- **Mabe v. San Bernardino County**, 237 F.3d 1101 (9th Cir. 2001)
  Holding: The court held a social worker was not entitled to summary judgment on a warrantless-seizure claim because officials must obtain prior judicial authorization before removing a child absent reasonable cause to believe the child faces imminent danger of serious bodily injury.
  Why here: States the standard that binds both caseworkers and officers who take a child during an investigation.
  Quote: “Government officials are required to obtain prior judicial authorization before intruding on a parent's custody of her child unless they possess information at the time of the seizure”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7086384/mabe-v-san-bernardino-county/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **State v. L. P. L. O.**, 280 Or. App. 292, 381 P.3d 846 (2016)
  Holding: The court held a youth's own dependency petition was not mooted by his turning 18, that the UCCJEA supplied subject matter jurisdiction, and that the juvenile court was required on its findings to take dependency jurisdiction over him.
  Why here: Addresses the juvenile court's obligation to act on a petition presented to it rather than declining jurisdiction as a matter of discretion.
  Quote: “We conclude that petitioner's appeal is not moot, there is subject matter jurisdiction under the UCCJEA, and petitioner is within the court's dependency jurisdiction.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8166769/state-v-l-p-l-o/
- **Dept. of Human Services v. M. P.**, 328 Or. App. 502, 537 P.3d 593 (2023)
  Holding: The court held a protective custody order under ORS 419B.150 is not an 'initial child custody determination' and the supporting declaration is not the first pleading, so the dependency proceeding commenced when the petition was filed, defeating UCCJEA home-state jurisdiction.
  Why here: Governs which court may act when a child's connection to Oregon is recent and the UCCJEA is invoked.
  Quote: “An issued but unexecuted protective custody order does not trigger the juvenile court's jurisdiction or commence any time period during which a petition must be filed.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10143884/dept-of-human-services-v-m-p/
- **Dept. of Human Services v. C. M. H.**, 368 Or. 96, 486 P.3d 772 (2021)
  Holding: The court held the juvenile court's 'exclusive original jurisdiction' under ORS 419B.100(1) is subject matter jurisdiction that attaches from the allegations and relief sought in a pending petition, and is not conditioned on a wardship determination.
  Why here: Governs the juvenile court's authority to act on collateral matters, including parentage, once a dependency petition invokes its jurisdiction.
  Quote: “The juvenile court had subject matter jurisdiction in this case.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10160830/dept-of-human-services-v-c-m-h/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **Troxel v. Granville**, 530 U.S. 57 (2000)
  Holding: The plurality held Washington's breathtakingly broad nonparental visitation statute unconstitutional as applied, because it gave no special weight to a fit parent's determination of the child's best interests.
  Why here: Establishes the presumption of parental fitness that frames any state response to a family that asks for help rather than being accused.
  Quote: “In subsequent cases also, we have recognized the fundamental right of parents to make decisions concerning the care, custody, and control of their children.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1087668/troxel-v-granville/
- **Dept. of Human Services v. S. M.**, 355 Or. 241, 323 P.3d 947 (2014)
  Holding: The court affirmed that DHS, as the children's court-appointed legal guardian, had statutory authority to consent to immunizations over the parents' objection, subject to its own rules requiring notice and an opportunity for the parents to be heard in juvenile court.
  Why here: Frames the residual rights a parent retains to invoke the juvenile court after voluntarily admitting jurisdictional allegations.
  Quote: “Indeed, immunization is less invasive and more routine than surgery, which DHS specifically may authorize as the wards' legal guardian.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2711395/department-of-human-services-v-s-m/
- **State v. L. P. L. O.**, 280 Or. App. 292, 381 P.3d 846 (2016)
  Holding: The court held a youth's own dependency petition was not mooted by his turning 18, that the UCCJEA supplied subject matter jurisdiction, and that the juvenile court was required on its findings to take dependency jurisdiction over him.
  Why here: This is the Oregon case on a young person petitioning on his own behalf for dependency jurisdiction and services.
  Quote: “We conclude that petitioner's appeal is not moot, there is subject matter jurisdiction under the UCCJEA, and petitioner is within the court's dependency jurisdiction.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8166769/state-v-l-p-l-o/

## INP-08 · Educational Disclosure
- **Greene v. Camreta**, 588 F.3d 1011 (9th Cir. 2009)
  Holding: In an Oregon case, the court held that the in-school seizure and interview of a child by a caseworker and deputy without a warrant, court order, exigency or parental consent violated the Fourth Amendment (though those defendants had qualified immunity), and that the subsequent removal and medical examination also violated the family's rights.
  Why here: This is the Oregon-origin decision on caseworker and law-enforcement interviews of a child at school; note that the Supreme Court later vacated the Fourth Amendment portion as moot, so it is persuasive rather than binding on that point.
  Quote: “we hold that the investigation conducted by Camreta and Alford and the removal and examination instigated by Camreta all violated Sarah and the girls' constitutional rights”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1309523/greene-v-camreta/
- **Camreta v. Greene**, 563 U.S. 692 (2011)
  Holding: The Court held that it generally may review a constitutional ruling at the behest of a government official who prevailed below on qualified immunity, but that this Oregon case had become moot because the child had grown up and moved away, and it therefore vacated the part of the Ninth Circuit's opinion deciding the Fourth Amendment question about the in-school interview.
  Why here: This is the disposition that stripped Greene v. Camreta of precedential force on caseworker and deputy interviews of a child at school; the vacated Fourth Amendment holding must never be cited without it.
  Quote: “we vacate the part of the Ninth Circuit's opinion that decided the Fourth Amendment issue.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7344718/camreta-v-greene/
- **State v. Judd**, 301 Or. App. 549, 457 P.3d 316 (2019)
  Holding: The court held Oregon's mandatory abuse-reporting statutes abrogate the psychotherapist-patient privilege only far enough to permit the required report, not to permit the reporter to testify about the privileged communication in a later judicial proceeding.
  Why here: Applies the same limited-abrogation principle to any privileged professional who is a mandated reporter.
  Quote: “the elder abuse reporting statutes only abrogate privileged communications to the extent that a report be submitted”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10133262/state-v-judd/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court held the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's 'reasonable efforts' clause, 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15), does not create a right enforceable by private individuals under § 1983 or by implied right of action.
  Why here: Establishes that oversight of state compliance with federal child-welfare conditions runs through the Secretary rather than private enforcement.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Humphries v. County of Los Angeles**, 554 F.3d 1170 (9th Cir. 2009)
  Holding: The court held that maintaining a child abuse central index of 'substantiated' abusers, with no meaningful procedure to challenge a listing, deprives listed persons of a stigma-plus liberty interest without due process.
  Why here: Provides the judicial oversight mechanism for systemic defects in how reports are recorded and disseminated.
  Quote: “We conclude that there is a substantial risk that individuals will be erroneously listed on the CACI, and that California offers insufficient means for correcting those errors.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1276651/humphries-v-county-of-los-angeles/
- **Wyatt B. v. Kotek**, No. 24-4689 (9th Cir. Aug. 12, 2025)
  Holding: In the Oregon foster care class action, the court reversed and held that children in ODHS legal custody who were placed with parents or in trial home visits are nonetheless afforded substantive due process protections.
  Why here: The class action itself functions as external oversight of Oregon's child welfare agency.
  Quote: “We hold that they are afforded substantive due process protections, and therefore, we reverse.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10652301/wyatt-b-v-kotek/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **State ex rel SOSCF v. Lucas**, 177 Or. App. 318, 33 P.3d 1001 (2001)
  Holding: The court held good cause existed to deny the tribe's transfer motion filed after most of the termination trial had been completed, and addressed the qualified expert witness requirement under ICWA.
  Why here: This is the Oregon authority on good cause to deny a tribe's mid-trial motion to transfer an Indian child's case to tribal court, and on the qualified-expert-witness requirement.
  Quote: “We hold that good cause existed to deny the motion to transfer jurisdiction to the tribe.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2516092/state-ex-rel-state-office-for-services-to-children-families-v-lucas/
- **Dept. of Human Services v. J. G.**, 260 Or. App. 500, 317 P.3d 936 (2014)
  Holding: The court held that ICWA § 1914 permits an unpreserved 'active efforts' claim to be raised for the first time on appeal, but that ICWA did not require an active-efforts finding in a durable guardianship judgment, and affirmed.
  Why here: Explains when ICWA's active-efforts requirement attaches to a particular Oregon juvenile proceeding and why the ordinary preservation rule yields to § 1914.
  Quote: “the juvenile court was not required under ICWA to make an "active efforts" finding in the guardianship judgment”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8165294/department-of-human-services-v-j-g/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court rejected every challenge to the Indian Child Welfare Act, upholding Congress's Article I authority to enact it and rejecting the anticommandeering attacks on its placement and record-keeping provisions, while holding that no petitioner had standing to press the equal protection and nondelegation claims.
  Why here: Confirms that ICWA's placement preferences and related commands are constitutional and binding on Oregon juvenile courts, which is the unstated premise of every ORICWA application in this brief.
  Quote: “the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners' challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/
- **Dept. of Human Services v. M. A. N.**, 303 Or. App. 600, 464 P.3d 506 (2020)
  Holding: On de novo review in an ICWA case, the court held mother was unfit but that the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that termination was in the child's best interest where he was placed long-term with bonded extended family, and reversed.
  Why here: Illustrates how ICWA's heightened burden of proof applies to every fact supporting termination once the tribe gives notice of membership.
  Quote: “DHS has failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that termination of mother's parental rights is in his best interest”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10133460/dept-of-human-services-v-m-a-n/
- **Dept. of Human Services v. R. F.**, 328 Or. App. 267, 538 P.3d 577 (2023)
  Holding: The court affirmed denial of former foster parents' ORS 419B.116 motion to intervene in an ICWA case, holding the juvenile court was not compelled to find that existing parties could not adequately present the placement question, while noting intervention might later be required.
  Why here: Applies ORICWA and ICWA placement-preference machinery, including the tribe's authority to say who counts as a relative placement.
  Quote: “Accordingly, the Fs have not demonstrated that the juvenile court committed reversible error when it denied the motion to intervene.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10143842/dept-of-human-services-v-r-f/

  Why here: This is the federal constitutional floor that Oregon's ORS 419B.521 clear-and-convincing standard implements, and the authority a parent invokes if any lesser standard is applied.
  Quote: “due process requires that the State support its allegations by at least clear and convincing evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/110676/santosky-v-kramer/
- **State ex rel Dept. of Human Services v. Rardin**, 340 Or. 436, 134 P.3d 940 (2006)
  Holding: Reversing a termination judgment, the court held that because ORS 419B.504 permits termination only on grounds of present unfitness, the statute assumes parents can change, and a father whose earlier conduct had changed genuinely and lastingly could not be found unfit for failing to present an agency-satisfactory reintegration plan.
  Why here: Completes the Stillman–Simmons line by holding that genuine and lasting change in a parent's conduct forecloses termination for unfitness, because ORS 419B.504 reaches only present unfitness.
  Quote: “the legislature assumes that parents can change their conduct and that, if the change is both genuine and lasting, the state may not terminate their parental rights for unfitness”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/835614/state-ex-rel-department-of-human-services-v-rardin/
- **State ex rel SOSCF v. Stillman**, 333 Or. 135, 36 P.3d 490 (2001)
  Holding: The court affirmed reversal of termination, construing ORS 419B.504 to require a two-part showing — present unfitness from seriously detrimental conduct or condition, and improbability of integration within a reasonable time — and holding an incarcerated father's drug history did not make him presently unfit.
  Why here: This is the leading Oregon Supreme Court construction of the ORS 419B.504 unfitness ground, and the source of the two-part test every later Oregon termination case applies.
  Quote: “ORS 419B.504 sets out a two-part test for determining whether to terminate parental rights, both parts of which must be met before the court orders termination.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/836145/state-ex-rel-state-office-for-services-to-children-families-v-stillman/
- **Dept. of Human Services v. T. M. D.**, 365 Or. 143, 442 P.3d 1100 (2019)
  Holding: The court held there is no statutory presumption or preference favoring termination over permanent guardianship, that the department bears the full burden on best interests, and on this record termination was not in the child's best interest.
  Why here: Controls the best-interests half of an Oregon termination case and rejects any thumb on the scale in favor of adoption over less drastic permanency.
  Quote: “The department must prove only that that the parent's conduct justifies termination and that the best interest of the child will be served by termination.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6658864/dept-of-human-servs-v-t-m-d-in-re-r-d-d-g/
- **State ex rel Dept. of Human Services v. Simmons**, 342 Or. 76, 149 P.3d 1124 (2006)
  Holding: Reversing termination, the court held the State had not proved by clear and convincing evidence that mother's mental illness and past substance addiction made her unfit at the time of the termination trial, the only time frame that matters.
  Why here: Sets the Oregon Supreme Court's controlling application of the clear-and-convincing unfitness standard, measured at the time of the termination trial rather than on historical conduct.
  Quote: “On this record, there is not clear and convincing evidence of a present drug abuse problem that would render mother unfit at the time of the termination trial.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7016038/state-ex-rel-department-of-human-services-v-simmons/
- **Dept. of Human Services v. M. A. N.**, 303 Or. App. 600, 464 P.3d 506 (2020)
  Holding: On de novo review in an ICWA case, the court held mother was unfit but that the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that termination was in the child's best interest where he was placed long-term with bonded extended family, and reversed.
  Why here: Shows that in an ICWA case the best-interests element must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt, not merely by clear and convincing evidence.
  Quote: “DHS has failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that termination of mother's parental rights is in his best interest”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10133460/dept-of-human-services-v-m-a-n/

## OUT-05 · Case Dismissal
- **Dept. of Human Services v. G. D. W.**, 353 Or. 25, 292 P.3d 548 (2012)
  Holding: The court held that a child who is the subject of a dependency petition is not automatically a "party-opponent" of the state, so the child's out-of-court abuse statements were inadmissible as nonhearsay under OEC 801(4)(b)(A) absent evidence the child had taken an adverse position, and it reversed the jurisdictional and aggravated-circumstances judgments because the error was not harmless.
  Why here: Holds that an order terminating DHS custody and dismissing juvenile court jurisdiction does not moot a parent's challenge to the underlying jurisdictional judgment.

  Holding: The court held that when wardship is terminated during an appeal, the department — not the parent — bears the burden of proving the jurisdictional judgment will have no practical effect, and here it carried that burden.
  Why here: Governs the mootness consequences of dismissal or termination of wardship for a pending appeal.
  Quote: “The burden is on the department to prove that a jurisdictional judgment will have no practical effect on the rights of the parties and is therefore moot.”

  Why here: Shows when a dependency-related federal appeal becomes moot because the child has outgrown the challenged practice, and what happens to the ruling below when it does.

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7344718/camreta-v-greene/
- **Dept. of Human Services v. T. J. N.**, 371 Or. 650 (2023)

It fills the gap at ACT-06, INP-07 and FAIL-02 far better than the mapped Oregon medical-guardianship cases, and it is the natural companion to the removal-and-examination portion of Greene v. - Missing (for a later pass): Rogers v. County of San Joaquin, 487 F.3d 1288 (9th Cir. 2007) — verified present in CourtListener (cluster 797914). This is the case the brief's own Kirkpatrick quote turns on ('under Rogers, the social workers here lacked cause to forgo a warrant'), and it states the rule that a warrantless removal is unlawful absent reasonable cause to believe the child is in imminent danger of serious bodily injury and that the scope of the intrusion is reasonably necessary. Quoting a case's reliance on an authority while omitting that authority from the table is exactly the defect the brief elsewhere identified and fixed for Querbach/Kasliner.

Humphries, 562 U.S. 29 (2010) — the Supreme Court's partial reversal of the mapped Humphries decision, holding Monell's policy requirement applies to claims for prospective relief. The brief confines this to a prose note, but Humphries is mapped to six nodes (DEC-01, DEC-03, FAIL-06, PMC-05, PMC-06, INP-09) and a practitioner reading the case table alone would cite it without its subsequent history. This is the same failure mode the brief corrected for Greene v. Camreta by promoting Camreta into the table; it should be corrected the same way here.
- Missing (for a later pass): Stanley v. Illinois, 405 U.S. 645 (1972) — the constitutional source of a parent's right to a hearing on fitness before the state takes custody of a child. INP-05, DEC-05 and OUT-01 rest on Troxel alone for the fitness presumption; Stanley is the case that makes it a procedural entitlement in a dependency posture rather than a substantive preference in a private visitation dispute, and Troxel is a poor substitute because it arose between private parties with no state child-protection action at all.
- Missing (for a later pass): M. L. B. v. S. L. J., 519 U.S. 102 (1996) — holding a state may not condition appeal from a termination judgment on the parent's ability to pay record preparation fees. The brief builds an appellate-remedy layer at FAIL-01 and OUT-02 (Geist, T.
