# FL — 45 authorities, verified 2026-09-20

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Urquhart v. Helmich**, 947 So. 2d 539 (Fla. 1st DCA 2006)
  Holding: A physician who reported suspected child abuse was immune from civil liability under section 39.203 because the record showed no evidence overcoming the good-faith presumption, and summary judgment for the reporter was affirmed.
  Why here: Urquhart is the Florida appellate application of the reporter-immunity statute that accompanies the universal duty to report.
  Quote: “Section 39.203, Florida Statutes affords a general grant of immunity to a person who makes a report of child abuse in good faith.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1801944/urquhart-v-helmich/
- **Department of Health & Rehabilitative Services v. Yamuni**, 529 So. 2d 258 (Fla. 1988)
  Holding: Florida's child welfare agency owes a statutory duty of care to prevent further harm to a child once reports of abuse are received, and caseworker investigation and protective-supervision activity is operational-level conduct for which sovereign immunity is waived under section 768.28.
  Why here: Yamuni converts the mandatory-report statute into an enforceable duty: once a report arrives, the agency owes the child a duty of care.
  Quote: “We agree with the district court below that HRS has a statutory duty of care to prevent further harm to children when reports of child abuse are received.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1134313/dept-of-health-rehab-servs-v-yamuni/
- **Floyd v. Department of Children & Families**, 855 So. 2d 204 (Fla. 1st DCA 2003)
  Holding: Summary judgment for the Department was reversed in a wrongful death action alleging that its investigator returned a child to a home with a known abuser, because reporting-related statutory immunity does not protect officials who fail to carry out their duty to protect the child.
  Why here: Floyd separates immunity for making or acting on a report from liability for failing to act on what the reports disclosed.
  Quote: “it was not intended to protect those who fail to fulfill their duty to protect children”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1909591/floyd-v-department-of-children-and-famil/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **31 Foster Children v. Bush**, 329 F.3d 1255 (11th Cir. 2003)
  Holding: In a systemic challenge to Florida's foster care system, standing must be shown claim by claim; some plaintiffs' claims were moot and others were dismissed for lack of standing, leaving only counts for which a particular plaintiff demonstrated injury in fact.
  Why here: This is the Eleventh Circuit template for systemic litigation aimed at Florida's child welfare agency as a whole.
  Quote: “Only Reggie B. has standing to pursue the procedural due process claims set out in Count II, because he is the only plaintiff who has demonstrated injury in fact”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/76190/foster-children-v-jeb-bush-kathleen-kearney-chuck-bates-robert/
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The 'reasonable efforts' requirement of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act creates no privately enforceable right, so beneficiaries could not sue state child welfare officials under section 1983 to enforce it.
  Why here: Because private enforcement is unavailable, federal reviews and audits are the practical oversight mechanism for Title IV-E compliance.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Department of Health & Rehabilitative Services v. Gainesville Sun Publishing Co.**, 582 So. 2d 725 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991)
  Holding: A trial court may order confidential child abuse records produced for in camera inspection and may find good cause for disclosure without an evidentiary hearing, balancing the child's and siblings' privacy against the public interest in evaluating agency performance.
  Why here: Public scrutiny of how the agency handled a case is an express component of the good-cause balance, which is how outside oversight is triggered.
  Quote: “Under the circumstances of this case, the trial court did not err in finding good cause for disclosure without an evidentiary hearing.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1790509/dhrs-v-gainesville-sun-pub-co/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **J.P.H. v. Florida Department of Children & Families**, 39 So. 3d 560 (Fla. 1st DCA 2010)
  Holding: Termination of parental rights to Indian children was reversed because the trial court applied Florida's clear-and-convincing standard instead of ICWA's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard and wrongly denied the tribe intervention for lack of Florida counsel.
  Why here: J.P.H. is the clearest Florida holding that ICWA displaces Chapter 39's proof standard and guarantees the tribe's right to intervene.
  Quote: “which requires that any order terminating parental rights to an Indian child be supported "by evidence beyond a reasonable doubt," rather than the clear and convincing evidence standard”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2545844/jph-v-florida-department-of-children-families/
- **Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Department of Children & Families**, 959 So. 2d 761 (Fla. 4th DCA 2007)
  Holding: A trial court's finding of good cause to deviate from ICWA's placement preferences is reviewed for abuse of discretion, and the deviation was affirmed where the record showed the court understood the statutory scheme and found the tribal family could not presently meet the child's needs.
  Why here: This is the leading Florida decision applying ICWA's placement-preference scheme once a tribe intervenes in a state dependency case.
  Quote: “choosing to apply Florida's abuse of discretion standard to the review of a trial court's finding of good cause to deviate”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1140780/seminole-tribe-v-dept-of-children/
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: 'Domicile' in ICWA section 1911(a) is defined by uniform federal law, not state law; twins born off-reservation to parents domiciled on the Choctaw reservation were themselves domiciled there, so the tribal court had exclusive jurisdiction and the state adoption decree was reversed.
  Why here: Holyfield is the Supreme Court's foundational ICWA decision and the starting point for any Florida jurisdictional analysis involving an Indian child, including the threshold question of exclusive tribal jurisdiction.
  Quote: “These congressional objectives make clear that a rule of domicile that would permit individual Indian parents to defeat the ICWA's jurisdictional scheme is inconsistent with what Congress intended”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl**, 570 U.S. 637 (2013)
  Holding: ICWA section 1912(f)'s beyond-a-reasonable-doubt 'continued custody' requirement does not apply to a parent who never had custody, section 1912(d)'s remedial-efforts requirement applies only where termination would precipitate the breakup of an Indian family, and section 1915(a)'s preferences do not bar a non-Indian adoption when no preferred party has sought to adopt.
  Why here: Adoptive Couple directly qualifies the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt proposition Florida's J.P.H. draws from section 1912(f), so INP-11 overstates that holding without it.
  Quote: “§1912(f) does not apply where the Indian parent never had custody of the Indian child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/931616/adoptive-couple-v-baby-girl/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court upheld Congress's Article I authority to enact ICWA and rejected the anticommandeering challenges on the merits, while vacating the equal protection and nondelegation rulings because no petitioner had standing to press them.
  Why here: Brackeen is the current baseline for ICWA's constitutional validity and binding force on Florida state courts, and it is the authority a 2026-dated brief must cite alongside the older Florida DCA decisions.
  Quote: “the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners' challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/

## DEC-01 · Screen-Out / Unfounded at Intake
- **Department of Health & Rehabilitative Services v. Yamuni**, 529 So. 2d 258 (Fla. 1988)
  Holding: Florida's child welfare agency owes a statutory duty of care to prevent further harm to a child once reports of abuse are received, and caseworker investigation and protective-supervision activity is operational-level conduct for which sovereign immunity is waived under section 768.28.
  Why here: Closing or screening out a case without assigning protective supervision exposed the agency to liability in Yamuni, so screen-out decisions are not immune judgment calls.
  Quote: “We agree with the district court below that HRS has a statutory duty of care to prevent further harm to children when reports of child abuse are received.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1134313/dept-of-health-rehab-servs-v-yamuni/
- **Floyd v. Department of Children & Families**, 855 So. 2d 204 (Fla. 1st DCA 2003)
  Holding: Summary judgment for the Department was reversed in a wrongful death action alleging that its investigator returned a child to a home with a known abuser, because reporting-related statutory immunity does not protect officials who fail to carry out their duty to protect the child.
  Why here: Closing an investigation and returning the child despite reported indicators of abuse can be actionable negligence.
  Quote: “it was not intended to protect those who fail to fulfill their duty to protect children”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1909591/floyd-v-department-of-children-and-famil/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative federal constitutional duty on a state agency to protect a child from violence by a private actor, so the agency's failure to remove Joshua DeShaney was not a constitutional violation.
  Why here: A decision to screen out or close a case is not itself a federal constitutional violation, which is why Florida tort law (Yamuni, Floyd) carries this node.
  Quote: “nothing in the language of the Due Process Clause itself requires the State to protect the life, liberty, and property of its citizens against invasion by private actors”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Trianon Park Condominium Ass'n v. City of Hialeah**, 468 So. 2d 912 (Fla. 1985)
  Holding: Section 768.28 waived sovereign immunity but created no new causes of action; sorting governmental activity into four categories, the Court held there has never been a common-law duty to individual citizens for the enforcement of police-power functions, so the city was not liable for negligent building-code inspection.
  Why here: Before a screen-out or closure can be actionable, Trianon Park requires the plaintiff to identify an underlying common-law or statutory duty of care, which is precisely the work Yamuni's statutory-duty holding does.
  Quote: “First, for there to be governmental tort liability, there must be either an underlying common law or statutory duty of care with respect to the alleged negligent conduct.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1821287/trianon-park-condominium-v-city-of-hialeah/
- **Wallace v. Dean**, 3 So. 3d 1035 (Fla. 2009)
  Holding: Where sheriff's deputies responded to a 911 call, engaged an unresponsive woman, conducted a safety check and assured others that no ambulance was needed, the undertaker's doctrine imposed a common-law duty of care, and because that conduct was operational rather than discretionary, sovereign immunity did not bar the wrongful-death claim.
  Why here: Wallace is the modern statement of when a government actor who undertakes to check on a person at risk assumes a duty, which is the analysis applied when an investigator looks in on a reported child and then closes the case.
  Quote: “we further hold that the affirmative actions of the deputies involved in this case were operational in nature; therefore, sovereign immunity does not bar the plaintiff-petitioner's negligence-based wrongful-death claim”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1598629/wallace-v-dean/

## FAIL-05 · Discrimination / Retaliation
- **Palmore v. Sidoti**, 466 U.S. 429 (1984)
  Holding: Reversing a Florida custody decision, the Court held that the possible effects of private racial prejudice cannot justify removing a child from the custody of a parent; such a racial classification cannot survive equal protection review.
  Why here: Palmore, itself a Florida case, is the controlling authority that race may not drive custody or placement decisions.
  Quote: “Private biases may be outside the reach of the law, but the law cannot, directly or indirectly, give them effect.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/111160/palmore-v-sidoti/
- **Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Department of Children & Families**, 959 So. 2d 761 (Fla. 4th DCA 2007)
  Holding: A trial court's finding of good cause to deviate from ICWA's placement preferences is reviewed for abuse of discretion, and the deviation was affirmed where the record showed the court understood the statutory scheme and found the tribal family could not presently meet the child's needs.
  Why here: Deviation from ICWA preferences requires a reasoned good-cause finding, which is the safeguard against placement decisions that override tribal affiliation.
  Quote: “choosing to apply Florida's abuse of discretion standard to the review of a trial court's finding of good cause to deviate”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1140780/seminole-tribe-v-dept-of-children/
- **31 Foster Children v. Bush**, 329 F.3d 1255 (11th Cir. 2003)
  Holding: In a systemic challenge to Florida's foster care system, standing must be shown claim by claim; some plaintiffs' claims were moot and others were dismissed for lack of standing, leaving only counts for which a particular plaintiff demonstrated injury in fact.
  Why here: The complaint included a black-children subclass alleging racial discrimination in care, which the standing analysis governs.
  Quote: “Only Reggie B. has standing to pursue the procedural due process claims set out in Count II, because he is the only plaintiff who has demonstrated injury in fact”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/76190/foster-children-v-jeb-bush-kathleen-kearney-chuck-bates-robert/
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: 'Domicile' in ICWA section 1911(a) is defined by uniform federal law, not state law; twins born off-reservation to parents domiciled on the Choctaw reservation were themselves domiciled there, so the tribal court had exclusive jurisdiction and the state adoption decree was reversed.
  Why here: It grounds ICWA in Congress's finding that Indian children were being removed from their families and culture by non-tribal agencies, which is the interest the placement-preference and deviation rules protect.
  Quote: “These congressional objectives make clear that a rule of domicile that would permit individual Indian parents to defeat the ICWA's jurisdictional scheme is inconsistent with what Congress intended”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)
  Holding: The Court upheld Congress's Article I authority to enact ICWA and rejected the anticommandeering challenges on the merits, while vacating the equal protection and nondelegation rulings because no petitioner had standing to press them.
  Why here: The equal protection challenge to ICWA's preferences was dismissed for want of standing rather than rejected on the merits, so the race-classification question remains open and must be described as unresolved.
  Quote: “the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners' challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9406660/haaland-v-brackeen/

## FAIL-06 · Disclosure / Transparency Failure
- **In re Records of the Department of Children & Family Services (Sarasota Herald-Tribune v. Department of Children & Family Services)**, 873 So. 2d 506 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004)
  Holding: A circuit court considering a petition for access to confidential child abuse records must conduct an in camera review before ruling; refusing to inspect the records was an abuse of discretion and the denial of access was reversed.
  Why here: A blanket refusal to review withheld records is itself reversible error, which is the remedy for disclosure failures.
  Quote: “the circuit court abused its discretion when it failed to conduct an in camera review of the records to which the Herald-Tribune seeks access”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7825351/sarasota-herald-tribune-v-department-of-children-family-services/
- **Department of Health & Rehabilitative Services v. Gainesville Sun Publishing Co.**, 582 So. 2d 725 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991)
  Holding: A trial court may order confidential child abuse records produced for in camera inspection and may find good cause for disclosure without an evidentiary hearing, balancing the child's and siblings' privacy against the public interest in evaluating agency performance.
  Why here: The decision forecloses the agency's argument that it may withhold records until the requester first proves good cause.
  Quote: “Under the circumstances of this case, the trial court did not err in finding good cause for disclosure without an evidentiary hearing.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1790509/dhrs-v-gainesville-sun-pub-co/
