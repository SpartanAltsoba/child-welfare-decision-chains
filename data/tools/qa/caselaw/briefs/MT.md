## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **Gross v. Myers**, 229 Mont. 509, 748 P.2d 459 (1987)
  Holding: A licensed clinical social worker who reported abuse disclosed by a client in a confidential therapy group was a mandatory reporter under § 41-3-201 and was immune under § 41-3-203 from the client's negligence, privacy, confidentiality and emotional-distress claims because no bad faith or malice was shown.
  Why here: This is the Montana Supreme Court's controlling construction of the § 41-3-203 immunity that shields a mandated professional who reports suspected abuse, and it fixes bad faith or malicious purpose as the only way to defeat it.
  Quote: “Unless Ms. Myers acted in bad faith or with malicious purpose, she is immune from civil liability and summary judgment would be proper.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/879367/gross-v-myers/
- **Newville v. State, Department of Family Services**, 267 Mont. 237, 883 P.2d 793 (1994)
  Holding: The Department of Family Services is not shielded by quasi-judicial immunity or by the § 41-3-203 reporter immunity from tort liability for negligently failing to protect a foster child who was severely injured by her foster father, and it was reversible error to refuse an instruction on the Department's statutory duty to report the incident to the county attorney.
  Why here: The Court held that the § 41-3-203 immunity protects individual reporters such as teachers, doctors and psychologists and does not extend to the agency itself, which is the key limit on that statute.
  Quote: “We hold the Department is not immune from tort liability for its failure to protect R.M. in this case.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/883232/newville-v-state-dept-of-family-services/

## INP-02 · Voluntary / Third-Party Report
- **Gross v. Myers**, 229 Mont. 509, 748 P.2d 459 (1987)
  Holding: A licensed clinical social worker who reported abuse disclosed by a client in a confidential therapy group was a mandatory reporter under § 41-3-201 and was immune under § 41-3-203 from the client's negligence, privacy, confidentiality and emotional-distress claims because no bad faith or malice was shown.
  Why here: It shows that the same statutory immunity attaches to the act of reporting itself, so a reporter who is wrong about the report is still protected absent bad faith.
  Quote: “Unless Ms. Myers acted in bad faith or with malicious purpose, she is immune from civil liability and summary judgment would be proper.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/879367/gross-v-myers/
- **Calabretta v. Floyd**, 189 F.3d 808 (9th Cir. 1999)
  Holding: A social worker and a police officer who coerced entry into a family home to investigate an anonymous abuse report, without a warrant, consent or exigency, and who had a child's clothing removed for a visual inspection, were not entitled to qualified immunity; the administrative-search doctrine does not exempt child-protection home entries.
  Why here: It holds that an anonymous report of bruises was not itself an exigency justifying a warrantless entry, which limits what a third-party report can authorize.
  Quote: “Under White, appellants' claim, that “a search warrant is not required for home investigatory visits by social workers,” is simply not the law.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7078750/calabretta-v-floyd/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **In re J.C.**, 2008 MT 127, 343 Mont. 30, 183 P.3d 22
  Holding: Termination was affirmed; a youth-in-need-of-care adjudication is not a prerequisite to temporary investigative authority under § 41-3-433 or to a court-ordered treatment plan under § 41-3-443(1) where the parents admit or stipulate to the allegations.
  Why here: It explains that the court may authorize the Department to investigate allegations of abuse, neglect or abandonment on petition, for no longer than 90 days.
  Quote: “a YINC adjudication is not always required for TIA and court-approved treatment plans”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8071677/in-re-jc/

  Why here: It describes the statutory route by which the court, on the Department's petition, authorizes investigation and protective services under §§ 41-3-402 and 41-3-403.
  Quote: “We hold, therefore, that an interlocutory order granting temporary investigative authority and protective services is not appealable under Rule 1, M.R.App.P.”

- **In re B.J.J.**, 2019 MT 129, 396 Mont. 108, 443 P.3d 488
  Holding: Termination was affirmed: the Department made the reasonable efforts required by § 41-3-423(1) where it prepared and explained a treatment plan and offered services, even though the father never signed or returned the plan and the court approved it on the Department's motion.
  Why here: It shows that a parent's initial willingness to cooperate does not convert the case into a voluntary one; the plan is imposed by court order once the parent fails to sign.

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Mercer v. Montana Department of Public Health & Human Services**, 2025 MT 9
  Holding: A mandatory preliminary injunction requiring DPHHS to disclose child abuse and neglect case records to a state legislator conducting a statutory review under § 41-3-205(4) was affirmed; the legislator had an absolute statutory right to the described records subject only to the statute's own limits, and a stipulation barring further disclosure minimized any harm to the Department.
  Why here: It is the Montana Supreme Court's enforcement of legislative oversight over DPHHS child-protection files and the concrete remedy — mandatory injunction — when the agency withholds them.
  Quote: “This statute requires that child abuse and neglect “case records” be kept confidential but also provides for disclosure of such records upon satisfaction of statutory criteria”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10316885/mercer-v-dphhs/
- **Shockley v. Cascade County**, 2014 MT 281, 376 Mont. 493, 336 P.3d 375
  Holding: Any Montana citizen has standing under Article II, Section 9 to seek judicial relief for a denial of access to the documents of any Montana public body, without needing a nexus to or residence in that body's jurisdiction; the individual-privacy limitation and any confidentiality provisions remain to be applied on remand.
  Why here: It gives any citizen — not just a party or resident — standing to compel production of the records that make outside oversight of a public agency possible.
  Quote: “We hold Article II, Section 9 grants a right to judicial relief to a Montana citizen alleging denial of access to a document of a Montana public body.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2744364/shockley-v-cascade-county/
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The 'reasonable efforts' clause of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act, 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15), does not create a right enforceable by private plaintiffs under § 1983, nor an implied private right of action; compliance is enforced by the Secretary through the plan-approval and funding process.
  Why here: It explains why federal child-welfare compliance is policed through administrative review and funding rather than private litigation, making oversight findings the practical remedy.
  Quote: “the “reasonable efforts” language does not unambiguously confer an enforceable right upon the Act's beneficiaries”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Schoof v. Nesbit**, 2014 MT 6, 373 Mont. 226, 316 P.3d 831
  Holding: Reversed and remanded: a county resident has standing to pursue right-to-know and right-of-participation claims without alleging an injury distinguishable from that of the public generally, because the personal stake those provisions require is the opportunity to observe and participate in the public body's decision-making, and Fleenor is overruled to the extent it held otherwise.
  Why here: It is the standing decision Shockley v. Cascade County builds on, and it removes the 'injury distinguishable from the public generally' barrier that agencies had used to defeat citizen oversight suits.
  Quote: “we hold that Schoof has standing to pursue further discovery on his claims, and overrule Fleenor to the extent that it is inconsistent with this opinion”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2648684/schoof-v-nesbit/

## INP-10 · Agency Escalation (Screening → Investigation)

  Why here: It holds that the Department must continue investigating, rather than escalate to placement, when the initial inquiry raises objective safety concerns about the proposed caregiver.
  Quote: “This further investigation when objective concerns regarding the safety of the non-custodial parent are raised during the initial investigation is required.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6444484/matter-of-dh-yinc/
- **In re J.C.**, 2008 MT 127, 343 Mont. 30, 183 P.3d 22
  Holding: Termination was affirmed; a youth-in-need-of-care adjudication is not a prerequisite to temporary investigative authority under § 41-3-433 or to a court-ordered treatment plan under § 41-3-443(1) where the parents admit or stipulate to the allegations.
  Why here: It identifies § 41-3-433 as the escalation mechanism from screening to court-authorized investigation.
  Quote: “a YINC adjudication is not always required for TIA and court-approved treatment plans”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8071677/in-re-jc/
- **In re E.Y.R.**, 2019 MT 189, 396 Mont. 515, 446 P.3d 1117
  Holding: Termination of an out-of-state, non-custodial father's rights was reversed for ineffective assistance of counsel and the case rewound to the adjudication stage: on removal from the custodial parent the Department must first consider placement with the non-custodial parent after a preliminary assessment, and may expand the investigation or impose an ICPC or treatment plan only where objective, demonstrable circumstances show an imminent safety risk.
  Why here: It states the escalation rule at the investigation stage: the Department expands its preliminary inquiry into fuller investigation only when objective, demonstrable circumstances indicate an imminent safety threat to the child.
  Quote: “Upon removal of a child from a custodial parent, the Department must first consider placement of the child with the non-custodial parent.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6659262/in-re-eyr/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: ICWA's term 'domicile' has a uniform federal meaning; twins born off-reservation to reservation-domiciled parents were themselves domiciled on the reservation, so 25 U.S.C. § 1911(a) vested exclusive jurisdiction in the tribal court and the state adoption decree was vacated.
  Why here: It is the Supreme Court's controlling decision on when a Montana district court has no jurisdiction at all because the Indian child is domiciled on a reservation.
  Quote: “In enacting the ICWA Congress confirmed that, in child custody proceedings involving Indian children domiciled on the reservation, tribal jurisdiction was exclusive as to the States.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **In re L.D.**, 2018 MT 60, 391 Mont. 33, 414 P.3d 768
  Holding: The district court abused its discretion by terminating parental rights without a conclusive tribal determination of the child's membership or eligibility where, for eighteen months, the court and parties had reason to believe the child might be an Indian child; the Department's passive reliance on the tribe's inaction and the parents' beliefs did not satisfy its ICWA burden.
  Why here: It imposes an affirmative duty on the Department to ask, actively investigate and obtain a conclusive tribal determination whenever there is reason to believe a child may be an Indian child.
  Quote: “Contrary to the State's assertion, a parent cannot waive application of ICWA by stipulation or acquiescence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6656217/in-re-ld/
- **In re K.B.**, 2013 MT 133, 370 Mont. 254
  Holding: Termination was reversed and remanded for a new hearing because the record did not substantiate ten-day ICWA notice to the mother and the tribe, the ICWA qualified expert did not testify that continued custody was likely to result in serious emotional or physical damage, and the court made no findings on active efforts.
  Why here: It is the Montana Supreme Court's enforcement decision on the three operative ICWA duties — notice, qualified expert testimony and active efforts — and it holds that active efforts must be shown beyond a reasonable doubt.
  Quote: “ICWA sets minimum federal standards for proceedings involving Indian children, and we are compelled to make sure its requirements are followed.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8071989/in-re-kb/
- **In re P.T.D.**, 2018 MT 206, 392 Mont. 376, 424 P.3d 619
  Holding: Termination of a putative father's rights to an Indian child was affirmed without active-efforts proof or qualified expert testimony because, under Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl, 25 U.S.C. § 1912(d) and (f) do not apply where the parent never had custody and no Indian family relationship existed to break up.
  Why here: It marks the outer limit of ICWA's § 1912 protections in Montana: a putative father who never had custody and never established a relationship cannot invoke active efforts or the qualified-expert requirement.
  Quote: “Although the District Court order does not comply with ICWA requirements, the § 1912 ICWA provisions do not apply because a family relationship does not exist.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6657042/in-re-ptd/
- **In re G.S.**, 2002 MT 245, 312 Mont. 108, 59 P.3d 1063
  Holding: An order adjudicating two Indian children youths in need of care and granting DPHHS temporary legal custody was affirmed: as a matter of first impression, the evidentiary standard for ICWA 'active efforts' under 25 U.S.C. § 1912(d) matches the underlying proceeding — clear and convincing evidence where the proceeding is foster-care placement under § 1912(e), and proof beyond a reasonable doubt where it is termination under § 1912(f).
  Why here: It is the source of Montana's ICWA active-efforts burden of proof, and it shows the rule is tiered — beyond a reasonable doubt only when the underlying proceeding is termination, clear and convincing evidence for foster-care placement.
  Quote: “we conclude that the proper evidentiary standard for determining "active efforts" under § 1912(d) is the same standard we apply to the underlying ICWA proceeding”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8070786/in-re-gs/
- **Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl**, 570 U.S. 637 (2013)
  Holding: The South Carolina judgment awarding custody to the biological father was reversed: 25 U.S.C. § 1912(f) does not apply where the Indian parent never had custody of the Indian child, § 1912(d)'s remedial-efforts requirement is inapplicable where the parent abandoned the child before birth and never had custody, and § 1915(a)'s placement preferences do not bar a non-Indian adoption when no other eligible candidate has sought to adopt.
  Why here: It is the Supreme Court holding on which Montana's In re P.T.D. and In re J.S. entirely turn, and it marks the outer boundary of ICWA's § 1912 protections where the parent never had custody of the child.
  Quote: “§1912(f) does not apply in cases where the Indian parent never had custody of the Indian child”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/931616/adoptive-couple-v-baby-girl/

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **Rogers v. County of San Joaquin**, 487 F.3d 1288 (9th Cir. 2007)
  Holding: A social worker who removed children without a warrant violated the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments where unsanitary home conditions, malnourishment and a child being locked in her room at night did not establish reasonable cause to believe the children faced serious bodily harm in the time it would take to obtain a warrant; reliance on factors closely tied to economic status would border on the unconstitutional.
  Why here: It is the Ninth Circuit's controlling statement of when protective custody may be taken without prior judicial authorization.
  Quote: “Thus, we hold that, under any view of the facts, the Rogerses' Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights were violated when Royal removed the children without a warrant.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/797914/rogers-v-county-of-san-joaquin/
- **Kirkpatrick v. County of Washoe**, 843 F.3d 784 (9th Cir. 2016) (en banc)
  Holding: Social workers who took a two-day-old from her mother in a hospital maternity ward without a warrant should have obtained one, but were entitled to qualified immunity because that obligation was not clearly established; summary judgment was reversed on the Monell claim because there was evidence the county maintained a policy of seizing children without warrants in non-exigent circumstances.
  Why here: It holds that a newborn under continuous hospital supervision is not in imminent danger, so protective custody there requires prior judicial authorization.
  Quote: “the social workers here lacked cause to forgo a warrant if they had adequate time to pursue one through the ordinary judicial process without risking B.W.'s well-being”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4328788/jamie-kirkpatrick-v-county-of-washoe/
- **Keates v. Koile**, 883 F.3d 1228 (9th Cir. 2018)
  Holding: Dismissal of a mother's and daughter's familial-association claims on qualified immunity was reversed: taking a child from her mother at a hospital and continuing to detain her at a treatment center after professionals concluded she was a low suicide risk required reasonable cause to believe she faced imminent danger, and concern about the mother's lack of health insurance did not supply it.
  Why here: It holds that protective custody must end when the factual basis for imminent danger dissolves; continued detention is separately actionable.
  Quote: “Lack of health insurance, by contrast, does not provide a reasonable cause to believe a child is in imminent danger.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4474827/ellen-keates-v-michael-koile/
- **In re B.P.**, 2000 MT 39, 298 Mont. 287, 995 P.2d 982
  Holding: An interlocutory order granting temporary investigative authority and protective services is not appealable under Rule 1, M.R.App.P.; review must be sought by certiorari, habeas corpus or supervisory control, although the Court suspended the rules and reached the merits here because the parties had relied on a contrary prior statement.
  Why here: The TIA order here immediately removed the children into therapeutic foster care, showing the front-end custodial power the statute confers.
  Quote: “We hold, therefore, that an interlocutory order granting temporary investigative authority and protective services is not appealable under Rule 1, M.R.App.P.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8070481/in-re-bp/
- **Wallis ex rel. Wallis v. Spencer**, 202 F.3d 1126 (9th Cir. 2000)
  Holding: Summary judgment for the City was reversed in substantial part: officials may not seize children on an uncorroborated account of abuse without first pursuing reasonable avenues of investigation, and absent a reasonable concern that physical evidence will dissipate or an urgent medical problem the state must notify parents and obtain judicial approval before subjecting a seized child to an investigatory physical examination.
  Why here: It holds that protective custody taken on a bizarre, uncorroborated third-party account, without any attempt to investigate it, cannot be defended as an emergency.
  Quote: “the police cannot seize children suspected of being abused or neglected unless reasonable avenues of investigation are first pursued”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7079922/wallis-ex-rel-wallis-v-spencer/

## DEC-04 · Emergency Removal Decision
- **Rogers v. County of San Joaquin**, 487 F.3d 1288 (9th Cir. 2007)

  Why here: It is the case that sets the emergency-removal threshold for Montana: reasonable cause to believe the child is likely to suffer serious bodily harm in the time needed to get a warrant.

  Holding: Social workers who took a two-day-old from her mother in a hospital maternity ward without a warrant should have obtained one, but were entitled to qualified immunity because that obligation was not clearly established; summary judgment was reversed on the Monell claim because there was evidence the county maintained a policy of seizing children without warrants in non-exigent circumstances.
  Why here: It is the en banc statement that if there was time to get a warrant there was no exigency, which is the operative test for any Montana emergency-removal decision.

## OUT-01 · Reunification
- **In re A.J.C.**, 2018 MT 234, 393 Mont. 9, 427 P.3d 59
  Holding: Reversed: once the father completed his court-ordered treatment plan, the district court unconstitutionally violated his fundamental right to parent by permanently placing the child with the maternal grandmother, a nonparent, and denying him placement; the abuse and neglect proceeding should have been dismissed at that point.
  Why here: It is the Montana authority requiring reunification once the parent has done what the court ordered, and it is the strongest state-law protection against open-ended agency custody.
  Quote: “At that time, this proceeding should have been dismissed, leaving further consideration of the parenting relationship and parenting plan establishment in the DR matter.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6657301/in-re-ajc/
- **In re J.H.**, 2016 MT 35, 382 Mont. 214, 367 P.3d 339
  Holding: The court affirmed approval of a permanency plan and an award of long-term custody until the child reached 18: the Department's ICPC-based efforts to place the child with his out-of-state father were reasonable, and placement with the long-term caregiver alongside his sibling served the child's best interests.
  Why here: It is the Montana case on how far the Department must go, including through the ICPC, before reunification with a non-custodial out-of-state parent may be rejected.
  Quote: “Rather, “the child's health and safety are of paramount concern,” when determining what efforts are required.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8072335/in-re-jh/
- **In re B.J.J.**, 2019 MT 129, 396 Mont. 108, 443 P.3d 488
  Holding: Termination was affirmed: the Department made the reasonable efforts required by § 41-3-423(1) where it prepared and explained a treatment plan and offered services, even though the father never signed or returned the plan and the court approved it on the Department's motion.
  Why here: It defines how much the Department must do — more than merely suggesting services, but not herculean efforts — before reunification may be abandoned.
  Quote: “From our review of the record, we conclude the Department provided reasonable efforts as required by § 41-3-423(1), MCA.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6658899/in-re-bjj/
- **In re L.M.A.T.**, 2002 MT 163, 310 Mont. 422, 51 P.3d 504
  Holding: A district court finding under § 41-3-403(2) that preservation or reunification services are not required because of aggravated circumstances must be supported by clear and convincing evidence, which a certified judgment for attempted rape of a child supplied here.
  Why here: It is the Montana authority defining when reunification services may lawfully be withheld, which forecloses the reunification outcome from the outset.
  Quote: “the district court's findings under § 41-3-403(2), MCA (1999), must be supported by clear and convincing evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8070781/in-re-lmat/
- **In re K.L.**, 2014 MT 28, 373 Mont. 421, 318 P.3d 691
  Holding: Termination of both parents' rights was affirmed; § 41-3-423 obliges the Department to make reasonable efforts to reunify but the term cannot be defined in the abstract, each case must be evaluated on its own facts, and the statute does not require herculean efforts, so the Department's unsuccessful attempts to secure scarce, waitlisted mental-health treatment for the mother satisfied it.
  Why here: It fixes the ceiling on what the Department must do before reunification may be abandoned, holding that months-long waiting lists for the very services a psychologist recommended do not defeat a finding of reasonable efforts.
  Quote: “each case must be evaluated on its own facts. But clearly the statute does not require herculean efforts.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8072118/in-re-kl/

## OUT-02 · Termination of Parental Rights / Adoption
- **Santosky v. Kramer**, 455 U.S. 745 (1982)
  Holding: Before a State may sever parental rights, the Due Process Clause requires proof by at least clear and convincing evidence; New York's preponderance standard was unconstitutional.
  Why here: It is the federal constitutional floor that Montana's § 41-3-609 clear-and-convincing requirement implements, and the authority to cite if any Montana proceeding applies a lesser standard to termination.
  Quote: “due process requires that the State support its allegations by at least clear and convincing evidence”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/110676/santosky-v-kramer/
- **In re the Custody and Parental Rights of C.J.K.**, 2005 MT 67, 326 Mont. 289, 109 P.3d 232
  Holding: The party seeking termination must prove each statutory requirement of § 41-3-609 by clear and convincing evidence, and in doing so the court gives primary consideration to the child's physical, mental and emotional conditions and needs, which take precedence over parental rights.
  Why here: It is the clean Montana statement of both the burden of proof and the best-interests priority that govern every termination of parental rights in this state.
  Quote: “Moreover, the party seeking to terminate parental rights must demonstrate by clear and convincing evidence that the statutory requirements for termination have been met.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/887347/in-the-matter-of-custody-and-parental-rights-of-cjk/
- **In re the Custody and Parental Rights of D.A.**, 2008 MT 247, 344 Mont. 513, 189 P.3d 631
  Holding: The § 41-3-604(1) presumption that termination serves the child's best interests once the child has been in foster care 15 of the most recent 22 months does not relieve the State of proving the substantive § 41-3-609 grounds by clear and convincing evidence; termination was affirmed because the court made those independent findings.
  Why here: It is the Montana case that stops the ASFA 15-of-22 timeline from operating as an automatic ground for termination and keeps the State to its full burden.
  Quote: “The party seeking termination must still meet the clear and convincing burden of proof, as well as the substantive statutory requirements of § 41-3-609, MCA.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/888390/in-re-the-custody-parental-rights-of-da/
- **In re M.A.L.**, 2006 MT 299, 334 Mont. 436, 148 P.3d 606
  Holding: Termination was affirmed: a certified copy of a prior conviction for a sexual offense against a child is clear and convincing evidence of an aggravated circumstance under § 41-3-609, and the State need not separately prove the circumstances of the conviction.
  Why here: It defines both who bears the burden and what proof suffices when Montana terminates on an aggravated-circumstances ground rather than treatment-plan failure.
  Quote: “It is the State's burden to prove by clear and convincing evidence that an aggravated circumstance exists, and thus that the termination of parental rights is appropriate.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8071367/in-re-mal/
- **In re A.M.G.**, 2022 MT 175
  Holding: Termination was affirmed; the district court did not abuse its discretion in finding that the conduct or condition rendering the mother unfit — her unaddressed mental health and domestic violence treatment goals — was unlikely to change within a reasonable time.
  Why here: It supplies the Montana standard for the most contested termination element — whether the parent's disqualifying condition is likely to change in a reasonable time — and confirms that a conclusion of no change can be undermined if the Department did not make reasonable efforts.
  Quote: “In determining what constitutes a reasonable time, courts must consider that the best interest of the child, though balanced with the right to parent, is “paramount.””
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8240926/matter-of-amg-smh-yinc/
- **Jackson v. State**, 1998 MT 46, 287 Mont. 473, 956 P.2d 35
  Holding: Summary judgment for the State was reversed: once the State volunteered information about an adoptive child's biological family it assumed a duty to convey that information with due care, and there were triable issues on foreseeability and on whether the Jacksons would have adopted but for the State's alleged withholding and misrepresentation.
  Why here: This is Montana's wrongful-adoption decision: it defines the disclosure duty the State owes adoptive parents at the point a child's permanency is finalized through adoption.
  Quote: “it assumed a duty to do so with due care and to refrain from negligently misrepresenting that information to the Jacksons”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/884783/jackson-v-state/

## OUT-03 · Guardianship / Kinship Permanency
- **In re J.S.**, 2014 MT 79, 374 Mont. 329, 321 P.3d 103
  Holding: A § 41-3-444 guardianship of an Indian child with his long-term foster family was affirmed: early ICWA notice defects were cured by the father's and the tribe's later participation, and 25 U.S.C. § 1912(d) and (e) did not defeat the guardianship where the father never had custody of the child.
  Why here: It is the leading Montana decision applying the eight § 41-3-444(1) findings that must precede a subsidized or unsubsidized guardianship, including the ICWA notice requirement.
  Quote: “Under Montana law, a guardian may be appointed for a child who has been placed in the custody of the Department during a child abuse or neglect proceeding.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8072121/in-re-js/
- **In re A.J.W.**, 2010 MT 42, 355 Mont. 264, 227 P.3d 1012
  Holding: Termination was affirmed; counsel was not ineffective for failing to move for long-term custody under § 41-3-445 or guardianship under § 41-3-444, because a parent may not petition for a guardian, the Department must consent, and there was virtually no chance the alternative would have been granted, so no prejudice was shown.
  Why here: It is the Montana authority explaining who may petition for a § 41-3-444 guardianship and that the Department's written consent is a precondition, which is the practical barrier to kinship permanency as an alternative to termination.
  Quote: “Even if there were ineffective representation, it is inconsequential unless the parent suffered prejudice as a result.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8071813/in-re-ajw/
- **In re A.J.C.**, 2018 MT 234, 393 Mont. 9, 427 P.3d 59
  Holding: Reversed: once the father completed his court-ordered treatment plan, the district court unconstitutionally violated his fundamental right to parent by permanently placing the child with the maternal grandmother, a nonparent, and denying him placement; the abuse and neglect proceeding should have been dismissed at that point.
  Why here: It limits the use of a relative caregiver as a permanency destination when a parent remains fit and available.
  Quote: “At that time, this proceeding should have been dismissed, leaving further consideration of the parenting relationship and parenting plan establishment in the DR matter.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6657301/in-re-ajc/

## OUT-06 · Child Fatality / Serious Injury
- **Newville v. State, Department of Family Services**, 267 Mont. 237, 883 P.2d 793 (1994)
  Holding: The Department of Family Services is not shielded by quasi-judicial immunity or by the § 41-3-203 reporter immunity from tort liability for negligently failing to protect a foster child who was severely injured by her foster father, and it was reversible error to refuse an instruction on the Department's statutory duty to report the incident to the county attorney.
  Why here: It is the controlling Montana authority for a damages action when a child in the Department's care suffers catastrophic injury.
  Quote: “We hold the Department is not immune from tort liability for its failure to protect R.M. in this case.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/883232/newville-v-state-dept-of-family-services/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative constitutional obligation on a state child-protection agency to protect a child from violence by a private actor, even where the agency knew of the danger and had intervened before, so the county was not liable under § 1983 for failing to remove the child.
  Why here: It is the reason a federal constitutional claim usually fails when a child is killed or injured after CPS failed to act, which is why the Montana negligence route in Newville matters.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/

## FAIL-01 · Court Reversal of Agency Decision
- **In re K.B.**, 2013 MT 133, 370 Mont. 254
  Holding: Termination was reversed and remanded for a new hearing because the record did not substantiate ten-day ICWA notice to the mother and the tribe, the ICWA qualified expert did not testify that continued custody was likely to result in serious emotional or physical damage, and the court made no findings on active efforts.
  Why here: It is the leading Montana example of an appellate court vacating a termination for the agency's and court's failure to comply with mandatory federal standards.
  Quote: “ICWA sets minimum federal standards for proceedings involving Indian children, and we are compelled to make sure its requirements are followed.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8071989/in-re-kb/
- **In re L.D.**, 2018 MT 60, 391 Mont. 33, 414 P.3d 768
  Holding: The district court abused its discretion by terminating parental rights without a conclusive tribal determination of the child's membership or eligibility where, for eighteen months, the court and parties had reason to believe the child might be an Indian child; the Department's passive reliance on the tribe's inaction and the parents' beliefs did not satisfy its ICWA burden.
  Why here: It is a Montana reversal of a termination for the court's own failure to resolve Indian-child status before proceeding.
  Quote: “Contrary to the State's assertion, a parent cannot waive application of ICWA by stipulation or acquiescence.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6656217/in-re-ld/
- **In re A.J.C.**, 2018 MT 234, 393 Mont. 9, 427 P.3d 59
  Holding: Reversed: once the father completed his court-ordered treatment plan, the district court unconstitutionally violated his fundamental right to parent by permanently placing the child with the maternal grandmother, a nonparent, and denying him placement; the abuse and neglect proceeding should have been dismissed at that point.
  Why here: It is a constitutional reversal of a district court's placement and permanency order in a dependency case.
  Quote: “At that time, this proceeding should have been dismissed, leaving further consideration of the parenting relationship and parenting plan establishment in the DR matter.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6657301/in-re-ajc/
- **In re P.D.L.**, 2004 MT 346, 324 Mont. 327, 102 P.3d 1225
  Holding: Although a parent must be represented by counsel at termination proceedings as a matter of fundamental fairness, a Rule 60(b) motion attacking a termination decree filed more than two years later was untimely and properly denied, and pro se status does not excuse compliance with procedural rules.
  Why here: It is the Montana authority on the narrow window for collaterally attacking a child-protection judgment, and it confirms that the right to counsel is part of the fundamental fairness that a late challenge cannot resurrect.
  Quote: “Fairness requires that a parent be represented by counsel at parental termination proceedings”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8071011/in-re-pdl/
- **In re A.S.**, 2004 MT 62, 320 Mont. 268, 87 P.3d 408
  Holding: The Court declined to apply Strickland to termination proceedings and adopted non-exclusive Montana factors — counsel's training and experience, and the adequacy of counsel's advocacy — then held that counsel who came to the termination hearing unprepared was ineffective, but affirmed termination because the mother showed no resulting prejudice.
  Why here: It is Montana's governing standard for attacking a termination judgment on ineffective assistance of counsel, and it supplies the prejudice requirement that In re A.J.W. and In re B.S. apply without restating its origin.
  Quote: “The above factors represent benchmark, although nonexclusive, criteria for evaluating claims of ineffective assistance of counsel in termination proceedings.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8070984/in-re-as/
- **In re D.B.**, 2007 MT 246, 339 Mont. 240, 168 P.3d 691
  Holding: Termination was reversed and remanded: the district court applied the wrong criteria by resting on 'reasonable efforts to accomplish alternatives' and the 15-of-22-month presumption instead of the two-prong § 41-3-609(1)(f) inquiry, and on remand it must decide whether the treatment plan adequately addressed the cognitively disabled mother's disabilities by setting reasonable deadlines and providing the support the law requires.
  Why here: It is a Montana reversal of a termination for applying the wrong statutory criteria, holding that reasonable-efforts findings and the 15-of-22-month presumption cannot substitute for the § 41-3-609(1)(f) inquiry.
  Quote: “The State's burden to ensure appropriateness and duty to act in good faith does not end once the court has approved a treatment plan.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8071529/in-re-db/

## FAIL-02 · Unlawful Removal
- **Rogers v. County of San Joaquin**, 487 F.3d 1288 (9th Cir. 2007)
  Holding: A social worker who removed children without a warrant violated the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments where unsanitary home conditions, malnourishment and a child being locked in her room at night did not establish reasonable cause to believe the children faced serious bodily harm in the time it would take to obtain a warrant; reliance on factors closely tied to economic status would border on the unconstitutional.
  Why here: It is the leading Ninth Circuit decision defining an unlawful warrantless removal and is the standard by which a Montana § 1983 wrongful-removal claim is measured.
  Quote: “Thus, we hold that, under any view of the facts, the Rogerses' Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights were violated when Royal removed the children without a warrant.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/797914/rogers-v-county-of-san-joaquin/
- **Kirkpatrick v. County of Washoe**, 843 F.3d 784 (9th Cir. 2016) (en banc)
  Holding: Social workers who took a two-day-old from her mother in a hospital maternity ward without a warrant should have obtained one, but were entitled to qualified immunity because that obligation was not clearly established; summary judgment was reversed on the Monell claim because there was evidence the county maintained a policy of seizing children without warrants in non-exigent circumstances.
  Why here: It is the Ninth Circuit's most authoritative recent treatment of warrantless removal, including municipal liability where an agency's ordinary practice is to remove without warrants.
  Quote: “the social workers here lacked cause to forgo a warrant if they had adequate time to pursue one through the ordinary judicial process without risking B.W.'s well-being”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4328788/jamie-kirkpatrick-v-county-of-washoe/
- **Keates v. Koile**, 883 F.3d 1228 (9th Cir. 2018)
  Holding: Dismissal of a mother's and daughter's familial-association claims on qualified immunity was reversed: taking a child from her mother at a hospital and continuing to detain her at a treatment center after professionals concluded she was a low suicide risk required reasonable cause to believe she faced imminent danger, and concern about the mother's lack of health insurance did not supply it.
  Why here: It is a recent, published circuit decision denying qualified immunity for a removal without a court order, directly applicable to Montana § 1983 claims.
  Quote: “Lack of health insurance, by contrast, does not provide a reasonable cause to believe a child is in imminent danger.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4474827/ellen-keates-v-michael-koile/
- **Calabretta v. Floyd**, 189 F.3d 808 (9th Cir. 1999)
  Holding: A social worker and a police officer who coerced entry into a family home to investigate an anonymous abuse report, without a warrant, consent or exigency, and who had a child's clothing removed for a visual inspection, were not entitled to qualified immunity; the administrative-search doctrine does not exempt child-protection home entries.
  Why here: It is binding Ninth Circuit law for Montana that child-protection workers need a warrant, consent or exigency to enter a home, and § 1983 liability follows when they do not.
  Quote: “Under White, appellants' claim, that “a search warrant is not required for home investigatory visits by social workers,” is simply not the law.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7078750/calabretta-v-floyd/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative constitutional obligation on a state child-protection agency to protect a child from violence by a private actor, even where the agency knew of the danger and had intervened before, so the county was not liable under § 1983 for failing to remove the child.
  Why here: It draws the line this dataset must respect: § 1983 liability attaches to what the State does (removal, seizure), not to what it fails to do.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Mabe v. San Bernardino County**, 237 F.3d 1101 (9th Cir. 2001)
  Holding: Summary judgment for the investigating social worker was reversed and remanded for trial: government officials must obtain prior judicial authorization before removing a child unless the information they hold at the time gives reasonable cause to believe the child is in imminent danger of serious bodily injury, and whether that exigency existed here was a disputed question of fact for the jury.
  Why here: It confirms that a caseworker who removes a child without a warrant loses qualified immunity if a reasonable jury could find the child faced no immediate threat of serious physical injury.
  Quote: “viewing the exigency evidence in the light most favorable to Mabe, a reasonable jury could conclude that Mabe's constitutional rights were violated by Perry's warrantless removal of MD”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/771798/mabe-v-san-bernardino-county/

## FAIL-03 · Federal Compliance Failure
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The 'reasonable efforts' clause of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act, 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15), does not create a right enforceable by private plaintiffs under § 1983, nor an implied private right of action; compliance is enforced by the Secretary through the plan-approval and funding process.
  Why here: It is the controlling authority that a state's failure to meet federal reasonable-efforts requirements is not privately actionable; the consequence is federal funding and corrective action.
  Quote: “the “reasonable efforts” language does not unambiguously confer an enforceable right upon the Act's beneficiaries”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative constitutional obligation on a state child-protection agency to protect a child from violence by a private actor, even where the agency knew of the danger and had intervened before, so the county was not liable under § 1983 for failing to remove the child.
  Why here: It forecloses the argument that federal child-welfare obligations create a constitutional duty to protect enforceable by the child.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Mercer v. Montana Department of Public Health & Human Services**, 2025 MT 9
  Holding: A mandatory preliminary injunction requiring DPHHS to disclose child abuse and neglect case records to a state legislator conducting a statutory review under § 41-3-205(4) was affirmed; the legislator had an absolute statutory right to the described records subject only to the statute's own limits, and a stipulation barring further disclosure minimized any harm to the Department.
  Why here: It shows how a Montana legislator's statutory review is used to examine the Department's handling of a case and to develop corrective legislation.
  Quote: “This statute requires that child abuse and neglect “case records” be kept confidential but also provides for disclosure of such records upon satisfaction of statutory criteria”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10316885/mercer-v-dphhs/

## FAIL-05 · Discrimination / Retaliation
- **In re M.C.**, 2017 MT 252, 389 Mont. 78, 403 P.3d 1266
  Holding: Termination was affirmed: a court-ordered psychological evaluation is part of the court record and may be used throughout the abuse and neglect proceeding, and the mother's claim that the Department failed to accommodate her reading disability under the ADA was not preserved because she never raised it in the district court.
  Why here: It is the Montana decision a disabled parent must know about: an ADA failure-to-accommodate claim is waived on appeal unless it is raised in the district court.

  Why here: It states ICWA's premise that protecting the child's relationship with the tribe is in the child's best interest, which is the answer to placement decisions that discount tribal ties.

## Notes
- Court structure: Montana has NO intermediate appellate court. All state authority in this brief is Montana Supreme Court (court_id 'mont'). The federal circuit for Montana is the Ninth Circuit (ca9), which supplies the § 1983 wrongful-removal law; no published Ninth Circuit or Montana federal appellate decision applying that law specifically to Montana DPHHS was found in this session, so Calabretta, Rogers, Kirkpatrick and Keates are cited as the binding circuit standard.
- No unpublished or non-citable decisions were used. Montana designates memorandum opinions with an 'N' suffix (e.g. 2016 MT 320N, 2024 MT 77N); several surfaced in searches and all were excluded. Every Montana case here is a published, citable opinion.
- Citation form: three recent Montana opinions (In re D.H., 2022 MT 37; In re A.M.G., 2022 MT 175; Mercer v. DPHHS, 2025 MT 9) carry only the Montana public-domain citation in the CourtListener cluster record; no Mont./P.3d parallel cites were present, so none are asserted. In re K.B., 2013 MT 133, likewise had only '370 Mont. 254' in the cluster. CourtListener stores duplicate clusters for many Montana cases (one from the reporter feed, one from the court's PDF); the cluster_id given is the one whose opinion text was actually read.
- The existing layers.case_law.key_cases entries in the MT node files are keyword-matched noise. Titles such as 'In re Marriage of Steinbeisser' (a dissolution case), 'Matter of K.H. & D.H., YINC', 'Matter of J.D.' and 'In re C.M.B.' carry synthetic citations of the form 'Montana Supreme Court Case da-25-0077 (2025)' and Justia URLs. None were verifiable as on point and none are carried forward here.
- Nodes with no on-point published Montana authority, populated with the nearest Montana procedure case plus the federal standard: OUT-04 (extended foster care / jurisdiction past 18) — targeted searches for Mont. Code Ann. § 52-2-603 and for continued jurisdiction past age 18 returned zero published Montana Supreme Court opinions, so OUT-04 rests on In re J.H. (long-term custody / planned permanent living arrangement to age 18 under § 41-3-445), In re J.S. (guardianship for a teenager) and In re A.J.W. (long-term custody as the statutory alternative to termination). ACT-05 (protective order) — no published Montana child-protection decision construing a Title 40 ch. 15 order of protection was found; ACT-05 rests on the emergency-protective-services and protective-services orders in In re B.P. and In re H.T., plus the Ninth Circuit's scope-of-intrusion limit in Rogers. DEC-06 (differential / alternative response) — Montana has no published case using that terminology; In re J.C. and In re B.J.J. are the closest analogues (services by admission or stipulation without a contested adjudication).
- Nodes where the federal standard is the governing authority because no Montana case exists: FAIL-03 (federal compliance failure) rests on Suter v. Artist M. (no private right to enforce reasonable efforts) and DeShaney; PMC-02 (OIG / Single Audit) and PMC-03 (CAPTA Citizen Review Panels) have no Montana case law at all — they are populated with the state right-to-know and § 41-3-205 record-access decisions (Shockley, Bitterroot Star, Mercer) that make such review possible, plus Suter for the federal enforcement channel. No Montana or federal decision was found on children missing from foster care (no node maps to it directly, but nothing exists to support one).
- Reporter duty and immunity: Gross v. Myers (1987) is the only Montana Supreme Court decision retrieved construing the § 41-3-203 immunity, and it is still the controlling construction — immunity unless the reporter acted in bad faith or with malicious purpose. Newville (1994) is the necessary companion: it holds that this immunity 'is not intended for the Department; rather, it is intended to protect individuals such as teachers, doctors, and psychologists who are required to report suspected abuse.' A search for a Montana criminal or civil case on FAILURE to report under § 41-3-207 returned zero results.
- Substantiation and central registry: Dowell v. DPHHS is the only retrieved Montana Supreme Court decision on challenging a substantiated finding. Lawyers should note two things it establishes: (1) no Montana statute or administrative rule sets any deadline for DPHHS to issue substantiation notice, conduct informal review, set a fair hearing or decide — ARM 37.5.325(1)(b) requires only a 'reasonable time and date'; and (2) admissions made in the district court abuse and neglect case collaterally estop the parent from relitigating substantiation administratively. Families should be told that facts conceded in the dependency case close off the registry challenge.
- Appealability trap: In re B.P. holds that an interlocutory order granting temporary investigative authority and protective services is NOT appealable under Rule 1, M.R.App.P.; review must be by writ of certiorari, habeas corpus or supervisory control. The Court suspended the rules to reach the merits there only because of reliance on a contrary dictum in Matter of K.H. (1985). Counsel should not assume the front-end removal order can be directly appealed. Separately, In re B.W.S. holds that the district court's failure to meet the show cause (20-day), adjudication (90-day) and disposition (20-day) deadlines does not divest it of subject matter jurisdiction — the challenge must be framed as prejudice, not jurisdiction.
- ICWA is the most developed and most consequential body of Montana child-welfare case law, which matches the state's demographics. Six decisions are included (H.T., K.B., L.D., M.S., J.S., A.L.D., P.T.D.) plus Holyfield. Two of them (K.B., M.S.) are outright reversals of terminations, and L.D. holds that a parent cannot waive ICWA by stipulation and that only a tribe may conclusively determine membership. Note the limit: In re P.T.D. applies Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl to hold § 1912(d) and (f) inapplicable where the parent never had custody. Montana also has its own Montana Indian Child Welfare Act, and the MT node files record that § 41-3-427(1)(d), (2) and § 41-3-422(5)(a)(i) require clear and convincing evidence (not probable cause) for emergency orders in ICWA cases — a state-law elevation of the standard that no retrieved opinion squarely construes.
- Greene v. Camreta, 588 F.3d 1011 (9th Cir. 2009) — the leading circuit case on seizing and interviewing a child at school without a warrant, parental consent or exigency — was retrieved but is deliberately EXCLUDED from this brief because of subsequent U.S. Supreme Court proceedings in that case (Camreta v. Greene) affecting the vitality of its Fourth Amendment holding; that subsequent history was not independently verified in this session, so the case should not be relied on without checking it. INP-08 (educational disclosure) is therefore covered by Gross v. Myers, Newville and Calabretta instead, and Montana has no published decision of its own on CPS interviews of children at school.
- T.M.B. v. West Mont & State, 2024 MT 319, was retrieved (a developmentally disabled adult sexually assaulted by an employee of a DPHHS-contracted community group home; the Court affirmed as to the State and reversed as to the private provider on a nondelegable duty of care). It is excluded because the nondelegable-duty passages could not be reliably attributed to the majority rather than the separate writings, and because it concerns an adult under the Developmental Disabilities Program rather than a child in foster care. It is nonetheless worth a lawyer's attention as the most recent Montana authority on a state contractor's duty to a person placed in its care.
- Practical gap a family should know: Montana's statutory protections at the front end are thin relative to the back end. There is a large body of published law on termination standards, treatment plans and ICWA, and almost none on screening, investigation conduct, in-home interviews, or central-registry process. The Fourth Amendment limits on caseworker entry and removal in Montana come entirely from the Ninth Circuit, not from the Montana Supreme Court — a targeted search for Montana cases applying Mont. Const. art. II, § 11 to a social worker's warrantless home entry returned zero results.

The brief reaches this rule only second-hand through In re J.H. and In re B.J.J., both of which quote it. Belongs on ACT-02 and OUT-01.

- CORRECTION to an earlier note in this brief: In re G.S. shows that Montana's ICWA active-efforts burden is TIERED, not uniformly 'beyond a reasonable doubt.' G.S. holds the § 1912(d) showing must meet the same standard as the underlying proceeding — clear and convincing evidence where the proceeding is foster-care placement under § 1912(e), and beyond a reasonable doubt only where it is termination under § 1912(f). The In re K.B. relevance sentence retained above (unchanged, as required) states the termination-level standard without that qualification; read it against G.S.
- CAUTION on Billings Gazette: it is the right-to-know framework case, but its result went AGAINST disclosure. The Court reversed the order requiring the City to identify five employees disciplined for viewing pornography on City computers, holding that an employee not occupying a position of public trust has a privacy expectation that clearly exceeds the merits of disclosure. Missoula County Public Schools reached the opposite result because the employee there DID hold a position of public trust. Requesters seeking child-welfare records should expect the individual-privacy balance, not a blanket right.
