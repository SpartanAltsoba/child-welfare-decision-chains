## INP-02 · Voluntary / Third-Party Report
- **In re Interest of B.B.**, 29 Neb. App. 1, 951 N.W.2d 526 (2020)
  Holding: The court held that the reporter-immunity provision of Neb. Rev. Stat. § 28-716 protects a person who makes a mandated child abuse or neglect report from civil or criminal liability but does not bar the juvenile court from acquiring jurisdiction over the reporting parent and her children under § 43-247(3)(a).
  Why here: The § 28-716 immunity the court construes runs to 'any person participating in ... the making of a report,' not only to listed professionals, which is the legal footing for voluntary third-party reports.
  Quote: “they are designed to compel notification of child abuse or neglect to proper authorities in order to place such children and their parents under the supervision of the juvenile court”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4798208/in-re-interest-of-bb/
- **Shearer v. Leuenberger**, 256 Neb. 566, 591 N.W.2d 762 (1999)
  Holding: The court held that the child abuse immunity provision of Neb. Rev. Stat. § 28-716 gives the Department's director and investigator state-law immunity for placing a mother's name on the Abused or Neglected Child Registry but does not reach a claim advanced under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, and affirmed dismissal because the Department and its officials in their official capacities are not 'persons' under § 1983 and the registry procedures used violated no clearly established right.
  Why here: The § 28-716 immunity the court construes runs to 'any person participating in an investigation or the making of a report,' which is the same provision that shields a non-mandated third-party reporter, and the court fixes its outer limit at maliciously false statements.
  Quote: “the immunity from liability under state law provided by § 28-716 does not apply to a claim advanced under federal law”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1313347/shearer-v-leuenberger/

  Quote: “due process rights include the right to be free from an unreasonable delay in providing a parent a meaningful hearing after the entry of an ex parte temporary custody order”

  Why here: The removal arose from a joint law-enforcement and county social services response to a corporal punishment report.
  Quote: “In child removal cases, the meaningful time and manner requirement means that the state must hold a hearing promptly after removal.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4754922/dwight-mitchell-v-dakota-county-social-services/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **In re Interest of Paxton H.**, 300 Neb. 446, 915 N.W.2d 45 (2018)
  Holding: The court held that the juvenile court did not err in ordering DHHS to arrange and pay for the child's placement and services at an out-of-state psychiatric residential treatment facility, and that such a cost order is a final, appealable order.
  Why here: The juvenile court directed the agency's course of action, illustrating judicially driven intervention within an open case.
  Quote: “we conclude that the juvenile court did not err in ordering DHHS to arrange and pay for Paxton to receive services at KidsTLC.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8284530/neb-dept-of-health-human-servs-v-penny-h-in-re-interest-of-paxton/
- **In re Interest of Mekhi S.**, 309 Neb. 529, 960 N.W.2d 732 (2021)
  Holding: The court held that a juvenile court retains jurisdiction over a child after placing the child in a guardianship, so it had authority to entertain the guardian ad litem's motion to terminate the disrupted guardianship and return the children to DHHS custody.
  Why here: The juvenile court's retained jurisdiction is what permits a court-initiated return of a child to agency custody after a permanency order.
  Quote: “the parents' rights have not been terminated, and Nebraska jurisprudence dictates that a juvenile court retains jurisdiction over a juvenile after it places the child in a guardianship”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5092727/in-re-interest-of-mekhi-s/
- **In re Interest of J.M.N.**, 237 Neb. 116, 464 N.W.2d 811 (1991)
  Holding: The court held that an order directing the Department of Social Services to pay the costs of a ward's treatment is a final, appealable order, but that the Department is not a 'parent' within Neb. Rev. Stat. § 43-290, so the order requiring it to pay for the juvenile's alcohol treatment was void from its inception and was reversed.
  Why here: A juvenile court order directing the agency to obtain and pay for a ward's treatment is the court-driven form of intervention, and the court holds such an order is void unless the statute actually reaches the agency.
  Quote: “An order which directs DSS to pay for the costs of treatment is a final order for purposes of § 25-1902.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1835037/jmn-v-nebraska-department-of-social-services/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court held that the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's requirement that States make 'reasonable efforts' to prevent removal and reunify families creates no privately enforceable right under § 1983 or directly under the Act.
  Why here: Because private enforcement is unavailable, federal oversight reviews and audits are the operative compliance mechanism.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **State ex rel. Veskrna v. Steel**, 296 Neb. 581, 894 N.W.2d 788 (2017)
  Holding: The court held that the requested judicial branch education records were public records under Neb. Rev. Stat. § 84-712.01 with no applicable statutory exception, and affirmed the mandamus judgment compelling their disclosure with one internal email redacted, together with costs and attorney fees.
  Why here: Public records litigation is the principal citizen tool for surfacing the systemic data that triggers oversight inquiries.
  Quote: “we affirm the judgment of the district court granting Veskrna access to the records found in exhibit 4, with the specified email redacted”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4413217/state-ex-rel-veskrna-v-steel/
- **OMNI Behavioral Health v. Nebraska Foster Care Review Bd.**, 277 Neb. 641, 764 N.W.2d 398 (2009)
  Holding: The court held that the State Foster Care Review Board's statutory authority under Neb. Rev. Stat. § 43-1303 to visit and observe foster care facilities is not facially unconstitutional for want of implementing regulations, and that any Fourth Amendment or state constitutional challenge to a particular home visit must be judged case by case under a reasonableness standard, affirming summary judgment for the Board.
  Why here: The Board's statewide foster care register, its case reviews, and its reports to DHHS, county welfare offices and the courts are the Nebraska oversight machinery through which systemic problems surface and prompt further agency or court action.
  Quote: “any claim that a home visit by the State Board infringes upon Fourth Amendment rights, or corresponding rights under the Nebraska Constitution, must be judged by a standard of reasonableness”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1238826/omni-behavioral-health-v-nebraska-foster-care-review-bd/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **In re Interest of Walter W.**, 274 Neb. 859, 744 N.W.2d 55 (2008)
  Holding: The court held that the ICWA and Nebraska ICWA 'active efforts' element must be proved by clear and convincing evidence rather than beyond a reasonable doubt, and that the Department met that burden, affirming the termination.
  Why here: This is Nebraska's controlling decision on the burden the State bears under ICWA and the Nebraska ICWA once a case involves an Indian child.
  Quote: “we decline to interpret § 1912(d) — and its Nebraska counterpart, § 43-1505(4) — as requiring the State to prove active efforts beyond a reasonable doubt”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2035819/in-re-interest-of-walter-w/
- **In re Guardianship of Eliza W.**, 304 Neb. 995, 938 N.W.2d 307 (2020)
  Holding: The court held that a guardianship proceeding brought by a private individual over an Indian child is a 'foster care placement' within ICWA and the Nebraska ICWA, so the Act's protections — including active efforts, qualified expert witness testimony, and counsel for an indigent parent — apply.
  Why here: It is the Nebraska Supreme Court's definitive statement of when ICWA and the Nebraska ICWA attach, reaching private proceedings and not only State-initiated ones.
  Quote: “we are not persuaded by Susan's arguments that the guardianship proceeding she initiated does not qualify as a "foster care placement" under ICWA and NICWA”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4750421/in-re-guardianship-of-eliza-w/
- **In re Interest of Mercedes L.**, 26 Neb. App. 737 (2019)
  Holding: The court held the November orders were final and appealable, affirmed on the ICWA issues raised by the incarcerated mother, but held that the December orders appointing guardians for the children were void for lack of subject matter jurisdiction and vacated them.
  Why here: The opinion applies ICWA and the Nebraska ICWA to active efforts, qualified expert witness testimony, and deviation from the placement preferences in a Nebraska juvenile case.
  Quote: “we affirm, but we hold that the court's orders of December 12, 2017, appointing a guardian for each of the children are void for lack of subject matter jurisdiction”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4583304/in-re-interest-of-mercedes-l/
- **In re Adoption of Micah H.**, 301 Neb. 437, 918 N.W.2d 834 (2018)
  Holding: The court held that the Nebraska Indian Child Welfare Act's active-efforts provisions in § 43-1503(1) are not a checklist requiring compliance with every subsection but a case-by-case standard, affirmed the findings of active efforts and of willful abandonment proved by clear and convincing evidence, but vacated the adoption decree because the county court did not strictly comply with the consent and criminal-history requirements of Neb. Rev. Stat. § 43-107.
  Why here: It holds that NICWA's active-efforts requirement governs proceedings terminating rights over an Indian child even where the parent is not of Native American descent, and that § 43-1503(1) is not a compliance checklist — the proposition In re Guardianship of Eliza W. relies on.
  Quote: “The conduct constituting abandonment as defined above must appear by clear and convincing evidence to be willful, intentional, or voluntary, without just cause or excuse.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4547595/linda-h-v-tyler-r-in-re-micah-h/

- Added In re Interest of Nicole M., 287 Neb. 685 (2014), for INP-12, DEC-04 and FAIL-01: it is the decision In re Interest of Carmelo G. cites for the premise that the proper starting point when the State involves itself in family relations is always the parent's fundamental constitutional rights. That exact sentence is broken by end-of-line hyphenation in the CourtListener text and therefore could not be quoted verbatim; the quote used is the court's adjacent statement of the unfitness requirement, and the fundamental-rights premise is reported in the relevance sentences. The case also reverses a termination as to a father the State failed to prove unfit, which is why it is mapped to FAIL-01.
- Added In re Interest of Brian B. et al., 268 Neb. 870, 689 N.W.2d 184 (2004), for DEC-03 and DEC-05: it is the authority In re Interest of Xandria P. relies on for the adjudication framework the brief uses — the adjudication phase exists to protect the child's interests and the parents' rights are determined at disposition — and it approves in-chambers child testimony at an adjudication hearing. A duplicate cluster exists at 8082570, styled 'State v. Justine R.'; cluster 2100736 is the one read.
- Added In re Interest of Becka P. et al., 296 Neb. 365, 894 N.W.2d 247 (2017), for ACT-02, FAIL-01 and OUT-05: it is the appellate-jurisdiction authority In re Interest of Paxton H. cites for the duty to confirm jurisdiction in a juvenile appeal, and on its own facts it shows how far a juvenile court's order can reach into parental decision-making, appointing an educational surrogate after the parents refused to authorize school assessments. A later appeal in the same case appears at 298 Neb. 98 (cluster 4446085) and was not retrieved.
- Added J.M.N. v. Nebraska Department of Social Services, 237 Neb. 116, 464 N.W.2d 811 (1991), for INP-04, ACT-03 and ACT-06: it holds that an order directing the agency to pay a ward's treatment costs is a final order under § 25-1902, which is the jurisdictional predicate In re Interest of Paxton H. rests its finality holding on, and it holds that the agency is not a 'parent' under § 43-290. CourtListener styles the case 'J.M.N. v. Nebraska Department of Social Services'; the prior pass's note called it 'In re Interest of J.M.N.' The companion finality authority the opinion cites, In re Interest of B.M.H., 233 Neb. 524, 446 N.W.2d 222 (1989), was not independently retrieved.
- Added In re Interest of Jahon S., 291 Neb. 97 (2015), for OUT-02: it is the published majority holding that incarceration alone cannot be the sole basis for terminating parental rights but is a factor to be considered, together with the rule that the criminal conduct causing the incarceration is voluntary. It replaces the sentence the prior pass had tried to source from In re Interest of Denzel D., which was in Funke, J.'s dissent. Denzel D. remains out of cases[].
- Added Maria A. on behalf of Leslie G. v. Oscar G., 301 Neb. 673, 919 N.W.2d 841 (2018), for ACT-05: ACT-05 previously rested on a single case. A targeted search of the Protection from Domestic Abuse Act in the Nebraska Supreme Court returned this decision, in which a mother sought a protection order on her daughter's behalf over the father's physical discipline. It is the necessary counterweight to Robert M. on behalf of Bella O.: abuse as defined by § 42-903 is a threshold that must be crossed, and even then the court decides prospectively whether the order should remain in effect. Miller-Lerman, J., dissented; the quote is from the majority.
- Added OMNI Behavioral Health v. Nebraska Foster Care Review Bd., 277 Neb. 641, 764 N.W.2d 398 (2009), for INP-09 and PMC-03: INP-09 previously rested only on Suter v. Artist M. and PMC-03 had no Nebraska case describing an actual review body. This decision sets out the State Foster Care Review Board's statutory structure — gubernatorial appointment, local review boards, the statewide foster care register, reporting to DHHS, county welfare offices and the courts, and § 43-1303 facility visits — which is the closest Nebraska institution to a CAPTA § 5106a(c) citizen review panel, and it decides the constitutional standard governing the Board's home visits. It remains true that no Nebraska case construes CAPTA citizen review panels as such.
