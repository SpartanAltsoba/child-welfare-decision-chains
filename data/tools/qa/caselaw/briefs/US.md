# US — 41 authorities, verified 2026-09-20

## INP-01 · Mandatory Report
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative duty on a state child protection agency to protect a child from private violence, even where the agency knew of the danger, absent a custodial relationship created by the State.
  Why here: A mandatory report that the agency receives and does not act on does not by itself create federal constitutional liability for the harm that follows.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Ferguson v. City of Charleston**, 532 U.S. 67 (2001)
  Holding: The Court held that a public hospital's nonconsensual drug testing of maternity patients, with results reported to police, was an unreasonable search not saved by the special needs doctrine because its immediate purpose was law enforcement.
  Why here: A mandatory reporting scheme that is designed and operated with law enforcement at every stage cannot claim the special needs exemption from the warrant requirement.
  Quote: “given the extensive involvement of law enforcement officials at every stage of the policy, this case simply does not fit within the closely guarded category of "special needs."”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/118414/ferguson-v-city-of-charleston/
- **Doe v. Heck**, 327 F.3d 492 (7th Cir. 2003)
  Holding: The court held that caseworkers violated the Fourth Amendment by entering a private school and seizing a student for a custodial interview without a warrant, consent, or probable cause, and violated the parents' right to familial relations, although qualified immunity shielded them from damages.
  Why here: A report from a mandated or institutional source does not authorize investigative steps untethered from reasonable suspicion.
  Quote: “although child welfare caseworkers may investigate allegations of child abuse without violating parents' constitutional right to familial relations, they may not do so arbitrarily”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/781752/doe-v-heck/
- **Prince v. Massachusetts**, 321 U.S. 158 (1944)
  Holding: The Court affirmed a child labor conviction, holding that the State acting as parens patriae may restrict parental control, including choices grounded in religious conviction, to protect a child's welfare.
  Why here: Prince supplies the parens patriae basis on which States may compel professionals to report suspected harm to children.
  Quote: “the state has a wide range of power for limiting parental freedom and authority in things affecting the child's welfare”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/103933/prince-v-massachusetts/

## INP-02 · Voluntary / Third-Party Report
- **Croft v. Westmoreland County Children and Youth Services**, 103 F.3d 1123 (3d Cir. 1997)
  Holding: The court reversed summary judgment for the agency, holding that a caseworker who forced a father from the home on the basis of an uncorroborated anonymous tip, while holding no opinion that abuse had occurred, lacked objectively reasonable grounds and committed an arbitrary abuse of government power.
  Why here: A third-party allegation that interviews fail to corroborate does not supply grounds to break up the household.
  Quote: “Even considered together, minor inconsistencies which provide no affirmative evidence of sexual abuse cannot alone establish the objectively reasonable grounds necessary to remove a family member from the family unit”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/732428/croft-v-westmoreland-county-children-and-youth-services/
- **Calabretta v. Floyd**, 189 F.3d 808 (9th Cir. 1999)
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of qualified immunity, holding that a social worker and police officer who coerced entry into a home and directed a strip search of a child, without a warrant, consent, or exigency, violated clearly established Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights.
  Why here: An unverified third-party report, without corroboration or urgency, does not authorize a warrantless entry to investigate.
  Quote: “Under White, appellants' claim, that "a search warrant is not required for home investigatory visits by social workers," is simply not the law”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/765930/robert-calabretta-v-floyd/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative duty on a state child protection agency to protect a child from private violence, even where the agency knew of the danger, absent a custodial relationship created by the State.
  Why here: Third-party reports do not generate a constitutional duty of protection; any duty to respond comes from CAPTA-conditioned state law.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Prince v. Massachusetts**, 321 U.S. 158 (1944)
  Holding: The Court affirmed a child labor conviction, holding that the State acting as parens patriae may restrict parental control, including choices grounded in religious conviction, to protect a child's welfare.
  Why here: Any person's report is received under a state protective power that Prince confirms extends into the family for the child's welfare.
  Quote: “the state has a wide range of power for limiting parental freedom and authority in things affecting the child's welfare”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/103933/prince-v-massachusetts/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Calabretta v. Floyd**, 189 F.3d 808 (9th Cir. 1999)
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of qualified immunity, holding that a social worker and police officer who coerced entry into a home and directed a strip search of a child, without a warrant, consent, or exigency, violated clearly established Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights.
  Why here: A social worker who brings police to compel entry does not thereby acquire an exemption from the warrant requirement.
  Quote: “Under White, appellants' claim, that "a search warrant is not required for home investigatory visits by social workers," is simply not the law”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/765930/robert-calabretta-v-floyd/
- **Andrews v. Hickman County**, 700 F.3d 845 (6th Cir. 2012)
  Holding: The court held that the Fourth Amendment governs social workers entering a home and that no social worker exception exists, but reversed the denial of qualified immunity to the caseworkers because that principle was not clearly established in the Sixth Circuit at the time, while affirming its denial to the officer.
  Why here: A caseworker accompanying officers into a home is bound by the same Fourth Amendment rules as the officers.
  Quote: “the actions of social workers in entering a home are governed by the Fourth Amendment, and we have concluded that no social worker exception applies in such situations”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/812950/robert-andrews-v-hickman-county-tennessee/
- **Gates v. Texas Department of Protective & Regulatory Services**, 537 F.3d 404 (5th Cir. 2008)
  Holding: The court held that the Fourth Amendment governs social workers' civil investigations, that the government may not seize a child from his parents absent a court order, parental consent, or exigent circumstances, and that removing a child from public school for a central-location interview requires a court order or a reasonable belief of abuse.
  Why here: Joint law enforcement and agency entries into a home are measured by ordinary Fourth Amendment rules on consent and exigency.
  Quote: “it is well established in this circuit that the Fourth Amendment regulates social workers' civil investigations”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/62905/gates-v-texas-deparment-of-protective-regulatory-services/
- **Wallis v. Spencer**, 202 F.3d 1126 (9th Cir. 2000)
  Holding: The court held that officials may seize a child without prior judicial authorization only on reasonable cause to believe the child faces imminent danger of serious bodily injury, that reasonable investigative avenues must first be pursued, and that subjecting seized children to invasive medical examinations without parental notice implicated the family's constitutional rights.
  Why here: Police acting on a child protection referral must investigate before seizing, and may not rely on an unverified assertion that a court order exists.
  Quote: “the police cannot seize children suspected of being abused or neglected unless reasonable avenues of investigation are first pursued”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/767557/no-97-55579/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **Wernecke v. Garcia**, 591 F.3d 386 (5th Cir. 2009)
  Holding: The court held that a valid ex parte emergency custody order authorized the caseworker's entry and search of the home for the named child, but that removal of the other children that evening was not supported by exigency and was not shielded by qualified immunity.
  Why here: A judicially issued ex parte emergency custody order supplies the authorization that makes agency entry and seizure reasonable.
  Quote: “Gates was not the first case to require, at a minimum, some evidence of imminent danger prior to removal”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1463447/wernecke-v-garcia/
- **Gates v. Texas Department of Protective & Regulatory Services**, 537 F.3d 404 (5th Cir. 2008)
  Holding: The court held that the Fourth Amendment governs social workers' civil investigations, that the government may not seize a child from his parents absent a court order, parental consent, or exigent circumstances, and that removing a child from public school for a central-location interview requires a court order or a reasonable belief of abuse.
  Why here: A properly issued emergency custody order satisfies the warrant requirement for taking a child into custody.
  Quote: “it is well established in this circuit that the Fourth Amendment regulates social workers' civil investigations”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/62905/gates-v-texas-deparment-of-protective-regulatory-services/
- **Tenenbaum v. Williams**, 193 F.3d 581 (2d Cir. 1999)
  Holding: The court held that removing a child from school for an examination on an emergency basis is unconstitutional where officials had reasonable time to seek judicial authorization consistent with the child's safety, though the defendants there received qualified immunity because the right was not clearly established in 1990.
  Why here: Where time permits, an ex parte judicial order, not a unilateral agency decision, is the constitutionally required route to custody.
  Quote: “it is unconstitutional for state officials to effect a child's removal on an "emergency" basis where there is reasonable time safely to obtain judicial authorization consistent with the child's safety”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/766396/tenenbaum-v-williams/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **Troxel v. Granville**, 530 U.S. 57 (2000)
  Holding: The plurality opinion announcing the judgment held that Washington's broad third-party visitation statute, as applied, violated the mother's due process rights because the trial court gave no special weight to a fit parent's determination of her children's best interests.
  Why here: When a parent asks the system for help, the fit-parent presumption limits how far the agency may convert that request into supervision over the parent's own judgment.
  Quote: “there is a presumption that fit parents act in the best interests of their children”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1087668/troxel-v-granville/
- **Parham v. J. R.**, 442 U.S. 584 (1979)
  Holding: The Court held that parents retain a substantial and presumptively dominant role in committing a child for mental health treatment, but that due process requires review of the admission decision by a neutral factfinder.
  Why here: A guardian's request for services is presumptively entitled to deference unless the agency has an actual finding of neglect or abuse.
  Quote: “our precedents permit the parents to retain a substantial, if not the dominant, role in the decision, absent a finding of neglect or abuse”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/110113/parham-v-j-r/
- **Prince v. Massachusetts**, 321 U.S. 158 (1944)
  Holding: The Court affirmed a child labor conviction, holding that the State acting as parens patriae may restrict parental control, including choices grounded in religious conviction, to protect a child's welfare.
  Why here: Even where a family seeks help voluntarily, the State retains independent authority to act for the child's welfare.
  Quote: “the state has a wide range of power for limiting parental freedom and authority in things affecting the child's welfare”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/103933/prince-v-massachusetts/
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court held that the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's 'reasonable efforts' requirement, 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15), creates no right enforceable by beneficiaries through § 1983 or an implied right of action.
  Why here: Families seeking prevention services have no federal cause of action to compel the State to provide them under Title IV-E.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Wyman v. James**, 400 U.S. 309 (1971)
  Holding: The Court reversed, holding that a periodic caseworker home visit required as a condition of continued AFDC assistance is not a Fourth Amendment search and, even if it had some characteristics of one, is not unreasonable, because the visit cannot be forced and refusal carries only the loss of benefits rather than criminal sanction.
  Why here: Where a family accepts public assistance or services, Wyman permits the State to condition continued aid on a caseworker home visit without a warrant, so long as refusal is met with termination of benefits rather than compulsion.
  Quote: “We note, too, that the visitation in itself is not forced or compelled, and that the beneficiary's denial of permission is not a criminal act”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/108223/wyman-v-james/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Suter v. Artist M.**, 503 U.S. 347 (1992)
  Holding: The Court held that the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's 'reasonable efforts' requirement, 42 U.S.C. § 671(a)(15), creates no right enforceable by beneficiaries through § 1983 or an implied right of action.
  Why here: Suter channels enforcement of IV-E plan requirements to the Secretary's administrative oversight rather than to private litigation.
  Quote: “We hold that the Act does not create an enforceable right on behalf of respondents”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112712/suter-v-artist-m/
- **Gonzaga University v. Doe**, 536 U.S. 273 (2002)
  Holding: The Court held that FERPA's nondisclosure provisions create no personal rights enforceable under § 1983, and that nothing short of an unambiguously conferred right will support a § 1983 action to enforce a federal spending statute.
  Why here: Findings by federal oversight bodies do not become privately enforceable rights unless Congress used unambiguous rights-creating language.
  Quote: “We now reject the notion that our cases permit anything short of an unambiguously conferred right to support a cause of action brought under § 1983”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/121157/gonzaga-university-v-doe/
- **Armstrong v. Exceptional Child Center, Inc.**, 575 U.S. 320 (2015)
  Holding: The Court held that the Supremacy Clause creates no private cause of action and that Medicaid providers could not sue in equity to enforce a Spending Clause requirement whose sole remedy Congress vested in the Secretary.
  Why here: Oversight-driven noncompliance is remedied by the federal agency's withholding power, not by private preemption suits.
  Quote: “To say that the Supremacy Clause does not confer a right of action is not to diminish the significant role that courts play in assuring the supremacy of federal law”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2790213/armstrong-v-exceptional-child-center-inc/
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services**, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that the Due Process Clause imposes no affirmative duty on a state child protection agency to protect a child from private violence, even where the agency knew of the danger, absent a custodial relationship created by the State.
  Why here: Oversight findings of systemic non-response do not translate into a federal damages remedy, because the Constitution imposes no general protective duty.
  Quote: “As a general matter, then, we conclude that a State's failure to protect an individual against private violence simply does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112202/deshaney-v-winnebago-county-department-of-social-services/
- **Health and Hospital Corporation of Marion Cty. v. Talevski**, 599 U.S. 166 (2023)
  Holding: The Court affirmed, holding that the Federal Nursing Home Reform Act's chemical-restraint and pre-transfer-notice provisions unambiguously confer individual rights presumptively enforceable under § 1983, and that the Act's remedial scheme was not comprehensive enough to rebut that presumption; the Court declined to disturb the Spending Clause § 1983 line running from Suter and Gonzaga.
  Why here: Whether an oversight-identified violation of a federal child welfare condition can also be sued on privately is now decided under Talevski's two-step conferral-then-rebuttal framework, not under Gonzaga alone.
  Quote: “our precedent sets a demanding bar: Statutory provisions must unambiguously confer individual federal rights”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9404968/health-and-hospital-corporation-of-marion-cty-v-talevski/
- **Blessing v. Freestone**, 520 U.S. 329 (1997)
  Holding: A unanimous Court held that Title IV-D's requirement that a State's child support program be in 'substantial compliance' with federal law confers no individual right enforceable under § 1983, because it is a systemwide performance yardstick for the Secretary, and remanded for the lower courts to identify with particularity which specific statutory provisions, if any, create rights.
  Why here: Blessing holds that a systemwide compliance standard of the kind CFSR and OIG reviews measure is a yardstick for the Secretary, not a right an affected family can enforce.
  Quote: “In order to seek redress through § 1983, however, a plaintiff must assert the violation of a federal right, not merely a violation of federal law”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/118101/blessing-v-freestone/

## INP-11 · Federal / Tribal Jurisdiction Trigger
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield**, 490 U.S. 30 (1989)
  Holding: The Court held that 'domicile' under ICWA is a matter of uniform federal law, that the twins were domiciled on the reservation through their mother, and that the tribal court therefore had exclusive jurisdiction, vacating the state adoption decree.
  Why here: Holyfield fixes the jurisdictional trigger: if the Indian child is domiciled on the reservation, the state system has no jurisdiction at all.
  Quote: “In enacting the ICWA Congress confirmed that, in child custody proceedings involving Indian children domiciled on the reservation, tribal jurisdiction was exclusive as to the States”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112241/mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield/
- **Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl**, 570 U.S. 637 (2013)
  Holding: The Court held that ICWA § 1912(f) does not bar termination where the Indian parent never had custody, and that § 1912(d)'s remedial-efforts requirement applies only where termination would precipitate the breakup of an existing Indian family.
  Why here: Adoptive Couple narrows which ICWA protections attach at intake by keying §§ 1912(d) and (f) to an existing custodial relationship.
  Quote: “we hold that §1912(d) applies only in cases where an Indian family's “breakup” would be precipitated by the termination of the parent's rights”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2959741/adoptive-couple-v-baby-girl/
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)

## INP-12 · Emergency / Protective Custody
- **Wallis v. Spencer**, 202 F.3d 1126 (9th Cir. 2000)

  Why here: Protective custody is lawful only where investigation has been pursued and imminent danger of serious bodily injury is shown.
  Quote: “the police cannot seize children suspected of being abused or neglected unless reasonable avenues of investigation are first pursued”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/767557/no-97-55579/

  Holding: The court reversed summary judgment for a social worker, holding that a material fact issue existed on whether there was reasonable cause to believe the child faced imminent danger of serious bodily injury justifying warrantless entry and seizure.
  Why here: Protective custody requires imminent danger of future harm, not merely a substantiated allegation of past abuse.
  Quote: “Nonetheless, imminent danger of future harm is required to show exigency”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/771798/mabe-v-san-bernardino-county/
- **Rogers v. County of San Joaquin**, 487 F.3d 1288 (9th Cir. 2007)
  Holding: The court reversed the grant of qualified immunity, holding that chronic medical neglect and unsanitary home conditions did not establish exigency and that the warrantless removal violated clearly established Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights.
  Why here: Chronic neglect conditions that pose no immediate risk of serious bodily harm do not justify taking a child into protective custody without a warrant.
  Quote: “we hold that it was clearly established that warrantless removal of children is permissible only in cases of exigency”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/797914/rogers-v-county-of-san-joaquin/
- **Tenenbaum v. Williams**, 193 F.3d 581 (2d Cir. 1999)
  Holding: The court held that removing a child from school for an examination on an emergency basis is unconstitutional where officials had reasonable time to seek judicial authorization consistent with the child's safety, though the defendants there received qualified immunity because the right was not clearly established in 1990.
  Why here: Emergency protective custody is available only when the danger is such that judicial authorization cannot safely be obtained first.
  Quote: “it is unconstitutional for state officials to effect a child's removal on an "emergency" basis where there is reasonable time safely to obtain judicial authorization consistent with the child's safety”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/766396/tenenbaum-v-williams/
- **Roska v. Peterson**, 328 F.3d 1230 (10th Cir. 2003)

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/781963/roska-v-peterson/
- **Wyman v. James**, 400 U.S. 309 (1971)
  Holding: The Court reversed, holding that a periodic caseworker home visit required as a condition of continued AFDC assistance is not a Fourth Amendment search and, even if it had some characteristics of one, is not unreasonable, because the visit cannot be forced and refusal carries only the loss of benefits rather than criminal sanction.

## FAIL-03 · Federal Compliance Failure

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9404968/health-and-hospital-corporation-of-marion-cty-v-talevski/

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/118101/blessing-v-freestone/
