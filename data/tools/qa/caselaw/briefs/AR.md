## INP-02 · Voluntary / Third-Party Report
- **Blanchard v. Arkansas Department of Human Services**, 2010 Ark. App. 785, 379 S.W.3d 686
  Holding: The court affirmed the adjudication of three sisters as dependent-neglected based on sexual abuse, holding that the juvenile code requires only proof by a preponderance of the evidence and that the child's out-of-court statement could support the finding.
  Why here: A report from any source is tested at adjudication by a preponderance standard, not a criminal one.
  Quote: “The juvenile code requires proof by a preponderance of the evidence in dependency-neglect proceedings.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5282978/blanchard-v-arkansas-department-of-human-services/

  Why here: It shows how an allegation of excessive corporal punishment moves from report to judicial finding.
  Quote: “The focus of an adjudication hearing is on the child, not the parent.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6243053/phillips-v-ark-dept-of-human-servs-minor-child/

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1743976/arkansas-department-of-human-services-v-ab/

## INP-03 · Law-Enforcement Referral
- **Webster v. Westlake**, 41 F.4th 1004 (8th Cir. 2022)
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of qualified immunity, holding that detectives who arrested a mother on her porch after she refused to let a child-protection worker enter or see her children lacked probable cause and violated her clearly established Fourth Amendment rights.
  Why here: It governs the moment a caseworker returns to the door with police: the officers' authority is bounded by ordinary Fourth Amendment probable-cause rules.
  Quote: “because the detectives subjected Webster to a warrantless arrest that was unsupported by probable cause, they violated her clearly established rights”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7441117/trenisha-webster-v-jennifer-westlake/
- **Stanley v. Hutchinson**, 12 F.4th 834 (8th Cir. 2021)
  Holding: In a § 1983 suit arising from an Arkansas DHS/State Police removal, the court held that the removal of children requires reasonable suspicion of child abuse, that the officials here had it, and that they were therefore entitled to qualified immunity.
  Why here: This is the controlling Arkansas-arising Eighth Circuit statement of what a law-enforcement/CACD investigator must have before children are taken at the end of a search.
  Quote: “it is clearly established that the removal of children from their parents' custody violates a constitutional right if the removal occurs without reasonable suspicion of child abuse”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5091802/hal-stanley-v-asa-hutchinson/
- **Mitchell v. Dakota County Social Services**, 959 F.3d 887 (8th Cir. 2020)
  Holding: The court held that removal supported by reasonable suspicion of child abuse, followed by a petition and hearing within days, violated neither substantive nor procedural due process, and the caseworkers had qualified immunity.
  Why here: It confirms that a referral generated by law enforcement or a family member can supply reasonable suspicion sufficient to justify the state's initial intervention.
  Quote: “In child removal cases, the meaningful time and manner requirement means that the state must hold a hearing promptly after removal.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4754922/dwight-mitchell-v-dakota-county-social-services/
- **Stanley v. Finnegan**, 899 F.3d 623 (8th Cir. 2018)
  Holding: The court affirmed the denial of qualified immunity on a motion to dismiss, holding that an Arkansas State Police Crimes Against Children Division investigator who participated in removing seven children from their Hot Springs home could be liable because the complaint plausibly alleged she acted without reasonable suspicion of child abuse.
  Why here: This is the Arkansas-arising Eighth Circuit decision that actually established the reasonable-suspicion standard a Crimes Against Children Division investigator must satisfy before children are taken, and it should be cited for the rule rather than the later Stanley v. Hutchinson appeal that quotes it.
  Quote: “whether there was reasonable suspicion of child abuse warranting removal must be determined by analyzing the totality of the circumstances at the time of the removal”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4525560/hal-stanley-v-katherine-finnegan/

## INP-04 · Court-Ordered Referral
- **Arkansas Department of Human Services v. Cox**, 349 Ark. 205, 82 S.W.3d 806 (2002)
  Holding: The court affirmed the Arkansas guardianship orders and held that DHS had no authority to seize a newborn on a Florida ex parte order that had never been registered or enforced through an Arkansas court.
  Why here: A referral or order from another court does not itself empower DHS to act; the order must be enforced through an Arkansas court.
  Quote: “DHS is utterly without authority to execute an order from a foreign jurisdiction on its own.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1745590/arkansas-department-of-human-services-v-cox/
- **Harwell-Williams v. Arkansas Department of Human Services**, 368 Ark. 183, 243 S.W.3d 898 (2006)
  Holding: The court affirmed, holding that under Ark. Code Ann. § 9-27-343(c) the juvenile division retains jurisdiction to conduct further hearings while an appeal is pending in a case involving an out-of-home placement.
  Why here: It identifies which juvenile-court orders are final and appealable and which merely continue the court's ongoing supervision of the case.
  Quote: “the juvenile division of the circuit court retained jurisdiction to conduct further hearings because the case involved a juvenile out-of-home placement”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2356792/harwell-williams-v-arkansas-department-of-human-services/

## INP-05 · Self-Report / Guardian Request
- **In the Matter of the Guardianship of S.H.**, 2015 Ark. 75, 455 S.W.3d 313
  Holding: On the second appeal in the same litigation, the court reversed again and ordered the child returned, holding that the circuit court applied a standard that failed to protect the fit natural parent's fundamental right to the care, custody and control of her child.
  Why here: It is the remedy side of a parent's voluntary request for help: a parent not found unfit is entitled to have the child returned, not merely to a new hearing.
  Quote: “We hold that the circuit court applied a legal standard that failed to safeguard the natural parent's fundamental right with respect to the care, custody, and control of her child.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2782464/in-the-matter-of-the-guardianship-of-sh/
- **Arkansas Department of Human Services v. Cox**, 349 Ark. 205, 82 S.W.3d 806 (2002)
  Holding: The court affirmed the Arkansas guardianship orders and held that DHS had no authority to seize a newborn on a Florida ex parte order that had never been registered or enforced through an Arkansas court.
  Why here: The family here obtained a guardianship through the Arkansas probate court, and that order controlled over the agency's unilateral action.
  Quote: “DHS is utterly without authority to execute an order from a foreign jurisdiction on its own.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1745590/arkansas-department-of-human-services-v-cox/
- **Devine v. Martens**, 371 Ark. 60, 263 S.W.3d 515 (2007)
  Holding: The court reversed an order granting the grandparents permanent guardianship of a child, holding that although temporary emergency jurisdiction under the UCCJEA was proper, the circuit court clearly erred in finding the natural mother unfit where she had corrected the conditions complained of before the final hearing.
  Why here: When a relative steps in and converts an informal caregiving arrangement into a guardianship petition, this case sets the limit on how far that can displace a parent who has since put her home in order.
  Quote: “The law prefers a parent over a grandparent or other third person unless the parent is proved to be incompetent or unfit.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1628659/devine-v-martens/

## INP-09 · Systemic Audit / Oversight Trigger
- **Arkansas Department of Health v. Westark Christian Action Council**, 322 Ark. 440, 910 S.W.2d 199 (1995)
  Holding: The court reversed, holding the requested reports were exempt vital records, while reaffirming that the FOIA is construed liberally toward disclosure and its exemptions are construed narrowly.
  Why here: It is the canonical Arkansas statement of the interpretive presumption that governs any records request used to trigger or document oversight.
  Quote: “we narrowly construe exceptions to the FOIA to counterbalance the self-protective instincts of the government bureaucracy”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1638347/arkansas-department-of-health-v-westark-christian-action-council/
- **Pulaski County v. Arkansas Democrat-Gazette, Inc.**, 370 Ark. 435, 260 S.W.3d 718 (2007)
  Holding: The court held that the dispositive question was whether the withheld communications were 'public records' under the FOIA and that an in camera review was necessary to decide it, rather than a categorical exemption for personal material.
  Why here: It establishes the procedure a court uses when an agency claims withheld material is not a public record at all.
  Quote: “It is necessary to conduct an in camera review to determine whether the e-mails at issue are public records, and thus should be disclosed pursuant to the FOIA.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1875537/pulaski-county-v-arkansas-democrat-gazette-inc/
- **Arkansas Department of Human Services v. Cole**, 2011 Ark. 145, 380 S.W.3d 429
  Holding: The court struck down Initiated Act 1, which barred cohabiting unmarried adults from adopting or fostering, holding that conditioning the privilege of fostering or adopting on surrendering the state constitutional right to private consensual conduct is unconstitutional.
  Why here: It shows the Arkansas Constitution operating as an independent oversight constraint on statewide child-welfare placement policy.
  Quote: “We affirm the circuit court's ruling that Act 1 is unconstitutional as a violation of fundamental privacy rights under the Arkansas Constitution.”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5283037/arkansas-department-of-human-services-v-cole/
- **Arkansas Department of Human Services v. Howard**, 367 Ark. 55, 238 S.W.3d 1 (2006)
  Holding: The court affirmed the invalidation of the Child Welfare Agency Review Board regulation barring any household with a homosexual adult from fostering, holding the rule had no rational relationship to child welfare and was an unauthorized exercise of legislative power over morality.
  Why here: It is the model for how a categorical agency placement rule is tested against the record and the agency's delegated authority when oversight bodies review it.
  Quote: “the circuit court did not err in finding that there was no rational relationship between the regulation's blanket exclusion and the health, safety, and welfare of the foster children”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2361521/arkansas-department-of-human-services-v-howard/

- **Cutright v. State**, 97 Ark. App. 70, 244 S.W.3d 702 (2006)
  Holding: The court reversed and ordered custody awarded to the appellant, holding the circuit court clearly erred by applying only a general best-interest test and failing to find good cause before departing from the ICWA placement preferences and the tribe's recommendation.
  Why here: It is the published Arkansas decision holding that once ICWA applies, a heightened standard displaces the ordinary best-interest analysis.

  Holding: The court reversed the permanency-planning order and every order entered after it, holding that changing the goal to adoption alone, while an approvable relative placement was pending, contravened the ICWA placement preferences and applied the wrong burden of proof.
  Why here: It is the most recent published Arkansas application of ICWA, including notice to the tribe, active efforts and the tribe's right to intervene.
  Quote: “the circuit court clearly erred by changing the case's goal to solely adoption following termination in contravention of the preferences mandated by the ICWA”

## OUT-02 · Termination of Parental Rights / Adoption

  Why here: It is the operative Arkansas Supreme Court statement of how the two best-interest factors, likelihood of adoption and potential harm, are applied, including to an incarcerated parent.
  Quote: “we conclude that the evidence of potential harm, combined with the children's adoptability, supports the circuit court's ruling that termination of Earls's parental rights is in the twins' best interest”
  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6241407/earls-v-ark-dept-of-human-servs-minor-children/

  https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3008926/brumley-v-ark-dept-of-human-servs/
- **Myers v. Arkansas Department of Human Services**, 2011 Ark. 182, 380 S.W.3d 906
  Holding: In one of the Tony Alamo Christian Ministries appeals, the court affirmed termination, holding that a termination order requires clear and convincing proof both of best interest and of at least one statutory ground.

- **In re Adoption of K.F.H.**, 311 Ark. 416, 844 S.W.2d 343 (1993)
  Holding: The court affirmed an adoption without the mother's consent after jurisdiction was transferred from Michigan, holding that Arkansas law applied to conduct predating the transfer and that her failure to communicate for over a year was without justifiable cause.

## PMC-01 · CFSR Monitoring
- **Ellis v. Arkansas Department of Human Services**, 2016 Ark. 441, 505 S.W.3d 678
  Holding: The court reversed, holding that the circuit court erred by failing to hold the mandatory review hearing and to apply the statutory relative-placement preference upon receipt of a satisfactory home study, and overruled cases limiting that preference to initial placement.
  Why here: Failure to hold statutorily mandated review hearings is precisely the timeliness defect federal reviews measure, and here it was reversible error.

  Why here: Permanency decisions and case closures are the data points federal reviews score, and this case shows the evidentiary standard they must meet.
  Quote: “This court reviews findings in dependency-neglect proceedings de novo, but we will not reverse the trial court's findings unless they are clearly erroneous.”

- **Wade v. Arkansas Department of Human Services**, 337 Ark. 353, 990 S.W.2d 509 (1999)

- Missing (for a later pass): Manzano v. South Dakota Department of Social Services, 60 F.3d 505 (8th Cir. 1995) — the circuit's balancing of the family's liberty interest against the State's interest in protecting a child during an abuse investigation. It is the standard companion to Kuehl and Heartland for DEC-02 and INP-12, and no case in the brief covers the investigation-stage familial-integrity balance.
